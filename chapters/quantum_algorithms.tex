\begin{abstract}
This chapter develops, trains and validates on physical hardware a hybrid quantum--classical convolutional neural network (Q-CNN) for binary satellite-image classification on a subset of the EuroSAT dataset. The architecture inserts a quanvolutional layer of nine qubits and eighteen trainable parameters into a classical convolutional trunk; the layer encodes patches into rotation-angle features, processes them through a shallow hardware-efficient entangling block, and reads out marginal Pauli-$Z$ expectations as quantum-derived feature maps.

Four independent contributions are reported. First, a multi-seed statistical campaign at $R=10$ seeds compares the hybrid Q-CNN against two purpose-built classical controls: a structurally isomorphic matched-capacity baseline at $0.07\%$ parameter-count tolerance, and a high-capacity reference at $\sim 185\times$ the parameter count of the hybrid. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank exact tests yield a final-epoch validation accuracy of $0.9445\pm 0.0243$ for the hybrid against $0.9700\pm 0.0133$ for the matched-capacity classical control ($p=2.7\times 10^{-2}$ at $R=10$, paired Cohen's $d=-0.86$); at the first training epoch, however, the same hybrid is approximately $29$ percentage points ahead of the matched-capacity control. The reading is one of training-efficiency advantage for the quantum block paired with a small asymptotic-accuracy deficit, both statistically supported on the same data.

Second, a runtime-aware cost model is calibrated from $195$ jobs of the IQM Emerald production campaign; the model links bound-circuit count, mini-batch size and per-circuit wall time to a closed-form a-priori credit-cost estimate, and is verified to six significant digits against the dashboard billing rule $\lceil\,\texttt{runtime\_seconds}\,\rceil\times 0.75$. The Python-side wall-time-to-credit predictor is found to be conservative by $\sim 24\%$ with respect to the QPU-side billing.

Third, the simulator-trained checkpoint is loaded onto the IQM Emerald 54-qubit superconducting processor and fine-tuned for two epochs on the device via parameter-shift gradients; validation accuracy returns to $100\%$ after the second epoch ($N_{\mathrm{val}}=20$, Wilson $95\%$ CI $[0.84,\,1.00]$), and the trainable-quantum-weight gradient norm decays by an order of magnitude over the campaign. The complete campaign --- backend connection, transpilation under partial-connectivity constraints, calibration-aware initial layout, cost-model calibration, mini-batch SGD adaptation and convergence of fine-tuning --- is documented end-to-end as a portable methodology for credit-bounded training on a $\geq 50$-qubit superconducting QPU.

Fourth, the noise sensitivity of the hybrid pipeline is characterised through a paired multi-seed study at $R=10$ seeds on a reduced 4-qubit version of the architecture, run under a calibrated noise model derived from the IBM Heron r2 device \texttt{ibm\_fez}. The final classification accuracy is statistically indistinguishable between the noisy ($96.20 \pm 1.36\%$) and noiseless ($96.40 \pm 1.47\%$) branches (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.219$), with only a transient perturbation on the loss visible in the first three training epochs and fully absorbed by epoch five. The result indicates that in the considered regime the hybrid architecture is resilient to gate-level noise of contemporary superconducting devices.
\end{abstract}

\section{Hybrid quantum--classical convolutional networks}
Quantum computing has entered the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, with devices
comprising tens to hundreds of noisy qubits~\cite{Preskill2018}. In parallel, deep learning
has become ubiquitous across domains, including satellite-based remote sensing~\cite{Goodfellow2016,Helber2019}.
Hybrid quantum--classical models embed parameterised quantum circuits (PQCs) within neural networks
to potentially augment representation power and learning dynamics~\cite{biamonte2017, cerezo2021}.

Among hybrid models, quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs) extend classical convolutions
with quantum kernels for local feature extraction~\cite{Cong2019}. Prior work has explored hybrid
architectures for remote sensing classification~\cite{Sebastianelli2021}, yet the specific role and
design of quantum convolutional layers remain under-explored. The present chapter builds on the quanvolutional ansatz design of the prior thesis work of Filippi~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}\footnote{M.Sc.\ thesis in Physics at the University of Pisa, co-supervised by the present author together with Oliver Morsch.} (Eq.~\ref{eq:parallel_circuit}, Fig.~\ref{fig:qconv}, Fig.~\ref{fig:qcnn_ansatz}), and extends it along four dimensions of our own: (i) a multi-seed statistical campaign with explicit matched-capacity classical controls and paired non-parametric significance testing (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}); (ii) a runtime-aware cost model that links circuit-level scheduling to credit-bounded training on cloud-accessed superconducting hardware (Sec.~\ref{sec:runtime-metric}); (iii) the first end-to-end transfer of the trained hybrid Q-CNN onto a $\geq 50$-qubit superconducting device, with parameter-shift fine-tuning on the IQM Emerald processor (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}); and (iv) a paired multi-seed characterisation of noise sensitivity on a reduced 4-qubit version of the architecture, run under a calibrated IBM Heron r2 noise model, which extends the noise-related claims of (iii) beyond the two-epoch cloud-credit budget (Sec.~\ref{sec:noise_resilience}). The chapter develops the QCNN layer formally, benchmarks multiple architectures on EuroSAT~\cite{Helber2019}, compares the chosen candidate with a classical CNN baseline~\cite{LeCun1998} and a published hybrid network~\cite{Sebastianelli2021}, and closes the loop on physical hardware. Where a figure or an equation is adapted from~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}, the caption flags the provenance explicitly; everything else is original to this work.

\section{Classical CNNs and quantum machine learning}
This chapter combines classical convolutional processing with parameterised quantum circuits in a single hybrid model. The two ingredients are reviewed separately before they are brought together: Sec.~\ref{sec:classical-cnns} recalls the elements of classical CNNs, and Sec.~\ref{sec:qml-qcnn} introduces quantum machine learning and quantum convolutional networks, closing with the hybrid pipeline that the rest of the chapter develops and benchmarks.


\subsection{Classical CNNs}\label{sec:classical-cnns}
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) exploit locality and weight sharing to extract
translation–equivariant features from images~\cite{Goodfellow2016,LeCun1998}.
Let $\mathbf{X}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_{\mathrm{in}}}$ be an input and
$\mathbf{K}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{k_h\times k_w\times C_{\mathrm{in}}\times C_{\mathrm{out}}}$ a bank of filters.
A 2D convolution produces feature maps $\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{H'\times W'\times C_{\mathrm{out}}}$
via
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,j,c} \;=\; b_c \;+\!
\sum_{u=0}^{k_h-1}\sum_{v=0}^{k_w-1}\sum_{d=0}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}-1}
K_{u,v,d,c}\; X_{i+u,\,j+v,\,d},
\label{eq:conv2d}
\end{equation}
optionally followed by a pointwise nonlinearity $\sigma$, e.g.\ $\mathrm{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z)$.
Spatial downsampling (pooling) aggregates over local neighborhoods $\Omega$,
\begin{equation}
P_{i,j,c} \;=\; \max_{(u,v)\in\Omega} Y_{s\,i+u,\,s\,j+v,\,c},
\end{equation}
controlling receptive fields while reducing resolution and computation.

For a classifier with logits $\mathbf{z}_n$ and one–hot targets $\mathbf{t}_n$, the cross–entropy objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}
t_{n,c}\,\log\!\left(\frac{e^{z_{n,c}}}{\sum_{c'} e^{z_{n,c'}}}\right).
\end{equation}
Parameters are updated by (stochastic) gradient descent using backpropagation,
which applies the chain rule layer–wise and reuses intermediate activations efficiently~\cite{Goodfellow2016}.
In our comparisons we adopt as classical reference a CNN that matches the trainable-parameter capacity of the hybrid Q--CNN, rather than the compact LeNet--5 baseline~\cite{LeCun1998} previously used in~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}; the rationale for this choice is detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines}.



\subsection{Quantum machine learning and QCNNs}\label{sec:qml-qcnn}
Hybrid quantum--classical learning integrates short-depth quantum computations into a larger classical optimisation loop, aiming to exploit quantum resources (superposition, entanglement, and measurement-induced nonlinearities) while remaining compatible with near-term noisy hardware. The conceptual and algorithmic underpinnings are well documented in the monograph by Schuld and Petruccione, which frames quantum models as feature maps and variational learners trained by classical optimisers~\cite{SchuldPetruccione2021}. In a quantum machine learning (QML) model the inductive bias---the model's built-in assumptions about the structure of the target function---emerges from how classical data are encoded into quantum states and from the topology of the entangling gates used, which together determine expressivity, trainability, and generalisation~\cite{Benedetti2019,cerezo2021}. A typical pipeline prepares an encoded state $U_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\ket{0}$, applies a trainable ansatz $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, and returns an expectation value of an observable $\hat{O}$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}) \;=\;
\bra{0}\,U_{\phi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})\,U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,
\hat{O}\,U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,U_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\,\ket{0}.
\label{eq:q-model}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Data encoding as a feature map.}
A central design choice is how classical inputs $\mathbf{x}$ are embedded into quantum states. Beyond initialisation, \emph{encoding} acts as a feature map $\phi:\mathbf{x}\mapsto \lvert \phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle$ into a high-dimensional Hilbert space, reshaping distances and decision boundaries~\cite{SchuldPetruccione2021,Schuld2019PRA}. Canonical strategies include: (i) \emph{basis encoding}, mapping bits to computational basis states (memory-expensive but simple); (ii) \emph{amplitude encoding}, loading normalised vectors into amplitudes (compact but sensitive to state preparation cost); and (iii) \emph{angle/rotation encoding}, where features modulate rotation angles on single qubits, producing trigonometric feature expansions whose richness grows with depth and entanglement. Interleaving encoders with trainable blocks (\emph{data re-uploading}) can enhance expressivity by composing nonlinear feature maps with learned unitaries~\cite{PerezSalinas2020,Schuld2020Expressivity}. In many vision-like tasks, locality-aware encodings (e.g., patch-wise rotations) align the quantum hypothesis class with the spatial inductive biases that make classical CNNs effective.


\paragraph{Variational circuits and trainable ans\"atze.}
A PQC defines a unitary transformation 
\begin{equation*}
U(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{x})=U_L(\theta_L)\,E(\mathbf{x})\cdots U_1(\theta_1)\,E(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation*}

\noindent that alternates fixed \emph{encoders} $E(\mathbf{x})$ with trainable layers $U_\ell(\theta_\ell)$ (the \emph{ansatz}) and outputs model predictions from expectation values $\langle \hat{O}\rangle$ of suitable observables. Hardware-efficient ans\"atze use native single-qubit rotations and entanglers repeated over shallow depth, while problem-inspired templates inject domain structure via symmetries, locality, or conserved quantities~\cite{Benedetti2019,cerezo2021}. The choice of entangling graph, depth, and encoder placement governs the bias--variance trade-off: too little entanglement limits expressivity; too much depth risks vanishing gradients and noise accumulation.


\paragraph{Forward propagation and the parameter-shift rule.}
In classical deep learning, backpropagation reuses intermediate activations via the chain rule across the entire network. In PQC-based models, \emph{forward propagation} consists of executing the circuit and estimating $\langle \hat{O}\rangle$ from a finite number of measurement shots, introducing statistical noise. The derivative $\partial \langle \hat{O}\rangle / \partial \theta_k$, however, is not available by straightforward reverse-mode differentiation through the unitary on hardware; instead, one leverages \emph{parameter-shift} rules that express exact partial derivatives as linear combinations of forward evaluations at shifted parameters (two evaluations per Pauli-generated rotation)~\cite{Mitarai2018,Schuld2019Grad}; for generators with $\pm 1$ eigenvalues the rule takes the form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_k}\,\hat{y}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
\;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[
\hat{y}\!\left(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{+}\right)
-\hat{y}\!\left(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{-}\right)\right],
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{\pm}:\ \theta_k \mapsto \theta_k \pm \tfrac{\pi}{2}.
\label{eq:psr}
\end{equation}
This yields unbiased gradients but increases cost roughly proportional to the number of trainable parameters and required shot precision. Simulator-specific \emph{adjoint} methods can reduce cost in silico, but on actual devices the parameter-shift (or closely related) estimators remain the norm. Compared with classical backprop, this gradient acquisition pipeline changes the computational budget and variance profile, motivating shot-frugal schedules and careful layerwise parameterisation.

\paragraph{Trainability, noise, and barren plateaus.}
Even with analytic gradients, training can fail when variances vanish (so-called \emph{barren plateaus}), which arise under sufficiently expressive or random ans\"atze, global cost functions, and in the presence of noise~\cite{McClean2018}. Practical recipes emphasize shallow, structured circuits, local cost terms, and encoders that restrict the effective hypothesis class to mitigate concentration-of-measure effects~\cite{SchuldPetruccione2021,cerezo2021}.


\paragraph{Quantum convolutional networks.}
Quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs) implement hierarchical, locality–aware processing
with shallow, repeating blocks that mirror convolution and pooling~\cite{Cong2019}.
Let $\rho^{(\ell)}$ denote the layer–$\ell$ state on a 1D register.\footnote{For 2D images we
tile registers/patches analogously.} A QCNN layer applies a local unitary $W^{(\ell)}$
over neighboring qubits and discards (traces out) pooled subsystems, yielding
\begin{equation}
\rho^{(\ell+1)} \;=\; \mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{pool}}
\!\left[\,W^{(\ell)}\,\rho^{(\ell)}\,W^{(\ell)\dagger}\right],
\label{eq:qcnn-pooling}
\end{equation}
which reduces degrees of freedom while propagating entanglement to coarser scales (MERA–like hierarchy~\cite{Cong2019,Vidal2007MERA}).
Measurements of local observables at each scale provide quantum ``feature maps'' that feed subsequent
quantum or classical processing. The choice of encoding $U_{\phi}$ on patches, entangling topology in $W^{(\ell)}$,
and measurement set $\{\hat{O}\}$ governs the inductive bias and the shot/gradient budget.
Feature–map perspectives (e.g.\ kernel embeddings) clarify how certain encoders may induce
advantageous geometries for classification~\cite{Havlicek2019,Schuld2019PRA}. Consequently, QCNN design favors shallow, locality–preserving ans\"atze with encoders aligned to image patches,
which we operationalize in the architecture and experiments that follow.

\paragraph{Implications for hybrid Q-CNNs.}
These ingredients inform our hybrid quantum convolutional design: (i) patch-wise rotation encoding to respect image locality and to keep state preparation scalable; (ii) shallow entangling blocks arranged in a convolution-like tiling to capture short-range correlations with limited receptive fields; (iii) measurement-defined feature maps serving as quantum ``filters'' whose outputs feed classical layers; and (iv) training via parameter-shift gradients with shot budgeting matched to layer sensitivity. By aligning encoding, ansatz structure, and gradient estimation with the inductive biases of convolutional processing, we obtain architectures that are both hardware-aware and well-posed for the benchmarks presented in the following sections; Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid_overview} renders this hybrid pipeline schematically.

The schematic in Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid_overview} traces the four stages of
the hybrid pipeline introduced above. Classical features
$x_1,x_2,x_3$ are first compressed by a conventional dense layer into a
low-dimensional latent representation $h_1,h_2$ that respects the patch
budget of the available qubits. The latent activations are then encoded
into single-qubit rotations $R(h_1)$ and $R(h_2)$, realising the
angle-encoding feature map of point (i) above. The variational unitary
$U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ implements the shallow entangling block of
point (ii) and acts as the quantum counterpart of a convolutional filter:
its parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are the learnable weights updated
by parameter-shift gradients (point iv). The two measured expectation
values $m_1,m_2$ — the measurement-defined feature maps of point (iii) —
are finally fed back into a classical readout layer producing the
network output $y$. The diagram is therefore a literal pictorial
realisation of the four design choices listed in the previous paragraph,
and the rest of this chapter quantifies each of them in turn.




\section{QCNN architecture and training protocol}

Our quantum convolutional layer maps small image patches to expectation values via a PQC composed of:
(i) angle (rotation) encoding of pixel intensities; (ii) a hardware-efficient entangling ansatz~\cite{Kandala2017};
and (iii) Pauli measurements whose outcomes form the convolved feature map. In our implementation the data are embedded by single-qubit $R_y$ rotations
(angle encoding), and the trainable block interleaves Hadamards, short-range CNOT
chains, and $R_z$ rotations~\cite{Qiskit2019}.
Device models and timings used throughout are taken from Qiskit \emph{BackendV2}/\emph{Target}~\cite{QiskitBackendV2Docs,QiskitTargetDocs}.


\subsection{Quantum convolutional layer}
We realise a quantum convolution as a local, patch-wise mapping from pixels to expectation values computed by a parameterised quantum circuit (PQC). For an image $X\!\in\![0,1]^{H\times W}$ and a sliding window of size $k\times k$ and stride $s$, each vectorised patch $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $d=k^2$ is transformed into $C$ quantum ``channels''
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{p}) \;=\; \big(\langle M_1\rangle_{\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta}},\dots,\langle M_C\rangle_{\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big),\qquad
\langle M_c\rangle_{\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta}} \;=\; \bra{0}U^\dagger(\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,M_c\,U(\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta})\ket{0},
\label{eq:qconv-core}
\end{equation}
where $M_c$ are Pauli observables and $U(\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta})=W(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,S(\mathbf{p})$ splits data encoding $S$ from the trainable ansatz $W$ \cite{Benedetti2019,cerezo2021}.
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:qconv_schematic}, the proposed QConv layer
follows the structure of a variational quantum circuit, where each image patch is encoded into a quantum state via a rotation-based feature map,
processed by a trainable variational ansatz, and measured to obtain expectation
values that act as quantum-derived features for the classical CNN. Hence, 
$W(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is a trainable block that we realise as a product of \emph{G-blocks}
\begin{equation*}
W(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \;=\; \overleftarrow{\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}} \;\Bigg(\; \prod_{i \in \mathcal{G}_{\ell}} G_{i}^{(\ell)} \Bigg),
\end{equation*}
with the left arrow indicating that blocks at larger $\ell$ are applied later (i.e., rightmost acts first).

Figure~\ref{fig:qconv_schematic} provides the conceptual template that
underpins our quantum convolutional layer. The diagram identifies four
functional blocks — classical input patches at the entry, a quantum
feature encoder, a stack of trainable unitary layers, and an output
measurement stage — and assigns to each the same role it would play in
a classical convolutional network: encoding maps raw inputs into a
representation that exposes the relevant inductive biases, the trainable
unitaries play the role of learnable filters, and the measurement stage
produces the channel outputs that feed the next layer. Reading our own
quantum convolutional layer through this lens, the patch-wise rotation
encoding $S(\mathbf{p})$ defined below realises the
\emph{encoder} block, the hardware-efficient ansatz $W(\boldsymbol{\theta})$
realises the trainable unitary stack, and the per-channel expectation
values $\langle M_c\rangle$ realise the output stage; the workflow
follows the principle outlined in~\cite{SchuldPetruccione2021}, in
which each parametrised quantum circuit acts as a non-linear feature
extractor whose measurement outcomes provide a compact representation
for classical post-processing.


In the baseline, we adopt rotation (angle) encoding,
\begin{equation}
S(\mathbf{p}) \;=\; \bigotimes_{i=1}^{d} R_y\!\big(\alpha\,\tilde p_i\big), \qquad \tilde p_i = \frac{p_i-\mu}{\sigma},
\label{eq:angle-enc}
\end{equation}
with a global scale $\alpha$ to adapt the dynamic range. This is hardware-friendly and produces a rich trigonometric feature expansion whose frequency support can be amplified by depth and data re-uploading. Two possibly useful alternatives to explore are: (i) \emph{amplitude encoding}, $|\psi(\mathbf{p})\rangle = \sum_{i} (p_i/\|\mathbf{p}\|_2)\,|i\rangle$, which is qubit-efficient but requires nontrivial state preparation; and (ii) \emph{kernel/phase feature maps} (e.g., IQP-style) that implement problem-inspired kernels via commuting entanglers and data-dependent phases \cite{Havlicek2019, Schuld2020Expressivity}. 
The $U(\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta})$ decomposition is, as a matter of fact, a way to increase expressivity without extra qubits, through \emph{data re-uploading}:
\begin{equation}
U(\mathbf{p},\boldsymbol{\theta}) \;=\; \prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big[\, W_\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\ell)\,S(\mathbf{p}) \,\Big],
\label{eq:reupload}
\end{equation}
which composes encoders with learnable layers, enlarging the accessible Fourier spectrum \cite{PerezSalinas2020, Schuld2020Expressivity}.

The trainable block is a hardware-efficient ansatz. In the baseline circuit each $d$-qubit patch register is processed by a layer of Hadamards, a forward nearest-neighbour CNOT chain, a single $R_z$ rotation per qubit, and a reverse CNOT chain:
\begin{equation}
W(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \;=\; \Big(\!\!\prod_{i=d-2}^{0}\!\mathrm{CX}_{i,i+1}\Big)\Big(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d} R_z(\theta_{i})\Big)\Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d-2}\mathrm{CX}_{i,i+1}\Big)\Big(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d} H_i\Big),
\label{eq:hea}
\end{equation}
so that a single depth-$D{=}1$ layer carries $d$ trainable parameters; deeper variants stack $D$ such layers. Entanglement couples pixels within a patch, enabling non-separable features akin to classical cross-channel mixing, but excessive expressibility can harm trainability (Sec.~\ref{sec:train-opt}) \cite{Cong2019,Holmes2022PRXQ,Marrero2021PRXQ}. The shot-based estimator for each channel uses $N_s$ repetitions:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\langle M_c\rangle} \;=\; \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{s=1}^{N_s} m_{c,s},\quad m_{c,s}\in\{-1,+1\},\qquad
\mathrm{Var}\big[\widehat{\langle M_c\rangle}\big] \;=\; \frac{1-\langle M_c\rangle^2}{N_s}.
\label{eq:shotvar}
\end{equation}
Placing the vectors $\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{p})$ on the patch grid yields a tensor of size $H'\times W'\times C$ (stride- and padding-dependent) that serves as the quantum analogue of a convolutional activation and feeds subsequent (classical or quantum) layers \cite{LeCun1998,Cong2019}.

\textbf{Design notes and upgrades.} (a) Mix $R_y$ and $R_z$ encodings per wire to diversify frequency content; (b) use small, overlapping patches to share context; (c) add a lightweight pooling step by measuring and discarding selected qubits to form multiscale features, inspired by QCNN/MERA \cite{Cong2019,Pesah2021PRX}. 


\subsection{QCNN architectures}
We explore where to insert the quantum layer (early vs late), the number of channels (repetitions per patch),
and circuit depth. These factors control expressivity and generalisation~\cite{Benedetti2019,cerezo2021}.
Dropout and batch normalisation are used in the classical stack~\cite{Goodfellow2016}.

\paragraph{Inserting quantum layers and shaping their capacity.}
Denote by $\mathrm{QConv}(k,C,D,L)$ a quantum layer using $k{\times}k$ patches, $C$ channels, depth $D$ and $L$ re-uploading cycles (Eqs.~\ref{eq:reupload}–\ref{eq:hea}) (see Fig.~\ref{fig:qconv}).

We extract local patches of side-length $k$ (i.e., $k\times k$), with $c_{\text{in}}$ input channels, and
flatten them into $d$-dimensional feature vectors, where
$d \;=\; k^2\,c_{\text{in}}$ or the post-projection dimension, if a linear reduction is applied.

Each $d$-dimensional vector is encoded into a $d$-qubit register via a data-encoding unitary
$U_{\text{enc}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to \mathrm{SU}(2)^{\otimes d}$ (see Fig.~\ref{fig:qcnn_ansatz}).

    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{chapters/qa_figures/basic_entangled.png}

    CNOT topology, analogous to a fully connected layer in classical neural networks.

    entanglement with neighboring qubits. 
    Adapted from~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}.}
    \label{fig:qcnn_ansatz}
\end{figure}


The variational block then applies
$L$ layers of trainable unitaries $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, followed by Pauli measurements to produce
$C$ quantum feature maps per spatial location. The number of shots per measured channel is $N_s$.

Two insertion patterns are particularly meaningful. In an \emph{early-fusion} arrangement ($\mathrm{QConv}\!\to\!\mathrm{Conv}\!\to\!\cdots$) the quantum layer is placed first, exposing the classical trunk to quantum-enriched local features; in a \emph{late-fusion} arrangement ($\mathrm{Conv}\!\to\!\cdots\!\to\!\mathrm{QConv}\!\to\!\text{head}$) the order is reversed, turning higher-level classical features into compact quantum embeddings and measurements. We benchmark both in Sec.~\ref{sec:variants}.
Let the per-patch parameter count be $T\!=\!D d$ for the rotation layers in Eq.~\ref{eq:hea} (one $R_z$ per qubit per layer) and total parameters $\mathcal{O}(C\,T)$; capacity scales linearly in $C$ and $D$.

\paragraph{Pooling and hierarchy.}
As previously stated, QCNNs provide a principled way to compress representations by tracing out measured qubits. Following the pooling map of Eq.~\ref{eq:qcnn-pooling}, tracing out the measured qubits reduces width while propagating entanglement to coarser receptive fields (a MERA-like hierarchy)~\cite{Cong2019}. Practically, we implement a ``quantum stride'' by computing a subset of observables per patch and discarding the rest, aligning the spatial downsampling with the entangling light-cone.

\paragraph{Entanglement range vs.\ generalisation.}
Local entanglers (nearest-neighbor CNOTs) match the short-range statistics of natural scenes and help avoid overly expressive, 2-design-like behaviour that correlates with vanishing gradients \cite{Holmes2022PRXQ}. QCNN-style locality and logarithmic depth guarantee that gradients need not vanish exponentially with system size, improving trainability relative to global ansätze \cite{Pesah2021PRX}. Empirically, moderate $D$ (1–3) and small $k$ (2–4) give the best accuracy-to-variance trade-off on EuroSAT while preserving hardware viability.


\paragraph{Alternate ans\"atze and hybrids.}
Three practical enhancements can be layered onto the baseline. \emph{Data re-uploading stacks} (Eq.~\ref{eq:reupload}) boost spectral richness without adding qubits~\cite{PerezSalinas2020,Schuld2020Expressivity}. \emph{Kernel-style encoders} on patches (commuting phase layers) feed a shallow $W(\theta)$ for stability~\cite{Havlicek2019}. \emph{Serial hybrids} of the form $\mathrm{Conv}\!\to\!\mathrm{QConv}\!\to\!\mathrm{Conv}$ use the first classical block as a denoiser, the quantum layer as a non-classical mixer, and the last classical block to refine decision boundaries~\cite{LeCun1998}.
When resources allow, a small second $\mathrm{QConv}$ after classical pooling can capture larger-scale correlations without a large qubit count by using bigger strides.

In contrast to the data re-uploading strategy~\cite{PerezSalinas2020}, 
our quantum convolutional layer employs a single data-encoding stage followed by $L$ trainable variational blocks, 
without re-injecting classical inputs at each layer.
This design mirrors the locality and weight-sharing principles of classical convolution kernels,
while reducing the number of parameters and mitigating barren plateau effects~\cite{cerezo2021}.


\paragraph{Complexity.}
For each patch and channel, we need $N_s$ shots per observable; a full forward pass costs $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{patch}}\,C\,L\,D\,N_s)$ circuit evaluations. Late-fusion reduces $N_{\mathrm{patch}}$ because it acts on feature maps of smaller spatial size; early-fusion may provide a stronger signal to the classical trunk. In our experiments, we report both patterns and ablations over $C, D, L$ to measure accuracy vs.\ variance vs.\ time.
\paragraph{Ablation studies.}
To assess the relative contribution of circuit width, depth, and output dimensionality,
we performed a set of ablation studies varying the number of qubits ($d=k^2$), 
the ansatz depth ($D$), and the quantum channels ($C$) while keeping all other settings fixed. 
Each configuration was trained over ten runs to evaluate accuracy, variance, and runtime. 
We also compared early- and late-fusion schemes, isolating the effect of the 
insertion point of the quantum layer on overall model performance and computational cost.


\subsection{Dataset and preprocessing}
\label{sec:dataset-and-preprocessing}
We use EuroSAT (Sentinel-2) with $64{\times}64$ tiles and evaluate a binary subset to isolate the effect of quantum feature extraction \cite{Helber2019,Sebastianelli2021}. We keep the classical and hybrid pipelines as comparable as possible.
In this work, we limited the analysis to a binary subset of the EuroSAT dataset, 
comprising two of the ten available classes from the original 27{,}000-image collection, 
as a trade-off between architectural simplicity and computational feasibility.


\paragraph{Band selection and scaling.}
For RGB experiments we extract the three visible bands; for multispectral variants we pick physically motivated composites (e.g., adding NIR) that accentuate vegetation/soil contrasts. All images are resized to $H{\times}W$ divisible by the stride $s$ and normalised per image:
\begin{equation}
\tilde x \;=\; \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad \mu=\frac{1}{HW}\textstyle\sum_{i,j}\!x_{ij},\quad
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i,j}(x_{ij}-\mu)^2.
\end{equation}
Rotation encodings use $\theta=\alpha\,\tilde x$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:angle-enc}), while phase maps use $\varphi=\beta\,\tilde x$ to keep phases within $[-\pi,\pi]$.

\paragraph{Patch extraction and channels.}
We extract vectorised patches $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2}$ with kernel $k\!\in\!\{2,3,4\}$ and stride $s\!\in\!\{1,2\}$. Overlap (small $s$) increases context at the cost of more circuits. Multi-channel outputs can be realised by (a) different observables $M_c$ on the same wires, or (b) channel-specific ansätze $W_c(\theta_c)$ with shared $S(\mathbf{p})$. The latter increases capacity but also shots and parameter budgets.

\paragraph{Augmentation and splits.}
We apply light augmentations (horizontal/vertical flips, $\pm 10^\circ$ rotations) equally to classical and hybrid models. The dataset is stratified into 80\% train and 20\% validation, balanced per class. Batches mix patches from multiple images to reduce inter-sample correlations
and approximate independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sampling, ensuring unbiased gradient estimates for the classical head~\cite{Goodfellow2016}.

\paragraph{Why encoding design matters here.}
Angle encodings are robust and cheap; re-uploading can significantly improve fit in low-depth settings by enriching the frequency content \cite{PerezSalinas2020,Schuld2020Expressivity}. Kernel-style feature maps (e.g., data-dependent ZZ-phases on neighboring wires) induce inner-product geometries beneficial for some land-use separations \cite{Havlicek2019}. We used angle-only encoding and leave to future work to compare: (i) angle-only; (ii) angle+phase; (iii) re-uploaded angle. For completeness, a small amplitude-encoding variant on downsampled patches should also be benchmarked to assess the trade-off between state-preparation cost and qubit efficiency.

\paragraph{Outputs for downstream heads.}
The quantum feature tensor $F\in\mathbb{R}^{H'\times W'\times C}$ feeds either a shallow classical CNN head or a linear classifier. We matched heads across baselines so that performance differences reflect the quantum layer rather than a stronger classical readout. This way, we kept the readout capacity normalised so that any gain is due to the quantum convolution, not a stronger classifier.


\subsection{Training and optimisation}\label{sec:train-opt}
Our optimisation protocol is staged. First, we conduct architecture search and hyperparameter tuning entirely on \emph{simulators} within the Qiskit ecosystem, selecting a single best candidate per setting. Second, we run a focused \emph{hardware validation} of the selected candidate on an IQM superconducting device (54 qubits) accessed via the Qiskit-IQM adapter, to assess its robustness under realistic noise and topology constraints. The full hardware-validation campaign and its results are reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}.

\paragraph{Objective and gradient evaluation.}
Let $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(X)$ denote the logit produced after pooling the quantum feature tensor and a linear head. For binary tasks we minimise cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
= -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\!\big[y_n\log \sigma(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(X_n)) + (1-y_n)\log \!\big(1-\sigma(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(X_n))\big)\big],
\end{equation}
and compute PQC gradients with the \emph{parameter-shift} rule \cite{Schuld2019Grad,Mitarai2018}: for gates generated by Pauli operators,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j}\,\langle M\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
= \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\,\langle M\rangle_{\theta_j+\tfrac{\pi}{2}} - \langle M\rangle_{\theta_j-\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,\Big),
\label{eq:psr-34}
\end{equation}
so each partial derivative requires two additional forward evaluations. On Qiskit, we use the \emph{Estimator} primitive to obtain expectation values consistently across simulators and hardware backends~\cite{QiskitEstimatorDocs,Qiskit2019}. With $N_s$ shots per expectation, the estimator variance follows Eq.~\ref{eq:shotvar}, so its standard error scales as $\mathcal{O}(N_s^{-1/2})$. Hence we schedule $N_s$ to increase during training (low $N_s$ for exploration, high $N_s$ for convergence) to lower the standard error.

\paragraph{Simulator-first search (Qiskit 2.2).}
For relatively rapid prototyping, we rely on Qiskit Aer backends under Qiskit~2.2 (primitives V2). Where relevant, we use (i) exact statevector simulation for sanity checks (no sampling noise), (ii) shot-based simulation to match the training signal of hardware, and (iii) device-aware transpilation (layout and coupling map) to estimate depth and two-qubit counts faithfully. For IQM-targeted experiments, we employ the IQM Qiskit adapter to simulate circuits with IQM-specific noise models (when available) and topology, so that the selected candidate is realistic with respect to gate set, connectivity, and expected error channels \cite{IQMClientDocs2025,QiskitOnIQMGuide}. The per-batch cost of a forward–backward pass is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}\!\big(N_{\text{patch}}\;C\,L\,D\,N_s\big)
\end{equation}
circuit executions (same notation as above), which we keep tractable by small patches, moderate depth $D$, and re-uploading for spectral richness rather than extra qubits.

\paragraph{Hardware validation (IQM Emerald, 54 qubits via Qiskit).}
The selected candidate is validated on the IQM Emerald 54-qubit superconducting processor through the Qiskit-IQM adapter (same Estimator interface as in simulation). Circuits are transpiled to the device's coupling graph using a calibration-aware initial layout that maps each parallel block onto a high-readout-fidelity 4-qubit chain (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}); the ansatz uses nearest-neighbor entanglers, and the shot budget is set to $N_s=500$ per observable, balancing gradient stability against queue and credit costs. Measurement error mitigation and basic readout calibration are enabled when supported by the provider, complementing the gradient-norm clipping and early-stopping heuristics already in use. Following common practice, we \emph{do not} re-tune the full architecture on hardware: only the trainable quantum weights $\boldsymbol{w}$---the $R_z$ angles of Eq.~\ref{eq:hea}, i.e.\ the quantum components of the variational parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$---are updated by parameter-shift gradients on the QPU, with the classical backbone frozen at its simulator-pretrained values, ensuring a fair sim-to-device transfer. The detailed hardware-validation methodology and results are reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}.

\paragraph{Entanglement, trainability, and generalisation.}
Entanglement within a patch is key to building non-separable features; however, over-expressive, randomizing ansätze correlate with vanishing gradients (\emph{barren plateaus}) \cite{McClean2018,Holmes2022PRXQ,Marrero2021PRXQ}. Our QCNN-style, locality-preserving construction and shallow depth mitigate this effect, and theory guarantees that QCNN gradients need not vanish exponentially with system size \cite{Pesah2021PRX}. From a sample-efficiency perspective, keeping the number of trainable gates modest and pooling before the linear head improves generalisation from few examples \cite{Caro2022NatCommun}. Empirically, on both simulators and hardware, we observe that re-uploading (fixed qubit count, moderate $D$) matches or outperforms deeper, more entangling variants—supporting the view that encoding frequency content is as impactful as raw circuit depth.

\paragraph{Optimizers and schedules.}
We adopt Adam with warmup and cosine decay, gradient-norm clipping, and a two-phase shot schedule $N_{s,t}$: (i) simulator phase—small $N_s$ for fast model selection; (ii) hardware phase—larger $N_s$ on the shortlisted candidate. This preserves iteration speed during search and allocates physical runs only where they are most informative.

\subsection{Runtime-aware metrics and batch-time estimation}
\label{sec:runtime-metric}

\subsubsection*{Preliminaries and related work}
Modern compilers expose a \emph{scheduled} circuit duration (the “makespan”) once gates are assigned start times from calibrated instruction durations. In Qiskit, this follows as-soon-as-possible / as-late-as-possible (ASAP/ALAP) scheduling using backend timing information (Target / \texttt{InstructionDurations}); the circuit then carries a total \texttt{duration} in units of \texttt{dt}~\cite{InstructionDurations,ASAPScheduleAnalysis,McKay2018BackendSpec,QiskitBackendV2Docs,QiskitTargetDocs,OpenQASM3TQC2022}. The same abstraction is available when evaluating via Qiskit Primitives (e.g., the \emph{Estimator}) on simulators and hardware~\cite{QiskitEstimatorDocs,Qiskit2019}. 


Closely related notions, such as \emph{critical-path} and \emph{latency estimation}, are standard in CAD (Computer-Aided Design) and EDA (Electronic Design Automation) methodologies, where timing analysis is used to estimate the execution latency of digital circuits.  
Analogously, quantum compilers and mappers adopt CAD-inspired approaches to analyse the dataflow and resource constraints of quantum programs, providing runtime estimates even before full scheduling or placement is performed.

A representative example is \textsc{LEQA} (Latency Estimation for a Quantum Algorithm)~\cite{Dousti2013LEQA}, 
which predicts the total execution latency of a mapped quantum circuit with negligible runtime overhead and less than a few percent error compared to complete scheduling.  
Similarly, timing- and resource-aware mapping techniques for NISQ processors~\cite{Lao2020TimingMapping} 
extend these CAD principles to architectures with limited qubit connectivity, optimising for both depth and physical latency.  
Within our framework, such concepts inform the definition of \emph{runtime-aware metrics}, 
where circuit duration (\texttt{circuit.duration} in Qiskit) and estimated batch execution time serve as proxies for hardware-level performance.


In all experiments, device timing (e.g., \texttt{dt}) and instruction properties are queried through Qiskit’s \emph{BackendV2}/\emph{Target} interface (SDK~2.x), which supersedes the legacy BackendV1 $\xrightarrow{}$ Qobj $\xrightarrow{}$ OpenPulse specification; timing semantics follow OpenQASM~3~\cite{QiskitBackendV2Docs,QiskitTargetDocs,OpenQASM3TQC2022}.


\subsubsection*{Batch-time and credit-cost model used in practice}
\label{sec:batch-time-model}

The runtime metrics of the previous section are circuit-level: they describe a
\emph{single} scheduled circuit. In practice, training a hybrid quantum--classical
model on a credit-bounded provider requires forecasting the cost of an entire
optimisation \emph{epoch}, which involves thousands of bound circuits dispatched
in jobs of bounded size. We adopt the same notation used in the production
notebook (Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}, Phase D and Phase E):

\begin{description}
\setlength\itemsep{0.15em}
\item[$K$] — number of parallel quanvolutional blocks instantiated inside one
  bound circuit (\texttt{num\_parallel\_blocks}; in our deployment $K=11$, capped
  by the toolchain rather than by the device's 54 qubits, see
  Sec.~\ref{subsec:transpilation}).
\item[$n_w$] — number of trainable quantum weights per block ($n_w=4$ in the
  ansatz of Sec.~\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd}).
\item[$m$] — mini-batch size (\texttt{MINIBATCH\_SIZE}; $m=2$ in production).
\item[$p_{\rm img}$] — number of patches extracted per image, with
  $p_{\rm img}=C_{\rm conv}\cdot Q^2$ where $C_{\rm conv}=6$ is the number of
  convolutional channels and $Q=5$ is the quanvolutional output side
  (Sec.~\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd}); thus $p_{\rm img}=150$.
\item[$N_s$] — shots per circuit (\texttt{SHOTS}; $N_s=500$).
\item[$\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$] — empirical wall-clock time per bound circuit
  observed at the Python client; calibrated in Phase D
  (Sec.~\ref{subsec:hw_results}).
\item[$r_{\rm wc}$] — wall-time-to-credit conversion rate (\texttt{CRED\_PER\_SEC\_EMERALD}),
  calibrated against the dashboard billing.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Number of bound circuits per training step.}
With frozen backbone (Sec.~\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd}), every optimiser step
requires $1+2n_w$ forward passes through the quantum filter (one for the
objective, $2n_w$ for the parameter-shift gradients of the quantum weights).
Each pass must process $m\cdot p_{\rm img}$ patches; with $K$ blocks
instantiated in parallel inside a single bound circuit, those patches are
packed into $\lceil m\,p_{\rm img}/K\rceil$ bound circuits. Hence
\begin{equation}
N_{\rm c}^{\rm step}
\;=\; \bigl(1+2 n_w\bigr)\,\Bigl\lceil \tfrac{m\,p_{\rm img}}{K} \Bigr\rceil .
\label{eq:N_step}
\end{equation}
For our production values ($n_w=4$, $m=2$, $p_{\rm img}=150$, $K=11$),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:N_step} gives $N_{\rm c}^{\rm step}=9\cdot\lceil 300/11\rceil = 9\cdot 28 = 252$
bound circuits per step.

\paragraph{Number of bound circuits per epoch.}
Let $n_{\rm steps}=\lfloor N_{\rm train}/m\rfloor$ be the number of optimiser
steps per epoch ($n_{\rm steps}=10$ for $N_{\rm train}=20$, $m=2$), and
$N_{\rm val}$ the size of the held-out validation set ($N_{\rm val}=20$). The
end-of-epoch validation pass is a single forward call and packs into
$N_{\rm c}^{\rm val}=\lceil N_{\rm val}\, p_{\rm img}/K\rceil$ bound circuits.
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
N_{\rm c}^{\rm ep}
\;=\; n_{\rm steps}\cdot N_{\rm c}^{\rm step} \;+\; N_{\rm c}^{\rm val}
\;=\; n_{\rm steps}\,(1+2 n_w)\,\Bigl\lceil \tfrac{m\,p_{\rm img}}{K} \Bigr\rceil
       \;+\; \Bigl\lceil \tfrac{N_{\rm val}\,p_{\rm img}}{K} \Bigr\rceil .
\label{eq:N_epoch}
\end{equation}
For the Phase~E configuration, Eq.~\eqref{eq:N_epoch} gives
$10\cdot 252 + \lceil 20\cdot 150/11\rceil = 2520 + 273 = 2793$ bound circuits
per epoch.

\paragraph{Wall-time and credit cost per epoch.}
The wall-clock time of one epoch and its credit cost are obtained by
multiplying the bound-circuit count by the per-circuit wall time and the
wall-to-credit rate, respectively:
\begin{equation}
T_{\rm ep}^{\rm wall} \;=\; N_{\rm c}^{\rm ep}\,\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall},
\qquad
\boxed{\;
\widehat{\mathrm{cred}}_{\rm ep}
\;=\; N_{\rm c}^{\rm ep}\,\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}\,r_{\rm wc} \;}
\label{eq:cred_epoch}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cred_epoch} is the \emph{in-flight} cost estimate computed by the
production notebook before each epoch is launched; it intentionally over-counts
with respect to the IQM dashboard billing because $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$
includes non-billed phases (validation, compile, queue, post-processing,
network return). The relationship between the in-flight estimate and the
\emph{a posteriori} billed cost is calibrated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}.

\paragraph{Calibration of $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$.}
The per-circuit wall time depends weakly on the bound-circuit count $N$ within
a job (because of the per-job initialisation overhead) and on the system load
at submission time. We calibrate $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$ in Phase~D
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:hw_results}) by submitting two diagnostic batches at
$N=20$ and $N=50$ and reading back the Python wall-clock time. The realised
in-production value over the 183 Phase~E jobs is
$\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}\approx 0.56$~s/circuit (median of the per-job
amortisation), bracketed by the two diagnostic measurements.

% --- Algorithm ---
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\texttt{estimate\_cost\_per\_epoch}$(\,$config$,\;\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall},\;r_{\rm wc}\,)$ —
matches Phase~D code in \texttt{hybrid\_qcnn\_v4\_3\_0\_emerald.ipynb}.}
\label{alg:estimate-cost-per-epoch}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Configuration with $K$, $n_w$, $m$, $p_{\rm img}$, $N_{\rm train}$,
         $N_{\rm val}$; calibrated wall-time-per-circuit
         $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$ (s); wall-to-credit rate $r_{\rm wc}$
         (cred/s).
\State $N_{\rm c}^{\rm pass} \gets \lceil m\, p_{\rm img}\,/\, K\rceil$
         \Comment{circuits per single forward / shift pass}
\State $N_{\rm c}^{\rm step} \gets (1+2 n_w)\,N_{\rm c}^{\rm pass}$
         \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:N_step}, frozen backbone}
\State $n_{\rm steps} \gets \lfloor N_{\rm train}/m\rfloor$
\State $N_{\rm c}^{\rm val} \gets \lceil N_{\rm val}\, p_{\rm img}\,/\,K\rceil$
\State $N_{\rm c}^{\rm ep} \gets n_{\rm steps}\, N_{\rm c}^{\rm step} + N_{\rm c}^{\rm val}$
         \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:N_epoch}}
\State $T_{\rm ep}^{\rm wall} \gets N_{\rm c}^{\rm ep}\,\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$
\State $\widehat{\mathrm{cred}}_{\rm ep} \gets T_{\rm ep}^{\rm wall}\,r_{\rm wc}$
         \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:cred_epoch}}
\State \Return $\widehat{\mathrm{cred}}_{\rm ep},\; T_{\rm ep}^{\rm wall},\; N_{\rm c}^{\rm ep}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Why we do not propagate per-shot times explicitly.}
The shot count $N_s$ does not appear explicitly in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:N_step}--\eqref{eq:cred_epoch}. The reason is operational: on
the IQM Resonance API the shot loop is amortised inside the per-circuit pulse
schedule and is not user-controllable on a finer granularity than the bound
circuit. The dependence of $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$ on $N_s$ is therefore
absorbed into the calibration constant of Phase~D, and any change in $N_s$
requires a re-calibration. For the campaign reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}
we kept $N_s=500$ throughout, so this distinction is immaterial in our setting.

\paragraph{Practical guidelines.}
Three operational habits keep the cost model trustworthy in practice. The wall-time-per-circuit $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$ must be calibrated on the actual submission pattern (same $N$ per job, same connectivity) immediately before launching a cost-bounded campaign, since cached schedules and cold-start effects can shift it by up to $\pm 30\%$. The number of parallel blocks $K$ should be set as close as possible to the toolchain limit $K_{\max}$ to amortise $\tau_{\rm circ}^{\rm wall}$ over more patches per circuit, with the caveat that the speed-up is linear only up to the additional depth and SWAP overhead introduced by the layout of Sec.~\ref{subsec:transpilation}. Finally, the in-flight cost projection $\widehat{\mathrm{cred}}_{\rm ep}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cred_epoch} should be treated as a conservative upper bound: on our campaign it overestimated the actual billed cost by $\sim 24\%$ (Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}), so if credit headroom matters one should plan against this estimate but reconcile against the dashboard after the first epoch.

\subsection*{The EuroSAT dataset}

As described in Sec.~\ref{sec:dataset-and-preprocessing}, we adopt the EuroSAT dataset~\cite{Helber2019}, a publicly available collection of
RGB and multispectral satellite images derived from Sentinel-2 data.
It comprises ten land-use and land-cover classes---\emph{Residential, Industrial,
Highway, River, Sea Lake, Forest, Annual Crop, Permanent Crop, Herbaceous Vegetation,}
and \emph{Pasture}---each containing 2,000--3,000 labeled patches of $64\times64$ pixels.
For our binary classification experiments, subsets of these classes were selected
and balanced to form the training and test partitions described in Sec.~\ref{sec:dataset-and-preprocessing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.5]{chapters/qa_figures/eurosat.png}

    \emph{Residential, Industrial, Highway, River, Sea Lake, Forest, Annual Crop,

The dataset provides a realistic testbed for quantum-assisted image classification,
as the spectral variability and inter-class overlap challenge both classical
and hybrid quantum--classical models.
To reduce dimensionality while retaining discriminative structure, each
$64\times64\times3$ image is converted to a set of $N_\mathrm{patch}$ subpatches,
normalised to $[0,1]$, and encoded into amplitude- or angle-based quantum embeddings
as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:dataset-and-preprocessing}

\paragraph{Implementation.}
Classical components are implemented in PyTorch; quantum circuits are executed via Qiskit primitives with parameter-shift gradients. Unless otherwise noted, we train for 10 epochs with minibatches sized to keep GPU and simulator utilisation high; 10 epochs are sufficient to bring both architectures past the plateau documented in~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis} while keeping the simulator-time per seed manageable across the $R=10$ replications of Sec.~\ref{sec:stats-significance}. Loss is cross-entropy; classical derivatives are computed by the host framework (and checked with finite-difference when needed), quantum derivatives via parameter-shift~\cite{Schuld2019Grad}. Accuracy is reported as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}\,,
\end{equation}
and complements loss curves to assess generalisation.

\paragraph{Hardware note.}
All model selection is performed on device-aware simulators. To validate portability and timing, we executed the selected candidate on an IQM Emerald 54-qubit backend through the Qiskit-IQM interface, confirming functional correctness and latency trends predicted by our scheduled-duration analysis (see Sec.~\ref{sec:runtime-metric}); the full hardware-validation report, including a credit-bounded fine-tuning campaign on Emerald, is given in Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}.

\subsection{Learning curves}
\label{sec:results-curves}

\paragraph{Why a simulator pre-training stage.}
We refer to the training run summarised in
Fig.~\ref{fig:training-dynamics} as a \emph{pre-training} stage rather
than simply ``training'' because of the hardware-validation strategy
that closes this chapter (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}). The
end-of-run weights of this simulator stage are not the final deployment
of the hybrid Q-CNN: they are the checkpoint from which a subsequent,
credit-bounded \emph{fine-tuning} run is launched directly on the IQM
Emerald quantum processor. The split between a simulator-based
pre-training phase and an on-device fine-tuning phase is forced by the
economics of cloud-accessed superconducting QPUs. A full $R=10$ multi-seed,
ten-epoch training campaign on Emerald at the production
$N_{\mathrm{train}}=200$ scale of the simulator would consume on the
order of $\sim 10^{5}$ credits (Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}), against
the $2{,}000$-credit budget allocated to this campaign; the production
runs on Emerald therefore freeze the classical trunk at its
simulator-pretrained values and update only the four trainable quantum
weights $\boldsymbol{w}$ via parameter-shift gradients on the device
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd}). This is a routine choice in the
hybrid quantum--classical literature, and it has the additional benefit
of decoupling the model-selection question (best on simulator) from the
noise-robustness question (best after on-device fine-tuning).

Figure~\ref{fig:training-dynamics} compares the learning curves (cross-entropy loss and binary accuracy versus epoch) of our hybrid Q-CNN backbone against the classical-only ablation, both trained on the simulator under identical preprocessing, optimiser settings and binary subset of EuroSAT (Forest vs.\ AnnualCrop, $10$ images/class for both train and val splits). The two models share the convolutional feature extractor; the only difference is whether the quanvolutional layer is replaced by a classical pointwise convolution (the classical ablation, Sec.~\ref{sec:variants}) or by the parameterised quantum filter (the hybrid Q-CNN). Both models reach $\geq 90\%$ validation accuracy within a handful of epochs and saturate at $\approx 95\%$ at the end of pre-training; the validation curves are essentially indistinguishable beyond epoch~6. The across-seed learning dynamics---including the early-epoch behaviour and the per-step loss variance---are quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:qcnn_training_dynamics}.

\subsection{Architectural search and the architecture adopted here}
\label{sec:variants}

The design space of the quanvolutional pipeline --- the placement of the
quantum convolution (early versus late fusion with respect to the classical
convolutional trunk), the number of parallel quantum channels per patch, and
the depth of the variational block --- was explored in the prior single-seed
master's thesis of Filippi~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}, carried out at the
University of Pisa under the co-supervision of the present author and Oliver
Morsch. That exploration found late fusion (classical $\to$ classical $\to$
quantum) to be systematically preferable to early fusion and identified
\texttt{C16--Q64} as its strongest single-seed configuration. We summarise it
here because the present chapter inherits its qualitative design lesson; the
architecture studied below, however, departs from that winner, for the reasons
set out at the end of this section.

Tab.~\ref{tab:qcnn-variants} reproduces the validation metrics obtained
in~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis} on the same binary EuroSAT subset adopted
throughout this chapter. The dominant pattern is that late fusion
(classical $\to$ classical $\to$ quantum) consistently outperforms
early fusion (quantum $\to$ classical $\to$ classical) by roughly
$8$~pp of accuracy, with the best configuration being
\texttt{C16--Q64} at validation loss $0.25$ and validation accuracy
$91\%$. The intuition supporting late fusion is that the classical
trunk supplies low-variance, denoised features to the quantum kernel,
which improves trainability of the parameter-shift gradients on a
shallow variational block; the alternate reading via the trainability
literature is that locality-preserving encoders on already pooled
features avoid the expressivity-induced barren-plateau regime of
deeper, globally entangling blocks~\cite{Holmes2022PRXQ,Pesah2021PRX}.
For the same reason, increasing the number of quantum channels (e.g.\
from \texttt{C6--Q16} to \texttt{C16--Q64}) is preferable to stacking a
second quantum layer in series (e.g.\ \texttt{C6--2Q6}), which yields
only partial gains over the single-layer counterpart, consistent with
the depth/SNR trade-offs known for PQCs~\cite{cerezo2021}.

A qualitative early-epoch behaviour also documented
in~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis} on the late-fusion variants is a
pronounced first-epoch accuracy jump (from $\sim 30\%$ to $\sim 85\%$)
followed by rapid stabilisation within $\sim 10$ epochs; this is
consistent with the feature-map view of the quantum
layer~\cite{Schuld2019PRA,Havlicek2019}, and is the original
observation that motivated the multi-seed statistical investigation we
develop in the next section to test whether the same epoch~$1$
advantage survives the seed-to-seed variability.

\paragraph{The architecture adopted here.}
We retain the late-fusion arrangement favoured by the search above, but we do
not adopt the \texttt{C16--Q64} winner itself. Two considerations motivate a
leaner design. First, the comparison we set out to make requires an $R=10$
multi-seed campaign (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}) and a fine-tuning campaign on
physical hardware (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}); replicating and transferring
the $\sim\!85.6\times10^{6}$-parameter \texttt{C16--Q64} ten times over is not
compatible with the available simulator time and hardware-credit budget. Second,
and independently of cost, a model of that capacity is disproportionate to the
task: the benchmark is a binary classification on a reduced EuroSAT subset of a
few hundred images, a regime in which tens of millions of parameters are far
more than the data can constrain and tend to favour overfitting over
generalisation. We therefore study a deliberately compact architecture of our
own: a six-channel convolutional trunk feeding a nine-qubit quanvolutional block
($\sim\!4.6\times10^{5}$ trainable parameters in total), reduced to four qubits
for the Emerald hardware runs. The high-capacity \texttt{C16--Q64} re-enters the
study only as the capacity target of the high-capacity classical reference
(Level~1 of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}), where it serves to bound, rather than
to instantiate, the comparison.

\subsection{Comparison to baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
We benchmark our hybrid Q-CNN against two classical/hybrid references. As a published hybrid remote-sensing model we cite Sebastianelli et al.~\cite{Sebastianelli2021}; we report performance figures only qualitatively, since the original publication does not provide seed-resolved data and a paired comparison is therefore not available.

\paragraph{Filippi's classical baseline and the capacity confounder.}
The prior thesis work of Filippi~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis} compared the same \texttt{C16--Q64} hybrid architecture against a compact LeNet-5-style CNN with $6/16$ convolutional channels~\cite{LeCun1998} on a two-class EuroSAT subset, reporting validation loss $0.37$ for the classical baseline against $0.25$ for the hybrid. As reported in~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}, the LeNet-5 baseline has substantially fewer trainable parameters than the hybrid \texttt{C16--Q64}, so the loss gap, when read as a quantum-vs-classical contrast, conflates two distinct effects: the contribution of the quantum convolutional layer, and the contribution of the larger $16/32/64$-channel classical trunk that surrounds it. The work reported in this thesis builds on Filippi's quanvolutional ansatz design (Eq.~\ref{eq:parallel_circuit}, Fig.~\ref{fig:qconv}, Fig.~\ref{fig:qcnn_ansatz}, all adapted from~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis} as flagged in the respective captions) and extends it along three new directions of our own.

\paragraph{Original contributions of this work.}
First, we replace Filippi's LeNet-5 reference with two newly trained classical controls described in detail in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}: a high-capacity ablation that matches the parameter count of Filippi's original \texttt{C16--Q64} hybrid (Level~1 of that section, $\sim 85.6\times 10^{6}$ trainable parameters), and a structurally isomorphic matched-capacity ablation that differs from the hybrid only by the substitution of the quanvolutional block with a parameter-equivalent $\mathrm{Conv2d}(6{\to}6,\,k{=}3)$ block (Level~2, $\sim 4.64\times 10^{5}$ trainable parameters, within $0.07\%$ of the simulator-trained hybrid of Level~3). Second, we run all three architectures in an $R=10$ multi-seed campaign on a fixed train/validation partition, enabling the paired non-parametric statistics that Filippi's single-seed report could not support (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}). Third, the hybrid Q-CNN is transferred to physical quantum hardware (IQM Emerald, Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}) with a credit-bounded fine-tuning campaign, an experimental dimension that is not present in~\cite{Filippi2025Thesis}.

The corresponding numerical results, reported in detail in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}, show that with both classical references in place the matched-capacity classical control (Level~2) reaches $0.9700\pm 0.0133$ final-epoch validation accuracy against $0.9445\pm 0.0243$ for the simulator-trained hybrid (Level~3), a paired difference of $-2.6$ percentage points at $p=2.7\times 10^{-2}$; conversely, at epoch~$1$ the hybrid is $\sim 29$ percentage points ahead of the same matched-capacity control, indicating a training-efficiency advantage that does not translate into an asymptotic-accuracy advantage on this dataset. The pattern is consistent with localised quantum feature maps producing informative features earlier than a freshly initialised classical convolution of equal parameter count, while not exceeding the asymptotic representational capacity of the latter on a saturated binary task.

\subsection{Statistical uncertainty across runs}
\label{sec:stats-significance}

The variability we report on accuracy and loss in the next sections comes
from a deliberately simple, replication-based protocol. Each of the three
architectures we benchmark in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats} — a
high-capacity classical CNN reference (Level~1, $\sim 85.6\times 10^{6}$
trainable parameters), a matched-capacity classical CNN control
(Level~2, $\sim 4.64\times 10^{5}$ trainable parameters, isomorphic to
the hybrid except for the substitution of the quanvolutional block with
an equivalent classical convolution), and the hybrid Q-CNN itself
(Level~3, $\sim 4.64\times 10^{5}$ trainable parameters) — is trained
from scratch over $R=10$ independent runs that differ only in the
random seed used to initialise the weights and to shuffle the
mini-batches; the train/validation partition is held fixed across runs: the EuroSAT
classes Forest and AnnualCrop are split $80/20$ into disjoint training and
validation directories, from each of which a fixed subset of $100$ images
per class is drawn, giving $N_{\mathrm{train}}=200$ and $N_{\mathrm{val}}=200$
items shared across all seeds. The purely quantum reference of
Sebastianelli et al.~\cite{Sebastianelli2021} is discussed qualitatively
in Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines} but is not included in the across-seed
protocol of this section, since the original publication does not
provide seed-resolved data. For every metric of
interest (epoch-level training and validation accuracy, validation
cross-entropy loss) we then compute, separately at each epoch
$e=1,\dots,10$, the across-run mean
\(
\bar{m}(e) = R^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R} m_r(e)
\)
and the across-run standard deviation
\(
s(e) = \bigl[\,(R-1)^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\,(m_r(e)-\bar{m}(e))^{2}\,\bigr]^{1/2}
\),
and we display $\bar{m}(e)\pm s(e)$ in the figures of
Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}. We complement the descriptive $\pm s$ bands
with three inferential ingredients applied only to the final-epoch validation
accuracy: a per-run Wilson 95\% confidence interval at $N_{\mathrm{val}}=200$
(which captures the binomial sampling noise of each single seed's
accuracy estimate), a non-parametric bootstrap 95\% confidence interval on
the across-seed mean (resampling the $R=10$ seeds with replacement,
$10\,000$ resamples), and a Wilcoxon signed-rank paired test on the $R=10$
seed-by-seed accuracy differences between the two architectures, with
pairing on the \texttt{run\_idx}. With $R=10$ the smallest two-sided
$p$-value achievable by the exact Wilcoxon test is
$2/2^{10}\approx 0.002$, reached when all ten signs of the per-seed
difference agree~\cite{Wilcoxon1945}.\footnote{The exact signed-rank test handles zero per-seed differences by Wilcoxon's reduction: they are discarded and the effective sample size $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is reduced accordingly. At $N_{\mathrm{val}}=200$ every accuracy is an integer multiple of $1/200$, so paired differences are snapped to this quantisation grid before ranking: ties in $|\Delta|$ are genuine and receive midranks, and rounding the accuracies alone would not remove floating-point artefacts from the differences. Where all non-zero $|\Delta|$ are tied at one grid step (the noise contrast of Sec.~\ref{sec:noise_resilience}), the signed-rank statistic degenerates to the sign test. Genuine zeros also occur (e.g.\ seed $486$ in the Level-1 contrast).} We did not compute McNemar
contingency tests on individual validation items, because the same items
appear in every run and are therefore not exchangeable across runs.

\subsection{Results and statistical robustness}
\label{sec:results-stats}

To ensure statistical robustness, three architectures are trained and
validated over $R=10$ independent runs with the seed schedule
$s_{r} = 42 + 111\,r$ for $r=0,\dots,9$ on the same train/validation
partition. The three architectures form a deliberate three-level
comparison aimed at separating capacity effects from architectural
effects:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Level 1 (CCNN-big).} A high-capacity classical convolutional
network with three convolutional blocks of $16$, $32$, $64$ channels
followed by a $\texttt{LazyLinear}(16384{\to}5221)$ head. Total trainable
parameters: $85{,}617{,}041$. This is the classical reference originally
named the ``matched-capacity'' control in the campaign because its
adaptation block was sized to the \texttt{C16--Q64} candidate of
Tab.~\ref{tab:qcnn-variants}; we re-label it here as ``high-capacity'' to
reflect that the hybrid Q-CNN actually trained in the simulator-based
campaign of this section is a lighter variant (see Level~3 below), and we
add an explicit \emph{matched-capacity} control at Level~2.
\item \textbf{Level 2 (CCNN-small).} A matched-capacity classical CNN
whose trunk is structurally isomorphic to the hybrid Q-CNN of Level~3
(two convolutional blocks of $6$ channels with $5{\times}5$ kernels and
$2{\times}2$ max-pooling, BatchNorm), with the quanvolutional layer
replaced by a $\mathrm{Conv2d}(6{\to}6,\,k{=}3,\,p{=}0)+\mathrm{BN}+\mathrm{ReLU}$
block of equivalent input--output shape, followed by an identical linear
head $\mathrm{Linear}(1176{\to}392{\to}2)$. Total trainable parameters:
$463{,}898$, i.e.\ within $0.07\%$ of the hybrid Q-CNN of Level~3 below.
This is the like-for-like comparison.
\item \textbf{Level 3 (hybrid Q-CNN).} The simulator-trained Hybrid
Q--CNN of this section, run on Qiskit~Aer in noiseless statevector mode.
The quanvolutional layer uses a $3{\times}3$ kernel mapped to nine
qubits via the Filippi encoding (Eq.~\ref{eq:parallel_circuit}, with
nine $R_Z$ trainable weights and nine input-scale parameters: $18$
quantum trainable parameters in total). The classical trunk and head are
sized exactly as in Level~2. Total trainable parameters: $463{,}574$.
\end{itemize}
A fourth level corresponds to the hybrid Q-CNN executed on real quantum
hardware (IQM Emerald, fine-tuning of the Level-3 simulator checkpoint
on the device); the methodology and the campaign budget are described
in Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}, and we recapture it here in
Fig.~\ref{fig:wave-K-four-level-overview} to integrate it
narratively into the same evidence ladder. Reported metrics are mean
values with one across-seed standard deviation ($\mathrm{mean} \pm
\sigma$), and pairwise comparisons between Levels~1--3 are performed
via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the $R=10$ final-epoch
validation-accuracy differences~\cite{Wilcoxon1945}. With $R=10$ the
smallest two-sided $p$-value achievable by the exact test is
$2/2^{10}\approx 0.002$, reached when all ten signs of the per-seed
difference agree.

\paragraph{Across-seed final accuracy.}
At the final training epoch, the three architectures reach respectively:
$\bar{a}_{\mathrm{big}}=0.9835\pm 0.0047$ (Level~1, bootstrap 95\% CI on
the mean $[0.9810,\,0.9865]$); $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{small}}=0.9700\pm
0.0133$ (Level~2, bootstrap CI $[0.9620,\,0.9780]$); and
$\bar{a}_{\mathrm{hybrid}}=0.9445\pm 0.0243$ (Level~3, bootstrap CI
$[0.9300,\,0.9590]$, per-seed range $[0.910,\,0.980]$). The
across-seed standard deviation of Level~3 is approximately twice that
of Level~2 and five times that of Level~1, consistent with parameter-shift
optimisation on a shallow variational block being more sensitive to
initialisation than full classical backpropagation. Bootstrap intervals
do not overlap between any two levels.

\paragraph{Capacity contribution to the gap.}
A Wilcoxon signed-rank exact paired test between Levels~1 and~2 — both
classical, one with $\sim$$185\times$ the parameter count of the other —
gives $W=3.5$, two-sided $p=2.3\times 10^{-2}$, paired Cohen's
$d=+0.97$; the bootstrap 95\% CI on the per-seed difference is
$[+0.005,\,+0.022]$. The Level-1 capacity advantage over Level~2 is
therefore real but quantitatively modest, $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{big}} -
\bar{a}_{\mathrm{small}} \approx +0.014$, on a dataset close to its
saturation regime. This sets the scale against which the hybrid
ablation has to be read.

\paragraph{Hybrid Q-CNN versus the matched-capacity classical control.}
The paired comparison most relevant to the architectural question is
Level~3 against Level~2, which differ only in whether the adaptation
block between the two convolutional stages is a $\mathrm{Conv2d}$ layer
(classical) or a quanvolutional layer (quantum), with all other layers,
the optimiser, the schedule, the seed schedule, and the data partition
identical. After rounding to four decimal places (the precision in which
run metrics are reported), the per-seed differences
$\Delta_{r}=a_{\mathrm{hybrid},r}-a_{\mathrm{small},r}$ are negative for
eight seeds, positive for two seeds, and zero for none; the Wilcoxon
exact two-sided test gives $W_{\mathrm{obs}}=6$,
$p_{\mathrm{obs}}=2.7\times 10^{-2}$, with paired Cohen's $d=-0.86$ and
mean $\bar{\Delta}=-0.0255$ (bootstrap 95\% CI on the per-seed difference
$[-0.043,\,-0.009]$). A complementary paired $t$-test gives
$t_{9}=-2.74$, $p=2.3\times 10^{-2}$, with the same direction. The
hybrid Q-CNN therefore underperforms its matched-capacity classical
control by approximately $2.6$ percentage points of final-epoch
accuracy, in a statistically significant way at the conventional
$5\%$ threshold.

\paragraph{Training efficiency at fixed capacity.}
The opposite reading emerges from the early training phase
(Fig.~\ref{fig:QCNN-with-std}). At epoch~$1$ the
hybrid Q-CNN reaches a mean validation accuracy of $0.87$ against the
$0.58$ of the classical matched-capacity control: the quanvolutional
encoding produces informative features from the very first parameter-shift
update, whereas a freshly initialised classical $3{\times}3$ convolution
at the same depth contributes essentially random predictions. The
two architectures' validation curves cross between epoch~$2$ and
epoch~$3$ and asymptotically separate by the $\sim$$2.6$~pp documented
above. The empirical pattern is therefore one of \emph{training
efficiency favouring the quantum block, asymptotic accuracy favouring
the classical control}, with both readings statistically supported on
the same $R=10$ data. We refrain from identifying the early-epoch
advantage as a category of computational speed-up, in line with the
discussion in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}~and the broader trainability
literature~\cite{Pesah2021PRX,Cong2019,Havlicek2019}.

\paragraph{Comparison against the high-capacity classical reference.}
For completeness, the Wilcoxon paired test between Level~3 and Level~1
(asymmetric in capacity by $\sim$$185\times$, see
Fig.~\ref{fig:qcnn-vs-ccnn-paired}) gives $W_{\mathrm{obs}}=0$
at $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=9$ (one tie at $s=486$), two-sided
$p_{\mathrm{obs}}\approx 2/2^{9}=3.9\times 10^{-3}$, with paired
Cohen's $d=-1.48$ and $\bar{\Delta}=-0.039$. This is the headline comparison when no matched-capacity control is
available; with the matched-capacity Level-2 control in place, approximately one third of this gap
($\sim$$1.4$~pp out of $\sim$$3.9$~pp) is attributable to classical
parameter count and the remainder ($\sim$$2.6$~pp) to the architectural
substitution itself. A compact summary of all four levels at the final
epoch is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wave-K-four-level-bars}.

\paragraph{Bridge to hardware (Level 4).}
The next subsection takes the Level-3 simulator-trained checkpoint of the
hybrid Q-CNN and fine-tunes it for two epochs directly on the IQM Emerald
54-qubit superconducting processor (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}). The
fine-tuning recovers a validation accuracy of $100\%$ on the on-device
validation subset of $N_{\mathrm{val}}=20$ images by the second epoch,
in line with the simulator pretraining target; the Wilson $95\%$
confidence interval on a single binomial proportion at $N_{\mathrm{val}}=20$
is, however, broad ($[0.84,\,1.00]$ at $p=1.0$), so the result is
qualitative and should be read as a feasibility statement on
device-side parameter-shift fine-tuning rather than as a statistical
comparison with the $N_{\mathrm{val}}=200$ data of Levels~1--3.
Fig.~\ref{fig:wave-K-four-level-overview} presents the four levels on a
single time axis to make the narrative explicit.\footnote{A small
transient is visible on the training-loss curve at epoch~2 of both
classical levels, where the per-seed loss briefly rises before returning
to its descending trajectory; the validation curve is essentially
unaffected. We attribute this to the interaction between the
\texttt{LazyLinear} parameter shape inference of the classification head
and the Adam optimiser: the head's parameter tensors are only
instantiated at the first forward pass, so Adam's running estimates of
the first and second moments of the gradient have not yet been populated
when the second optimiser step begins, and one to two steps are needed
for the moment buffers to settle on this layer. The effect is independent
of the quanvolutional block, which has no \texttt{LazyLinear} dependency,
and appears symmetrically in both classical controls (Levels~1 and~2),
which serves as a useful internal cross-check.} As discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines}, the purely quantum reference
of~\cite{Sebastianelli2021} is excluded from the paired test because
seed-resolved data are not available for it.

\paragraph{Item-level corroboration (vs.\ Level~1).}
For the three hybrid Q-CNN seeds in which the per-item correctness
vector is available (seeds $708,819,930$), and against
the Level-1 high-capacity classical control whose per-item correctness
is available for all ten seeds on the same image ordering as the hybrid,
McNemar's paired test on the $200$ validation items can be evaluated
seed by seed. Letting $b$ count the items on which the hybrid is wrong
and the classical Level-1 is correct, and $c$ the items on which the
hybrid is correct and Level-1 is wrong, we obtain $(b,c)=(6,1)$ for
seed~$708$, $(10,2)$ for seed~$819$, and $(14,0)$ for seed~$930$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:qcnn-vs-ccnn-mcnemar}).
Aggregating the three contingency tables (a legitimate pooling because
the validation partition is identical across seeds, only the model
weights and the run-to-run noise differ) gives $b_{\mathrm{tot}}=30$,
$c_{\mathrm{tot}}=3$ out of $600$ paired items, i.e.\ a per-item odds
ratio of $10:1$ in favour of the classical model and an exact binomial
$p$-value of $1.4\times 10^{-6}$ (equivalently, the asymptotic
$\chi^{2}$ McNemar statistic is $22.1$ on one degree of freedom). This
is the item-level counterpart of the per-seed Wilcoxon test against
Level~1: the two evidences agree on the same direction and qualitative
magnitude, with the item-level $p$-value much smaller only because it
sees $600$ paired draws rather than the $9$ usable per-seed contrasts
of the across-seed test. Extending this analysis to the Level-2
matched-capacity control would require aligning the per-item validation
ordering across the two campaigns; the across-seed paired Wilcoxon test
already supplies the matched-capacity comparison at the seed level.

\paragraph{A note on the gradient flow through the quanvolutional layer.}
In the simulator-based training of this section we propagate gradients
through the trainable parameters of the quanvolutional layer (the nine
$R_Z$ rotation angles) but not through its inputs: the upstream classical
convolutional trunk receives its training signal directly from the
classification loss via the rest of the network, but not through the
parameter-shift Jacobian of the quanvolutional block itself. This
``input-detached'' configuration halves the number of parameter-shift
\emph{gradient} evaluations per backward pass (from $2(W+n)=36$ to $2W=18$
for $W=9$ trainable weights and $n=9$ input variables): two shifted
circuit evaluations per differentiated parameter. Including the single
unshifted forward evaluation, which is shared with the loss computation and
counted once, the per-step totals are $1+2(W+n)$ and $1+2W$ respectively ---
the convention in which the hardware budget of
Sec.~\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd} is stated. This input-detached saving is the
dominant simulator-time reduction of the present campaign. The matched-capacity classical control is trained
end-to-end without any such restriction, so the comparison is conservative
in the direction of the hybrid: any residual advantage of the hybrid
reflects expressivity of the quantum block in isolation, with its
classical trunk adapted only via the standard classification gradient.

\subsection{Training dynamics}
\label{sec:qcnn_training_dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:QCNN-with-std} together with the pre-training curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:training-dynamics} characterises the training dynamics of the hybrid model on the simulator. We report three observations of these curves without inferring any asymptotic behaviour.

First, the hybrid Q-CNN reaches an across-seed mean final-epoch validation accuracy of $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{hybrid}}=0.945$, against $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{small}}=0.970$ for the matched-capacity classical control (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}); the two architectures therefore plateau within $\sim 2.6$ percentage points of each other within roughly five epochs on the binary EuroSAT subset, with run-to-run variability that is consistent with the per-seed dispersion of Sec.~\ref{sec:stats-significance}. Second, at the first epoch the hybrid model exhibits a substantially higher validation accuracy than the matched-capacity classical control (Fig.~\ref{fig:QCNN-with-std}, right panel): the across-seed mean gap of $\sim 29$ percentage points at epoch~$1$ shrinks over the subsequent epochs, reverses sign around epoch~$3$, and asymptotes to the $\sim 2.6$~pp deficit reported above once both models have reached their respective plateaus. Third, the per-step training loss of the hybrid model carries a moderately larger step-to-step variance than the classical control, in a manner consistent with the residual sampling noise of the parameter-shift gradient estimator at $N_s=500$ shots per observable.

These observations should be read narrowly. The binary subset of EuroSAT used here saturates close to the inherent class-overlap ceiling already after a handful of epochs, so the convergence scale itself is short, and any epoch-count differential measured on it has limited resolving power. The first-epoch margin is presented here as a property of the simulated training trajectory in the present setting; it does not by itself support a claim that the quantum filter accelerates training in a meaningful, transferable sense. A quantitative comparison with stronger classical baselines and on multi-class targets, together with a controlled study of the effect of seed and initialisation, would be required before any inference about the relative learning speeds of the two architectures is drawn.

\subsection{Reproducibility and controls}
To isolate architectural effects, we ablated max-pooling near the quantum layer and found that removing pooling improves downstream metrics (lower loss, higher accuracy), indicating that aggressive spatial downsampling before the quantum kernel can discard discriminative high-frequency content. All results are averaged over multiple seeds; we report mean validation metrics at the last epoch and monitor early stopping to check for instability.

\subsection{Hardware validation on real quantum backends}
\label{sec:hw_validation}

The hybrid Q-CNN adopted in Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines} --- the compact late-fusion design motivated there, rather than Filippi's high-capacity \texttt{C16--Q64} winner --- was carried to physical hardware to assess whether it transfers to the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) regime. We have executed it, in the reduced four-qubit form dictated by the device budget, on IQM's 54-qubit superconducting processor \emph{Emerald}~\cite{IQMResonance2025}, accessed through the IQM Resonance cloud platform via the \texttt{iqm-client} Python adapter for Qiskit~\cite{IQMClientDocs2025,QiskitOnIQMGuide}. The transition from simulator to physical device required addressing three classes of challenges that are seldom discussed in published Q-CNN benchmarks: (i) the interaction between our parallel-block circuit topology and the physical connectivity graph of the device, (ii) accurate calibration of the credit-based cost model that bounds the experiment, and (iii) selection of training hyperparameters compatible with a strict computational budget. We document each of these challenges in turn, since their resolution constitutes a methodological contribution that we believe is portable to other QPU-bound experiments.

\subsubsection{Backend selection and connection setup}
\label{subsec:backend_setup}

\paragraph{Target device.} 
Emerald is a 54-qubit Crystal-architecture device with native gate set $\{R(\theta,\phi),\; CZ\}$ supplemented by \texttt{measure}, \texttt{reset}, \texttt{delay}, and \texttt{if\_else} primitives. The device exposes 82 directed CZ couplings; characterisation metrics for the 2026-05-01 calibration cycle yield a mean two-qubit gate fidelity of 0.987 (range 0.904--0.999) and a mean readout fidelity of 0.978 (range 0.902--0.994 across the 54 qubits). A 4-qubit transmon block with linear connectivity, which is what our QCNN ansatz requires per quantum filter, fits comfortably within the local connectivity but is non-trivially distributed across the global device.

\paragraph{Software stack.}
The simulator-based architecture search and pre-training of Sec.~\ref{sec:train-opt} are run on Qiskit~2.2 (primitives V2). The hardware campaign on Emerald, by contrast, is constrained to a different Qiskit minor version by the IQM-side software pairing described below.
The IQM Resonance platform exposes its devices through the \texttt{iqm-client} Python adapter, whose major version determines the Qiskit version against which the adapter is binary-compatible. The version available at the time of this work, \texttt{iqm-client}~34.0.2 (with the underlying \texttt{iqm-station-control-client}~12.1.2), is paired with Qiskit~1.4.2. This pairing reflects the present synchronisation between the IQM stack and the upstream Qiskit release cadence, and an alignment to Qiskit~2.x is expected as the IQM adapter tracks future Qiskit releases. The methodological content of this section -- transpilation strategy, calibration-aware layout selection, cost-model calibration, and mini-batch stochastic-gradient-descent (SGD) adaptation -- is independent of the specific Qiskit minor version and applies equally to subsequent versions of the toolchain. All numerical results in this section are reproducible against the explicit version pinning reported in the supporting code repository.\footnote{Repository \texttt{qh\_algorithms} (\url{https://github.com/rcapp2506/qh_algorithms}), notebook \texttt{HQCNN.ipynb} for simulator benchmarks and the \texttt{hybrid\_qcnn\_v4\_2\_*\_emerald.ipynb} family for hardware execution. Calibration data are archived as JSON snapshots downloaded from the IQM Resonance dashboard.}

\paragraph{Authentication and connection.}
Connection to Emerald is established with the API endpoint convention introduced in \texttt{iqm-client}~$\geq$~33, in which a single root URL is paired with a \texttt{quantum\_computer} keyword that selects the device:
\begin{verbatim}
provider = IQMProvider("https://resonance.meetiqm.com",
                        quantum_computer="emerald")
backend = provider.get_backend()
\end{verbatim}
The earlier convention based on a per-device sub-path (\texttt{cocos.resonance.meetiqm.com/emerald}) is deprecated and emits a runtime warning. Authentication is supplied through the \texttt{IQM\_TOKEN} environment variable; if a token is also passed as a constructor argument while the variable is set, the client raises a \texttt{ClientConfigurationError} due to mixed sources. We accordingly restrict explicit-argument passing to environments where the variable is unset.

\paragraph{Functional sanity check.}
Before submitting any production circuit, we run a Bell-state preparation 
(Hadamard on qubit 0, CNOT to qubit 1, computational-basis measurement) 
at $N_s=1000$ shots. On the calibration set used throughout this section, 
the observed Bell fidelity $P(00)+P(11)=0.989$ confirms that the device 
is operational, that authentication and routing are correct, and that the 
shot statistics are consistent with the gate-level error budget. The same 
test is repeated against the device-side mock simulator (\texttt{emerald:mock}); 
while the mock reproduces the static circuit faithfully, we observed it is 
\emph{not} a faithful baseline for parametric circuits: a one-qubit 
$\hat R_x(\theta)$-rotation sweep returned $\langle Z\rangle\!\approx\!0$ 
independently of $\theta$, a behaviour we did not investigate further but 
that argues against using the mock as a noise-free reference for variational 
workloads.

\subsubsection{Transpilation to a partially disjoint topology}
\label{subsec:transpilation}

The Hybrid Q-CNN of Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines} processes patches of size $2\times 2$ stride 3 through 4-qubit quantum filters. To amortise per-circuit overhead, we batch $K$ filters into a single physical circuit by allocating $K$ disjoint blocks of $n=4$ qubits, sharing the trainable weight register $\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ across blocks while carrying distinct input registers $\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}$ per block. The circuit on $K\times n$ qubits has the structure
\begin{equation}
U_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w}}
\;=\;
\bigotimes_{k=0}^{K-1}\;
U^{(k)}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}\!,\boldsymbol{w}},
\qquad
U^{(k)} = \mathrm{CX}_{\downarrow}^{(k)} \cdot R_z(\boldsymbol{w}) \cdot \mathrm{CX}_{\uparrow}^{(k)} \cdot H^{\otimes n} \cdot R_y(\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}),
\label{eq:parallel_circuit}
\end{equation}
followed by single-qubit $Z$-basis measurements on the first qubit of each block. The classical post-processing of Eq.~\ref{eq:psr-34} requires only the marginal expectations $\langle Z_0^{(k)}\rangle$, so the measurement outcomes of the $K$ blocks are independent observables.

\paragraph{Logical-physical mismatch.}
On a fully connected simulator topology, $K$ may be set arbitrarily up to the qubit count. On Emerald the situation is more delicate: the device provides 54 physical qubits but its coupling graph does not naturally decompose into 13 disjoint 4-qubit linear chains, which would be the embedding required to host a 13-block parallel circuit \emph{without routing overhead}. As a result, the circuit-to-device embedding is performed by Qiskit's standard layout pipeline (\texttt{SabreLayout} followed by \texttt{SabreSwap}~\cite{Li2019Sabre}), which implicitly identifies connected components in the coupling graph and allocates blocks to them. Three software-level issues, which we report here for completeness as they may be encountered by other practitioners running multi-block circuits on similar architectures, surfaced in this regime.

\paragraph{Issue 1 -- multiple classical registers on partially disjoint topologies.}
Our initial implementation declared $K$ classical registers of one bit each (\texttt{c0}, \texttt{c1}, \dots, \texttt{c}$_{K-1}$) so that the $k$-th measurement is naturally addressed in post-processing. When transpiling against Emerald, the layout pass that runs over connected components of the coupling graph fails with
\begin{verbatim}
DAGCircuitError: 'not a ClassicalRegister instance.'
\end{verbatim}
inside \texttt{disjoint\_utils.run\_pass\_over\_connected\_components}. The error is silent on simulator backends that expose all-to-all connectivity. The minimal fix consists in collapsing the $K$ one-bit classical registers into a single $K$-bit classical register, so that the $k$-th measurement targets bit $k$ of the unified register. Per-block expectation values are then recovered from the joint counts via the standard big-endian convention $b_k = \mathrm{bitstring}[K-1-k]$.

\paragraph{Issue 2 -- barrier recombination across components.}
After the first fix, transpilation reaches the swap-routing stage and fails at
\begin{verbatim}
DAGCircuitError: 'Number of qubits in the replacement
operation (52) is not equal to the number of qubits
in the block (51)!'
\end{verbatim}
inside \texttt{disjoint\_utils.combine\_barriers}. The pass splits a global \texttt{barrier} acting on all qubits into per-component micro-barriers, then merges them by accumulating qubit counts; on circuits where Sabre redistributes qubits across components, the off-by-one accumulator yields the observed discrepancy. As the global barriers in our circuit serve only as visual separators between blocks that are physically disjoint by construction, removing them eliminates the failure path without altering the unitary realised by the circuit.

\paragraph{Issue 3 -- maximum number of parallel blocks.}
With Issues 1 and 2 resolved, the transpiler pipeline progresses further but still fails at $K=12$ and $K=13$ with the same symbolic signature as Issue 2 (off-by-one in qubit accumulation), now triggered \emph{within} components rather than across them. To localise the failure mode we built a \emph{compatibility test} (Tab.~\ref{tab:compat_scaling}) that submits the parallel circuit at $K\in\{2,5,10,11,12,13\}$ to a synthetic backend that mimics Emerald's gate set and a partially disjoint coupling map. The test passes deterministically up to $K=11$ and fails deterministically at $K\geq 12$, identifying $K_{\max}=11$ as the largest parallel-block count compatible with the current Qiskit/IQM toolchain on Emerald.

\paragraph{Calibration-aware initial layout.}
By default, \texttt{SabreLayout} chooses a logical-to-physical mapping based on the coupling graph alone, ignoring the calibration-resolved per-qubit readout fidelity. On a 54-qubit device with substantial qubit-to-qubit fidelity dispersion (Emerald's 2026-05-01 readout fidelities span the range $[0.902,\, 0.994]$, see Fig.~\ref{fig:emerald_topology}a), this choice can place a logical block on a low-quality region of the chip. We mitigate this in two stages. First, we parse the calibration JSON snapshot of the day, rank qubits by readout fidelity, and mark the six lowest-ranked qubits (indices $\{4,21,28,31,35,46\}$ in 1-based labeling) as unusable; in addition, we set aside four further qubits as a routing reserve, available to the transpiler should the snapshot fail to reflect a real-time degradation. The two sets combined account for the ten qubits removed from the assignable pool. Second, on the remaining 44 qubits we run a greedy-iterative search over the coupling subgraph to identify $K=11$ disjoint chains of 4 qubits, requiring per-chain minimum readout fidelity above 0.95. The resulting layout, depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:emerald_topology}b and listed in Tab.~\ref{tab:initial_layout}, has a global minimum readout fidelity of 0.954 (chain~10) and a worst-case CZ fidelity of 0.987 across all chains. We pass this mapping as \texttt{initial\_layout} to Qiskit's \texttt{transpile()} call so that Sabre's layout pass becomes a no-op while routing optimisation remains active.

\paragraph{Transpilation outcome.}

\subsubsection{Cost model calibration for run budgeting}
\label{subsec:cost_model}

Within a fixed credit budget $B$, we need an a priori estimate of the runtime per training step accurate enough to allocate epochs without overrun. The IQM Resonance platform bills jobs by net QPU runtime through a per-second rate that the platform exposes in each job record. Because the available cost signal at the Python client is the wall-clock time, while the billing is computed on the QPU side, the relationship between the two needs to be characterised empirically.

\paragraph{Three distinct cost figures.}
Before describing the calibration, it is useful to fix the terminology, because three different ``credit'' numbers coexist in this section and the gaps between them are exactly what the calibration is meant to characterise. By \emph{continuous-rate cost} we mean the idealised value obtained by multiplying the realised QPU runtime (read from the job record) by the nominal per-second rate, without any per-job rounding; this is the lower bound of what can be billed under the published price. By \emph{billed cost} we mean what IQM actually charges, namely the per-job continuous-rate cost rounded up to the next integer second (Eq.~\ref{eq:billing_rule}) and summed over jobs; this is what the dashboard reports and what is debited from the credit balance. By \emph{in-flight estimate} we mean the cost figure that the production notebook computes \emph{before} an epoch is launched, by multiplying the Python-side wall-clock time --- which includes compile, queue scheduling, post-processing and network return --- by an empirical wall-time-to-credit coefficient $r_{\mathrm{wall}\to\mathrm{cred}}$ calibrated on previous jobs (Eq.~\ref{eq:wall_rate}); this is the figure used to gate the per-epoch run policy. The three cost figures are related by two well-defined gaps: the \emph{ceiling overhead} between continuous-rate and billed, and the \emph{wall-vs-QPU gap} between in-flight and billed, and we report both gaps explicitly in what follows.

\paragraph{Anatomy of an IQM job.}
A submission to Emerald goes through a sequence of platform-side phases that are individually timestamped and that we reconstruct \emph{a posteriori} from the dashboard JSON of each job. Fig.~\ref{fig:iqm_job_anatomy} shows the phase decomposition for the two job types of our campaign: a 28-circuit forward / weight-shift job (median of 180 occurrences) and a 273-circuit validation job (median of 3 occurrences). The salient features of the decomposition are the following.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Validation, compile, and post-processing} (blue/light-green segments) run on the IQM platform side and are not billed. Their median wall durations are below 1.5~s for the 28-circuit job, and grow modestly with the circuit count (about 6.6~s for the 273-circuit job, dominated by per-circuit pulse-schedule generation).
\item \emph{Queue (QPU scheduling)} is a near-constant overhead between the end of compilation and the start of execution: $4.45\pm 0.59$~s across the 195 jobs of the campaign, essentially independent of circuit count. This is the QPU-scheduler latency before the device is allocated to the job.
\item \emph{Execution on QPU} (orange) is the dominant component for any non-trivial circuit count. Its median is 6.16~s for the 28-circuit job and 56.73~s for the 273-circuit job.
\item \emph{Billing window} (yellow hatched) starts at \texttt{execution\_start} and lasts \texttt{runtime\_seconds} (a field in the job record), which exceeds the wall-clock execution duration by a small additive overhead ($\sim 1.2$~s) attributable to QPU initialisation and teardown that the platform attributes to the user but does not expose as a separate timestamp.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Billing rule.}
The IQM billing rule for the PAYG (pay-as-you-go) execution mode reduces, on the 195 jobs of our campaign, to the exact integer formula
\begin{equation}
\text{credit\_cost} \;=\; \lceil\,\texttt{runtime\_seconds}\,\rceil \;\times\; 0.75 \quad\text{(IQM credits)},
\label{eq:billing_rule}
\end{equation}
that we verified to hold to 6 significant digits on 195 of 195 jobs. The $0.75$ figure matches the per-second rate published in the job record (\texttt{payg\_price\_per\_second}). The ceiling rounding induces an average overhead of approximately $8\%$ with respect to the continuous-rate cost: on the realised total QPU runtime of $1581$~s of the Phase~E campaign, the continuous-rate cost would be $1581 \times 0.75 = 1185.75$ credits, against an actually billed $1284$ credits, for a gap of $\approx 98$ credits (Sec.~\ref{subsec:finetune_results}). The relative gap is largest on short jobs (where the ceiling is a substantial fraction of the runtime) and decays to a fraction of a percent for long jobs (Tab.~\ref{tab:billing_rule_check}).

\paragraph{Per-circuit time amortisation.}
The QPU runtime per job (\texttt{runtime\_seconds}) is highly linear in the number of circuits, with negligible scatter at fixed $N$. A per-job linear fit on the full set of $195$ Phase~E jobs (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost_model_validation}) gives
\begin{equation}
\texttt{runtime\_seconds}(N) \;=\; (1.49 \pm 0.07)\;\text{s} \;+\; (0.225 \pm 0.001)\;\text{s/circ} \cdot N,
\qquad R^2 = 0.992 .
\label{eq:job_time_model}
\end{equation}
The intercept $1.49$~s is interpretable as the per-job QPU initialisation+teardown overhead, while the slope $0.225$~s/circuit is the per-circuit shot loop at $N_s=500$ shots. Combining Eq.~\ref{eq:job_time_model} with the billing rule of Eq.~\ref{eq:billing_rule} provides a closed-form a-priori cost model that is exact up to the ceiling rounding.

\paragraph{Wall-time vs.\ billed-time discrepancy.}
The wall-clock time observed at the Python client is consistently larger than \texttt{runtime\_seconds} (Fig.~\ref{fig:iqm_job_anatomy}). For the 28-circuit job, the wall median is 12.10~s while the billing window is 7.41~s; for the 273-circuit job, the corresponding values are 70.04~s and 63.38~s. The gap is attributable to compilation, queue scheduling, and post-processing, which are not billed but contribute to the throughput observed by the user. The empirical wall-time-to-billing ratio is
\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{wall}\to\mathrm{cred}}
\;=\; \frac{\sum_{j} \text{credit\_cost}_j}{\sum_{j} t_{\mathrm{wall},j}^{\text{client}}}
\;=\; 0.510\;\text{credits/s},
\label{eq:wall_rate}
\end{equation}
which is what an in-flight cost model based on Python-side wall measurements would observe. We adopted this value as the predictive coefficient during Phase~E to gate the per-epoch credit consumption \emph{a priori}; \emph{a posteriori} comparison with the dashboard data (Sec.~\ref{subsec:finetune_results}) shows that this estimate is conservative by $\sim 24\%$ with respect to the actual billing.

Aggregating over the two fine-tuning epochs of Phase~E, the in-flight

$\widehat{\mathrm{cred}}^{\rm ep1}=796$ and $\widehat{\mathrm{cred}}^{\rm ep2}=802$

\subsubsection{Mini-batch SGD adaptation for credit-bounded fine-tuning}
\label{subsec:minibatch_sgd}

The simulator-trained candidate of Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines} converges in 50--80 optimiser steps to its plateau accuracy. Reproducing this regime on hardware within the available 2000-credit budget requires three departures from the simulator setup, motivated by the cost model of Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}.

\paragraph{Pre-train on simulator, fine-tune on device.}
We split the training in two phases. In Phase~A, the full Hybrid Q-CNN (classical CNN backbone + 4-qubit quantum filter + linear classifier) is pre-trained on the Aer statevector simulator for 10 epochs. The resulting classical and quantum weights are checkpointed. In Phase~B, the simulator weights are loaded as the initial point on Emerald, the classical backbone is frozen (\texttt{requires\_grad = False}), and only the four trainable quantum weights $\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^4$ continue to receive gradient updates from the parameter-shift rule applied on the QPU. Freezing the backbone halves the per-step gradient cost: with both backbone and weights trainable the parameter-shift rule of Eq.~\ref{eq:psr-34} requires $1+2\,n_w+2\,n_q=17$ forward evaluations per step (forward, weight shifts, and input shifts), whereas with frozen backbone only $1+2\,n_w=9$ evaluations are required.

\paragraph{Mini-batch size and dataset.}
On Emerald we use a binary subset of EuroSAT (Forest vs.\ AnnualCrop) with 10 images per class for both training and validation, for a total of 20 train and 20 val images. The dataset is small but appropriate: simulator results in Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines} show that the classification target saturates at $100\%$ accuracy already at this regime, which makes the binary task well-suited to expose the effects of hardware noise without confounding factors due to insufficient training signal. We retain the simulator mini-batch $m=2$, yielding 10 optimiser steps per training epoch. Each optimiser step processes $m\cdot N_{\mathrm{patch}}\cdot C_{\mathrm{conv}} = 2\cdot 25\cdot 6 = 300$ patches, packed into $\lceil 300/K\rceil = 28$ parallel-circuit invocations per forward pass; with $1+2\,n_w=9$ passes per step, each step issues 9 jobs of 28 circuits each.

\paragraph{Budget projection.}
With $K=11$, $m=2$, frozen backbone, an epoch consists of $10$ optimiser steps and a final validation pass, totaling $C_{\mathrm{ep}}\!=\!2793$ circuits ($10\cdot 9\cdot 28 = 2520$ training circuits plus $\lceil 20\cdot 150/K\rceil = 273$ validation circuits). The in-flight cost projection multiplies the wall-time-to-credit coefficient $r_{\mathrm{wall}\to\mathrm{cred}}=0.510$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:wall_rate} by an estimate of the per-circuit wall time $t_{\mathrm{circ}}^{\text{wall}}$, ranging from $\sim 940$ credits (pessimistic, $t_{\mathrm{circ}}^{\text{wall}}=0.672$~s) to $\sim 420$ credits (optimistic, $t_{\mathrm{circ}}^{\text{wall}}=0.30$~s). As discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}, this in-flight projection is conservative with respect to the actual IQM billing because wall time includes non-billed phases; the actual realised cost (Sec.~\ref{subsec:finetune_results}) is in the lower part of the band.

\paragraph{Run policy.}
To insulate against an under-budget mis-estimation, the production notebook adopts a manual ``traffic-light'' workflow: the first epoch executes automatically and reports its actual credit consumption; each subsequent epoch is gated behind a boolean flag (\texttt{RUN\_EPOCH\_N\,=\,True}) that the user toggles only after verifying the dashboard credit balance. We did not implement an automatic budget guard, preferring an explicit human-in-the-loop decision to preserve the option of stopping the campaign mid-way.

\subsubsection{Hardware characterisation results}
\label{subsec:hw_results}

Before launching the fine-tuning campaign, we executed a minicheck phase (\emph{Phase~D}) on Emerald with the calibration-aware layout of Tab.~\ref{tab:initial_layout} to (i) verify that the per-block measurement statistics are consistent with the device calibration, and (ii) measure the empirical per-circuit time required by the cost model of Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}. The phase submitted small-$N$ exploratory probes at $N\in\{1,5,20,50\}$ to estimate the per-circuit execution time entering the cost model of Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}; the timing-estimation batch run immediately before fine-tuning ($N=1,20,50$) cost 18 credits.

\paragraph{Device functional sanity at session boundaries.}
The Bell-state fidelity test (Sec.~\ref{subsec:backend_setup}) was repeated at the start of each session that used the device. Across three independent sessions over a single day, the observed Bell fidelity $P(00)+P(11)$ took values $0.989$, $0.980$ and $0.990$, consistent within shot noise with the per-qubit readout fidelity floor of the device. We take this stability as evidence that the calibration set used by Emerald did not change appreciably across the campaign, and that the fine-tuning epochs of Sec.~\ref{subsec:finetune_results} are mutually comparable.

\paragraph{Per-block bias under zero input.}
With $\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}=\mathbf{0}$ and $\boldsymbol{w}=\mathbf{0}$ for all blocks $k$, the output of each block in Eq.~\ref{eq:parallel_circuit} reduces to a single $H$ followed by a chain of CX gates and a $Z$-basis measurement on qubit $q^{(k)}_0$, which produces an unbiased estimator $\langle Z_0^{(k)}\rangle = 0$ in the absence of noise. We submitted 50 such circuits and recorded the per-block sample mean $\widehat{\langle Z_0^{(k)}\rangle}$ and its standard error. The standard error of the per-block mean over $N_z$ independent circuits, each estimated from $N_s$ shots, is $\sigma_{\mathrm{mean}}=1/\sqrt{N_s\,N_z}$. The corresponding 3-$\sigma$ shot-noise threshold is $3/\sqrt{N_s\,N_z}=0.019$ with $N_s=500$ shots and $N_z=50$ circuits.

The data of Tab.~\ref{tab:per_block_bias} support three observations. First, the largest residual bias (Block~10, $+0.558$) is concentrated on the chain with the lowest readout-fidelity floor, in a way that is consistent with readout asymmetry — the per-qubit error rate $0\!\to\!1$ differs from $1\!\to\!0$, with the latter typically larger; for example, on the 2026-05-01 calibration snapshot, $\mathrm{err}_{1\to0}$ exceeds $\mathrm{err}_{0\to1}$ by up to one order of magnitude on the worst qubits. Second, the Pearson correlation $\rho(\,|b_k|,\,\min_q F^{\mathrm{ro}}_q\,)=-0.682$ between bias magnitude and chain fidelity floor is statistically robust, but $\rho^2\!\approx\!0.47$ leaves about half of the variance in $|b_k|$ unexplained by readout fidelity alone; plausible additional contributors include CZ-gate fidelity within the chain, calibration drift between the snapshot date and the run date, and the asymmetry of the readout error itself (which is not captured by the symmetric fidelity figure of merit). Third, the maximum off-diagonal correlation between blocks was $0.351$, with mean $-0.003$, so the blocks are statistically near-independent: this supports the design assumption that the parallel circuit of Eq.~\ref{eq:parallel_circuit} produces independent observables, which can be aggregated by the classical post-processing without correction for inter-block correlations.
For the production fine-tuning of Sec.~\ref{subsec:finetune_results} we proceed with the layout as fixed, with the understanding that Block~10 contributes proportionally more shot-noise-equivalent bias to the aggregated quanvolutional output. A potential mitigation would be to swap Block~10 onto a different chain on a future calibration day, but we did not pursue this within the campaign budget.

\paragraph{Per-circuit timing.}
Two diagnostic batches were submitted in this phase. A 20-circuit calibration batch ran in $14.2$~s ($t_{\mathrm{circ}}=0.711$~s/circuit), and a 50-circuit zero-input batch ran in $21.8$~s ($t_{\mathrm{circ}}=0.436$~s/circuit). The amortisation is consistent with Eq.~\ref{eq:job_time_model}; the production-run batch size of $N=28$ circuits per job (Sec.~\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd}) is intermediate, and the realised per-circuit time during the fine-tuning epochs was $t_{\mathrm{circ}}=0.561$~s, a value bracketed by the two diagnostic measurements.

\subsubsection{Fine-tuning results on Emerald}
\label{subsec:finetune_results}

We executed two fine-tuning epochs on Emerald, with the per-epoch decision to proceed taken manually after inspection of the dashboard credit balance. The simulator pre-trained checkpoint loaded at the start of the run achieved $\text{val acc}=100\%$ ($20/20$) with $\text{val loss}=0.148$ on the Aer reference simulator. We use this point as the \emph{epoch 0} benchmark in the convergence plots that follow.

Three threads in the data are worth tracing.

First, the load of the simulator-trained weights onto Emerald causes one validation sample out of $20$ to be misclassified at the end of the first hardware epoch, and the misclassification is recovered in the second epoch, returning to the perfect classification of the simulator. This trajectory is consistent with the interpretation that hardware noise displaces the decision boundary at load time, and that the parameter-shift gradient updates re-align the boundary in one further epoch; the compensation is non-trivial since only the four trainable quantum weights $\boldsymbol{w}$ change between epochs, while the frozen classical head receives the noisy quanvolutional output unchanged. Second, the validation loss decreases monotonically across the three points (Epoch~$0$, $1$, $2$), from $0.148$ to $0.123$ to $0.120$; the apparent oscillation of the validation accuracy in Epoch~$1$ ($100\%\to 95\%\to 100\%$) is therefore not symptomatic of an unstable optimisation, but of the discrete nature of accuracy as a metric — the modest improvement of the cross-entropy loss is enough to flip the sign of the logit margin on the recovered sample. Third, the norm $\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}\mathcal{L}\|$ falls by roughly an order of magnitude between the two epochs ($2.53\times 10^{-2}\!\to\!2.58\times 10^{-3}$), which we read as evidence that the optimiser is approaching a local minimum on the noisy quantum loss surface. A third epoch would have offered modest additional convergence at the cost of approximately $800$ further credits, which were not available within the campaign budget.

\paragraph{Step-level training dynamics.}
Within each epoch, the per-step training accuracy oscillates between $50\%$ and $100\%$ -- a direct consequence of the very small mini-batch size $m=2$, where a single misclassified sample produces a $50\%$ batch accuracy. The averaged training accuracy across the $10$ steps of an epoch was $85\%$ in Epoch~1 and $90\%$ in Epoch~2, while the per-step training loss (mean across 10 steps) decreased from $0.238$ to $0.228$. The aggregated metric of interest is the validation accuracy at the end of each epoch (Tab.~\ref{tab:emerald_convergence}), measured on a held-out set of 20 images that were not seen during training.

\paragraph{Cost realisation vs.\ projection.}
The two epochs were billed $642$ and $642$ credits respectively (cf.\ the in-flight estimates of $796$ and $802$ credits derived from the wall-time-based coefficient $r_{\mathrm{wall}\to\mathrm{cred}}=0.510$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:wall_rate}). The discrepancy arises because the in-flight estimator multiplies the Python-side wall time -- which includes compile, queue scheduling, post-processing and return network -- by the empirical wall-time-to-credit ratio, while the actual billing only charges the QPU runtime window of Fig.~\ref{fig:iqm_job_anatomy}. The wall-time-based projection therefore overestimates the realised cost by approximately $24\%$ on this campaign. Total Phase~E billed consumption was $1284$ credits out of the $2000$-credit campaign budget (\emph{a priori} estimate: $1598$ credits), leaving an actual residual of $\sim 700$ credits which we elected not to spend on a third fine-tuning epoch given the diminishing-returns indicated by the gradient-norm decay and the fact that validation accuracy had already saturated at $100\%$ after Epoch~2.

\paragraph{Comparison to a noise-free reference.}
The closest noise-free benchmark in the same regime is the Phase~C \emph{simulator dry-run fine-tune} (4 epochs of frozen-backbone fine-tuning on the Aer simulator, starting from the same checkpoint), which produced validation accuracies of $\{100\%, 100\%, 90\%, 90\%\}$ across the four epochs. The fact that the Emerald fine-tuning recovers the simulator's full accuracy by Epoch~2 -- on a substantially noisier loss surface -- supports the conclusion that the parameter-shift adaptation is effective even under realistic hardware conditions, at least at the modest scale of trainable parameters of our ansatz ($n_w=4$). We do not claim that this conclusion extrapolates to deeper variational ansätze or larger trainable-parameter counts, where barren-plateau effects~\cite{McClean2018,Holmes2022PRXQ} and noise-induced gradient suppression~\cite{Wang2021NoiseInducedBP} are expected to play a larger role.

\subsubsection{Summary and implications for QPU-bound training}
\label{subsec:hw_summary}

The work documented in this section establishes a reproducible methodology for executing parameterised-quantum-circuit training on a 54-qubit superconducting device under a fixed credit budget, and reports the result of a 2-epoch fine-tuning campaign on the IQM Emerald device. Five threads of the campaign are worth abstracting as transferable insights, which we present below as named paragraphs.

\paragraph{Topology-aware circuit design is not optional.}
A natural choice such as ``one classical register per block'' or ``a global \texttt{barrier} between blocks'' interacts unfavorably with disjoint-component handling in current Qiskit transpiler passes. The fixes are local (Sec.~\ref{subsec:transpilation}) but require diagnostic effort that should be budgeted in a hardware campaign.

\paragraph{Maximum parallel-block count is a property of the toolchain, not of the device.}
The limit $K_{\max}=11$ in Tab.~\ref{tab:compat_scaling} is software-imposed; the underlying device has 54 qubits and could host more blocks. As Qiskit's disjoint-component handling matures, this limit is expected to rise.

\paragraph{Cost models must be calibrated, not nominal.}
The IQM billing rule of Eq.~\ref{eq:billing_rule} ($\lceil\,\texttt{runtime\_seconds}\,\rceil \times 0.75$) is exact and verifiable on every job, but the wall-time-based predictor $r_{\mathrm{wall}\to\mathrm{cred}}=0.510$ that the in-flight cost model uses (Sec.~\ref{subsec:cost_model}) is conservative by $\sim 24\%$ on this campaign because Python-side wall time captures non-billed phases (compilation, queue scheduling, post-processing). A short rate-calibration phase (10--15 credits) is sufficient to determine the wall-to-credit coefficient for the specific submission pattern of an experiment, but practitioners should be aware that the resulting in-flight projection is an upper bound, not the median outcome. \emph{Ex post}, our 1284-credit Phase~E was projected as $\sim 1598$ credits in flight, leaving headroom that we could have invested in a third epoch had we trusted the QPU-side billing directly.

\paragraph{Calibration-aware layout helps but does not eliminate per-block bias.}
The greedy chain selection of Sec.~\ref{subsec:transpilation} keeps the worst-chain readout fidelity above $0.95$ and produces a correlation $\rho=-0.682$ between $|b_k|$ and $\min_q F^{\mathrm{ro}}_q$ (Sec.~\ref{subsec:hw_results}), but residual bias remains uncorrelated with readout fidelity alone. A more aggressive layout strategy that also accounts for readout-error asymmetry, intra-chain CZ-fidelity dispersion and calibration recency would likely reduce the residual; we leave this as an open direction.

\paragraph{Parameter-shift fine-tuning recovers simulator accuracy under hardware noise.}
Despite the per-block biases of Tab.~\ref{tab:per_block_bias}, the binary-classification accuracy of the Hybrid Q-CNN returns to $100\%$ on the validation set within two on-device fine-tuning epochs, and the gradient norm decays by an order of magnitude over the campaign (Sec.~\ref{subsec:finetune_results}). The result is restricted to the modest scale of trainable parameters of our ansatz ($n_w=4$) and a binary classification target, but is consistent with the parameter-shift rule providing a usable training signal on noisy quantum hardware in the small-parameter regime.

\medskip
The infrastructure described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:backend_setup}--\ref{subsec:minibatch_sgd}, together with the empirical results of Sec.~\ref{subsec:hw_results}--\ref{subsec:finetune_results}, realises an end-to-end parameter-shift fine-tuning of a Hybrid Q-CNN on a $\geq 50$-qubit superconducting QPU under a credit-bounded budget; we are not aware of a prior demonstration of this kind.



\subsection{Noise resilience under a calibrated IBM Heron r2 noise model}
\label{sec:noise_resilience}

The credit-bounded hardware campaign of Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation} establishes that the Hybrid Q-CNN can be fine-tuned on a real device, but the two-epoch on-device budget limits the statistical claims that can be made about the gate-level noise contribution to training dynamics. To address this restriction without exceeding the cloud-credit budget, we ran a paired multi-seed simulator experiment on a 4-qubit reduction of the parallel-block ansatz, identical to its hardware counterpart in every aspect except the simulator backend, with the goal of isolating the effect of calibrated gate-level noise on training dynamics and on final classification accuracy.

\subsubsection{Experimental design}
\label{subsec:noise_design}

Both branches share the same architecture (one ParallelQuanv block of $K=4$ parallel sub-circuits, each on $n=4$ qubits, kernel size $2{\times}2$, stride $3$, $500$ shots per measurement), the same classical backbone (two convolutional layers, $6$ channels, kernel $5$, batch normalisation, max pooling), the same dataset (EuroSAT, two classes \texttt{AnnualCrop} and \texttt{Forest}, $100$ images per class for both training and validation), and the same hyperparameters (Adam, $\mathrm{lr}=10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch $16$, $10$ epochs). Pairing is exact: the same $R=10$ random seeds (range $[42,51]$) determine initial weights, data ordering, and Aer sampler RNG in both branches. The two branches differ only in the simulation method:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Noiseless}: \texttt{AerSimulator(method=statevector)}, no error model, shot noise from $500$ shots only.
  \item \textbf{Noisy}: \texttt{AerSimulator(method=density\_matrix)} with a noise model derived from the IBM Heron r2 device \texttt{ibm\_fez} (snapshot date 2026-05-16), restricted to the four physical qubits ($0$--$3$) used by each sub-circuit. The model includes single- and two-qubit gate errors, readout errors, and $T_1/T_2$ relaxation. These four qubits are good-quality: on them the snapshot gives average gate errors $\bar{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{sx}}\approx 0.08\%$ and $\bar{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{cz}}\approx 0.47\%$ (the three couplings $0\!-\!1$, $1\!-\!2$, $2\!-\!3$ carry $0.93\%$, $0.27\%$ and $0.22\%$ respectively), well below the device-wide averages of $0.90\%$ and $2.80\%$, which are inflated by a small number of poorly calibrated qubits and couplings (several with gate errors above $50\%$) that the sub-circuit does not touch. After transpilation in the device basis the 4-qubit sub-circuit contains $17$ \texttt{sx} and $6$ \texttt{cz} gates, so the predicted cumulative circuit fidelity under the multiplicative-error model is $F_{4q}=(1-\bar{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{sx}})^{17}(1-\bar{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{cz}})^{6}\approx 0.96$, a per-pass infidelity of about $4\%$.
\end{itemize}
The noiseless branch ran in $30.2 \pm 2.5$~min per seed on a local workstation ($8$ Aer worker processes); the noisy branch ran in $89.1 \pm 0.4$~min per seed on a single node of the Magellano cluster ($8$ worker processes), a $\sim 3{\times}$ overhead per seed consistent with the density-matrix arithmetic cost.

\subsubsection{Final classification accuracy}
\label{subsec:noise_final_acc}

The final validation accuracy is statistically indistinguishable between the two regimes:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{noiseless}} &= 96.40 \pm 1.47\ \% \quad \text{(mean $\pm$ std over $R=10$)} \nonumber \\
  \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{noisy}}     &= 96.20 \pm 1.36\ \% \nonumber
\end{align}
with paired difference $\Delta = +0.20 \pm 0.35$ percentage points and two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank exact test $p = 0.219$ (all six non-zero paired differences equal one quantisation step of $0.5$~pp, so the signed-rank statistic degenerates to the sign test). The result is consistent with the null hypothesis of no accuracy effect from the calibrated noise model in this regime. Figure~\ref{fig:noise_curves} compares the full learning trajectories.

\subsubsection{Transient perturbation during the early epochs}
\label{subsec:noise_transient}

The two branches do exhibit a measurable difference during the first three epochs of training. The validation loss is systematically higher in the noisy branch in epochs $1{-}3$, with paired difference $\Delta_{\mathrm{val\_loss}}(\text{ep.\,1--3}) = -1.14 \pm 0.64 \times 10^{-2}$ (sign convention $\Delta = \mathrm{NL}-\mathrm{N}$; two-sided paired Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-4}$ on the $30$ paired observations, Cohen's $d_z = -1.80$, very large effect). The training loss shows the same direction with comparable significance. From epoch $5$ onwards the two trajectories are no longer distinguishable: $\Delta_{\mathrm{val\_loss}}(\text{ep.\,5--10})$ yields a two-sided paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.23$. The epoch-by-epoch paired distribution is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_paired_diff}.

This pattern --- both training and validation loss elevated in the noisy branch in early epochs, then converging to the noiseless trajectory --- is what we would expect from an additive perturbation on the gradient that the optimiser absorbs once the quantum weights settle into a sufficiently good region of parameter space. It is not the signature of a stochastic regulariser, which would have lowered validation loss while raising training loss. After approximately four epochs the classical head appears to compensate for the residual noise contribution, and the rest of the training proceeds as in the ideal regime.

\label{subsec:noise_conv_metrics}

\subsubsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:noise_interpretation}

Taken together, these observations are consistent with the following picture. The calibrated noise model of \texttt{ibm\_fez} restricted to the four (good-quality) qubits used by the sub-circuit produces a $\sim 4\%$ cumulative infidelity per quantum convolutional pass. Even this modest perturbation leaves a measurable, transient imprint on the early-epoch loss trajectory, yet it does not propagate to the final classification accuracy, to the late-epoch convergence rate, or to the train--validation gap.

This noise resilience is consistent with the structure of the hybrid pipeline. The quantum layer outputs $C \times P$ scalar expectation values which feed a fully connected classical head with $\sim 10^{4}$ trainable parameters. The head has ample capacity to absorb small input perturbations during training, provided that the perturbation is uncorrelated across patches and channels --- which it is, because each sub-circuit acts on independent qubit groups and each shot draw is independent. The final classifier therefore inherits very little of the per-circuit infidelity, in agreement with what is observed.

The picture differs substantially in the algorithmic regime of quantum computing. Routines such as quantum phase estimation, Hamiltonian simulation, and the linear-systems algorithm rely on the coherent accumulation of phase information over circuits whose depth scales with the desired precision~\cite{NielsenChuang2011}; in those routines, gate errors translate directly into phase errors which do not average out and degrade the final result roughly linearly with the circuit depth. The contrast with the present results suggests that variational quantum machine learning, in regimes where a classical post-processing layer can absorb the residual noise, may be a more natural target for current NISQ-class hardware than coherent quantum algorithms whose correctness depends on phase precision. We do not, however, claim that this conclusion generalises beyond the small binary classification task considered here: deeper quantum circuits or harder classification tasks may yet reveal noise-induced degradation that the classical head can no longer absorb.

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:noise_limitations}

Three caveats are in order. First, the experiment uses a $2$-class subset of EuroSAT with $100$ images per class; the small task makes the classical head powerful enough to compensate for the quantum-layer perturbations. Whether the same robustness holds in higher-class regimes or with smaller classical heads remains to be checked. Second, the quantum architecture used here is shallow ($23$ noise-bearing gates per sub-circuit after transpilation) and the cumulative infidelity on the good-quality qubits used is small ($\sim 4\%$); deeper circuits, or placement on poorly-calibrated qubits, with infidelity approaching unity would not be expected to retain this resilience. Third, the noise model is derived from a calibration snapshot and does not include coherent crosstalk between qubits beyond the two-qubit gate fidelities reported by the device; correlated noise modes may modify the picture.



\subsection{Takeaways}
On the binary EuroSAT subset, late fusion with moderately wide quantum channels gives a stable design point across the configurations tested, with a visible margin over the purely classical ablation at the first epoch that does not, however, persist once both architectures have reached the convergence plateau. Beyond the simulator results, the candidate has been transferred to physical hardware: the credit-bounded fine-tuning campaign on IQM Emerald (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}) recovers the simulator's $100\%$ validation accuracy in two on-device epochs, despite per-block readout biases consistent with the device calibration. This indicates that parameter-shift fine-tuning of the trainable quantum weights remains usable at the modest scale of the present ansatz ($n_w=4$); the result is not, by itself, evidence of a behaviour that would extrapolate to deeper variational ansätze. The complementary paired multi-seed study under a calibrated IBM Heron r2 noise model (Sec.~\ref{sec:noise_resilience}) extends this picture beyond the on-device budget: the classification accuracy of the hybrid pipeline is statistically indistinguishable from the noiseless reference (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.219$ on $R=10$ seeds), with only a transient perturbation on the loss visible in the first three training epochs and fully absorbed by epoch~5. Future work will extend the evaluation to multi-class EuroSAT and other remote-sensing benchmarks, and explore noise-aware training (gradient-aware shot allocation, advanced error mitigation, deeper variational ansätze) where barren-plateau and noise-induced gradient suppression effects~\cite{McClean2018,Holmes2022PRXQ,Wang2021NoiseInducedBP} are expected to play a larger role.

\section{Interpretation and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
On the binary EuroSAT subset and within the model family explored here, inserting a shallow, locality-preserving quantum layer into a CNN pipeline produces (i) a visible margin at epoch~$1$ of the simulated training trajectory and (ii) validation losses that are comparable to, and not systematically larger than, those of the classical ablation at convergence. We read these observations as compatible with the view that patch-wise quantum embeddings provide an inductive bias that is complementary to classical convolutions~\cite{Havlicek2019,Schuld2019PRA,Cong2019}, without further inferring a category of speed-up. In particular, \emph{late fusion} with moderately wide quantum channels appears to yield the most favourable trade-off between accuracy and variance among the configurations tested, while merely increasing circuit depth does not — a behaviour that is consistent with the relation between expressibility and gradient magnitude reported in the trainability literature~\cite{Holmes2022PRXQ,cerezo2021}.

\paragraph{Statistical robustness.}
The dispersion bands reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats} come from $R=10$ independent runs that differ only in the random seed used at initialisation and at mini-batch shuffling, on a fixed train/validation split. The protocol is described in Sec.~\ref{sec:stats-significance}; we report mean and one across-run standard deviation per epoch and architecture, and refrain from upgrading this to a frequentist confidence statement or to a paired significance test on the validation items themselves, since the items are not exchangeable across runs.

\paragraph{What may drive the early-epoch margin.}
Rotation (angle) encoding followed by short-range entanglers creates non-separable features on small receptive fields; data re-uploading further expands the accessible Fourier spectrum without adding qubits~\cite{PerezSalinas2020,Schuld2020Expressivity}. The QCNN-style locality (logarithmic light-cone) is known to mitigate exponential gradient decay~\cite{Pesah2021PRX}, and we observe empirically that shallow depth ($D{=}1$–$3$) with local entanglement is preferable to globally entangling, highly expressive blocks that risk entering barren-plateau regimes~\cite{McClean2018,Marrero2021PRXQ,Holmes2022PRXQ}. These observations are consistent with the ``feature-map'' viewpoint~\cite{Havlicek2019,Schuld2019PRA}: the quantum layer acts as a task-aligned kernel on top of already denoised classical features (late fusion), and may explain why the simulated curves show a margin at epoch~$1$ on top of an otherwise comparable classical trajectory.



\paragraph{Limitations and threats to validity.}
First, the evaluation uses a binary subset of EuroSAT~\cite{Helber2019}; while useful for controlled comparisons, it narrows class diversity and may overstate gains that depend on specific spectral contrasts. Second, model selection was performed on simulators, with hardware validation restricted to a single architecture (our compact hybrid Q-CNN) on a single device (IQM Emerald) and a credit-bounded fine-tuning campaign of two on-device epochs (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw_validation}). The noise sensitivity is additionally characterised through a paired multi-seed study on a reduced 4-qubit version of the architecture under a calibrated IBM Heron r2 noise model (Sec.~\ref{sec:noise_resilience}), which extends the statistical claims beyond the on-device two-epoch budget; nevertheless, conclusions about noise robustness should be read as restricted to the studied regime (binary task, shallow circuit, small $\sim 4\%$ cumulative infidelity), and a broader cross-device study would be needed to claim portability. Third, the classical references we adopt for the multi-seed statistical comparison are (i) a high-capacity reference sized to Filippi's \texttt{C16--Q64} capacity, containing $\sim$$185\times$ more trainable parameters than the simulator-trained hybrid of Sec.~\ref{sec:results-stats}, and (ii) a matched-capacity ablation that is structurally isomorphic to that hybrid at $0.07\%$ parameter-count tolerance; the second of these is what we read as the genuine like-for-like comparison, while the first sets the absolute classical asymptote on this task. With both references in place the asymptotic accuracy gap of the hybrid is $\sim$$2.6$~pp (matched) and $\sim$$3.9$~pp (high-capacity), with the early-epoch advantage of the hybrid largest at epoch~$1$ ($\sim$$29$~pp on the matched comparison). The hybrid result has not been tuned independently for the classical task, and we make no claim about its persistence against architectures that have been independently tuned to the classical optimum. Finally, patch extraction introduces choices (stride, overlap) that affect $N_{\text{patch}}$ and information leakage across borders; we mitigated this with consistent preprocessing, but a broader ablation is warranted.

\paragraph{Scalability and time-to-train.}
The practical bottleneck for hybrid training is the number of circuit evaluations,
\(
N_{\text{exec}}\!\approx\!N_{\text{patch}}\,S\,[\,M+2K\,M_{\!\nabla}\,]
\),
and the scheduled per-shot duration \(T_{\text{sched}}\) (Duration-Weighted Depth) reported by the compiler. We found \(T_{\text{sched}}\) a reliable wall-time surrogate under BackendV2/Target timing, whereas plain (unweighted) depth can be misleading when 2Q gates dominate layer latency—echoing timing-aware mapping results~\cite{Lao2020TimingMapping,Dousti2013LEQA} and recent “gate-aware depth’’ discussions~\cite{GateAwareDepth2025}. Late fusion also helps time-to-train because it acts on spatially downsampled feature maps (smaller $N_{\text{patch}}$). That said, the linear dependence on $N_s$ and on the number of active parameters $K$ remains a key constraint for larger models.

\paragraph{Generalization and sample efficiency.}
Keeping parameter count modest (via shallow depth and re-uploading rather than wider/global entanglement) appears beneficial. This echoes bounds and empirical evidence that quantum models can generalise from relatively few examples when capacity is controlled~\cite{Caro2022NatCommun}. In our setting, pooling before the linear head and limiting entanglement range likely reduce variance while preserving class-discriminative features.

\paragraph{Hardware considerations.}
All timing and device properties were obtained through Qiskit's modern \emph{BackendV2}/\emph{Target} interface, with scheduling defined over calibrated instruction durations and OpenQASM~3 timing semantics; expectations and gradients were evaluated via Primitives (Estimator) uniformly across simulators and on the IQM Emerald hardware backend~\cite{QiskitBackendV2Docs,QiskitTargetDocs,OpenQASM3TQC2022,QiskitEstimatorDocs}. While we enabled basic readout mitigation when available, we did not perform noise-aware training (e.g., gradient-aware shot allocation, advanced error mitigation), which could further alter the accuracy--time trade-off. The empirical noise study of Sec.~\ref{sec:noise_resilience} suggests that in the regime considered the dominant constraint on training cost is not the gate-level noise itself but the trade-off between shot count, circuit depth and capacity of the classical post-processing head; aggressive error mitigation may therefore have a smaller payoff than careful management of that trade-off.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
(i) \emph{Encoding design}: beyond angle and phase maps, amplitude encoding on aggressively downsampled patches might yield a different accuracy–time Pareto, but state preparation overheads are nontrivial. (ii) \emph{Entanglement scheduling}: learning \emph{where} to entangle (pattern/range) per patch may outperform fixed linear/ring couplings while staying shallow. (iii) \emph{Measurement grouping}: systematic grouping to minimise $M$ and $M_{\!\nabla}$ could reduce variance and wall time without changing expressivity. (iv) \emph{Classical parity}: testing against stronger classical baselines and kernel methods would clarify when hybrid layers add value beyond classical feature engineering.

\paragraph{Future work.}
We plan to: (a) extend to multi-class EuroSAT and additional remote-sensing benchmarks, (b) incorporate shot-scheduling and commuting-group measurement to shrink $N_{\text{exec}}$, (c) explore learned entangling patterns and encoder re-uploading schedules, and (d) run larger-scale hardware studies with fixed transpilation seeds and session-based execution to tighten latency estimates. Together, these steps will stress-test whether the observed early-learning gains and lower losses persist under stronger baselines, larger label spaces, and realistic device noise.

\subsection*{Summary of findings}
Our results indicate that Late Fusion architectures achieve a more favourable runtime--accuracy
trade-off, and that duration-weighted metrics can effectively predict training latency.
The architecture is shown to be noise-resilient in the studied regime: under a calibrated IBM Heron r2 noise model the final classification accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from the noiseless reference (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.219$ on $R=10$ paired seeds), with only a transient perturbation visible in the first three training epochs and fully absorbed by epoch~5.
Limitations remain in encoding expressivity and in the scaling of gradient estimation.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}

This chapter has developed a hybrid quantum--classical convolutional neural network for binary satellite-image classification on a subset of EuroSAT, characterised it against matched and high-capacity classical controls at $R=10$ independent seeds, and validated the chosen architecture on the IQM Emerald 54-qubit superconducting processor within a fixed credit budget. A runtime-aware cost model was calibrated against $195$ production jobs on the same device, and a complementary multi-seed study under a calibrated IBM Heron r2 noise model addressed the noise sensitivity of the pipeline beyond the on-device two-epoch budget.

Three findings stand out. The hybrid architecture exhibits a $\sim 29$~pp training-efficiency advantage at the first epoch against the structurally isomorphic matched-capacity classical control, alongside a $\sim 2.6$~pp asymptotic-accuracy deficit at convergence on the same comparison ($p=2.7\times 10^{-2}$, paired Wilcoxon exact). Two on-device fine-tuning epochs on IQM Emerald recover the simulator's full validation accuracy under parameter-shift gradients, on a $1284$-credit campaign tracked by an explicitly calibrated cost model. Under the IBM Heron r2 noise model the final classification accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from the noiseless reference (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.219$), with only a transient perturbation on the loss visible in the first three training epochs and fully absorbed by epoch~5.

The scope of these findings is restricted: a binary task, a single architecture family, two devices, and a credit-bounded on-device budget. Whether the early-epoch training-efficiency advantage persists against independently tuned classical baselines, whether the on-device parameter-shift adaptation extrapolates to deeper variational ansätze where barren-plateau effects become relevant, and whether the noise resilience observed here survives multi-class settings or non-uniform error profiles are open questions that the present work does not settle. The directions identified in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} --- multi-class benchmarks, shot scheduling, learned entangling patterns, larger-scale hardware studies --- are the natural follow-ups; each of them would tighten one of the limitations stated above without, on its own, resolving them all.
