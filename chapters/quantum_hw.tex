This chapter analyses an asymmetric-SQUID transmon design first at conventional cryogenic temperatures and then in an elevated-temperature regime that has, to our knowledge, not yet been realised experimentally. Before turning to the survey of recent developments that motivates the design (Sec.~\ref{sec:state_of_the_art_survey}) and to the elevated-temperature project itself (Sec.~\ref{sec:nbn_project_overview}), this section gathers, in compact form, the four ingredients on which the rest of the chapter relies: the notion of a qubit as realised in a superconducting circuit, the Josephson junction, the SQUID and its asymmetric variant, and the framework of circuit quantum electrodynamics.

\subsection{Preliminaries: qubits, Josephson junctions, SQUIDs, and circuit QED}
\label{sec:hw_preliminaries}

\subsubsection*{Qubits in superconducting circuits}

A qubit is a quantum two-level system used as the elementary unit of quantum information; formally, it is the smallest non-trivial Hilbert space, $\mathbb{C}^2$, on which the standard quantum-mechanical machinery (states, observables, unitary evolution, measurement) acts. In a superconducting implementation, the physical system that hosts the two relevant levels is a non-linear $LC$ circuit cooled below the superconducting transition of its electrodes. The non-linearity is essential: a harmonic $LC$ oscillator has equally spaced levels and cannot be addressed selectively as a two-level system, whereas a non-linear element separates the $\ket{0}\!\to\!\ket{1}$ transition from the $\ket{1}\!\to\!\ket{2}$ transition, and the resulting frequency difference -- the \emph{anharmonicity} -- can be made large enough that microwave pulses tuned to $\omega_{01}$ leave the higher levels essentially unpopulated.

The non-linear element of choice in superconducting qubits is the Josephson junction, introduced below. The relative weight of two energy scales -- the Josephson energy $E_J$ associated with Cooper-pair tunnelling across the junction and the charging energy $E_C = e^2/(2C)$ associated with the junction capacitance -- defines the operating regime of the device and selects, among three archetypal designs, the one that the device behaves as. In the \emph{charge-qubit} regime ($E_J/E_C \lesssim 1$), the computational basis is closely related to states of definite Cooper-pair number on a small superconducting island; the device is then very sensitive to charge offsets caused by environmental fluctuations. In the \emph{flux- or phase-qubit} regime ($E_J/E_C \gg 1$ with a phase-biased junction in a loop), the basis corresponds instead to states of definite circulation or phase. The \emph{transmon}, introduced by Koch \emph{et al.}~\cite{Koch2007Transmon}, is the variant of the charge qubit obtained by raising $E_J/E_C$ above approximately $50$: in this regime the dependence of the qubit transition frequency on background charge is exponentially suppressed, while a small but non-zero anharmonicity $\alpha \approx -E_C$ is retained, sufficient for coherent gate operation~\cite{kjaergaard2020,krantz2019}. The transmon is the de-facto standard architecture in current large-scale superconducting processors, and is the starting point for the design analysed in this chapter.

\subsubsection*{Josephson junctions and SQUIDs}

A \emph{Josephson junction} is a sandwich of two superconducting electrodes separated by a thin barrier (an oxide layer in the standard Al/AlO$_x$/Al technology, an epitaxial AlN spacer in the NbN/AlN/NbN platform considered in Sec.~\ref{sec:nbn_project_overview}) thin enough to permit the coherent tunnelling of Cooper pairs between the two superconductors. The current-phase relation $I = I_c \sin\varphi$ -- with $\varphi$ the macroscopic phase difference across the barrier and $I_c$ the junction critical current -- and the corresponding energy $E_J = \Phi_0 I_c / (2\pi)$, with $\Phi_0 = h/(2e)$ the superconducting flux quantum, are the two ingredients that turn the junction into a non-linear inductor and that ultimately produce the anharmonicity required by the qubit~\cite{devoret2013}.

A \emph{Superconducting Quantum Interference Device} (SQUID) is a closed superconducting loop interrupted by two Josephson junctions in parallel. Because the macroscopic phase across each junction must be consistent with single-valued superconducting wavefunction around the loop, the two junctions form a Josephson interferometer. The effective Josephson energy of the combined element depends on the magnetic flux $\Phi$ threading the loop:
\begin{equation}
    E_J^{\rm eff}(\Phi)
    \;=\;
    (E_{J1}+E_{J2})\,\bigl|\cos(\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)\bigr|\,
    \sqrt{\,1+d^2\tan^2(\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)\,},
    \label{eq:effective_EJ_squid}
\end{equation}
where $d \equiv (E_{J1}-E_{J2})/(E_{J1}+E_{J2})$ is the junction asymmetry. Replacing the single junction of a transmon with a SQUID therefore makes the qubit transition frequency $\omega_{01}(\Phi)$ tunable in situ by means of an external magnetic field threading the loop. Two regimes of this device matter for the present work.

In a \emph{symmetric} SQUID ($d=0$), $E_J^{\rm eff}(\Phi)$ varies between a maximum at $\Phi=0$ and exactly zero at $\Phi=\Phi_0/2$: the qubit has a frequency-maximum sweet spot at zero flux, where the first-order sensitivity to flux noise vanishes ($\partial \omega_{01}/\partial\Phi = 0$), but the second sweet spot, at $\Phi=\Phi_0/2$, lies where the qubit frequency itself collapses to zero and is therefore not operational. In an \emph{asymmetric} SQUID ($d \neq 0$), the cancellation at $\Phi=\Phi_0/2$ is incomplete: $E_J^{\rm eff}(\Phi_0/2) = (E_{J1}+E_{J2}) |d|$ remains finite, and the qubit retains a non-zero frequency at this second flux-insensitive point. The asymmetric design therefore offers \emph{two} operational sweet spots per flux period, both first-order insensitive to flux noise, with the lower one accessible at a frequency that can be chosen by engineering the asymmetry $d$~\cite{Hutchings2017}. This second sweet spot is the design lever that the rest of the chapter relies on, especially in the elevated-temperature regime where pushing the upper sweet spot to several hundred GHz, while retaining a controllable lower sweet spot at flux $\Phi_0/2$, is the central architectural idea.

\subsubsection*{Circuit quantum electrodynamics (cQED)}

\emph{Circuit quantum electrodynamics} (cQED) is the framework in which a superconducting qubit is coupled to a microwave electromagnetic resonator fabricated on the same chip~\cite{blais2021circuit}. The construction is the on-chip analogue of \emph{cavity QED} (CQED), the older atomic-physics framework in which natural atoms are coupled to optical or microwave cavities; following the convention adopted in the recent literature, we use the lower-case `c' (cQED) for the circuit version and reserve the upper-case `C' (CQED) for the cavity version, since the two regimes -- one with engineered, lithographically defined ``atoms'' and a transmission-line resonator, the other with natural atoms and a high-finesse cavity -- share the same Jaynes--Cummings Hamiltonian but differ substantially in coupling strengths and operating frequencies.

The regime relevant to the present work is the \emph{dispersive} regime of cQED, in which the qubit-resonator detuning $\Delta = \omega_q - \omega_r$ is large compared with the bare coupling rate $g$, $|\Delta| \gg g$. In this limit the resonator frequency acquires a state-dependent shift $\pm\chi$ (with $\chi$ the dispersive coupling) according to the qubit being in $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$, and a homodyne measurement of the transmitted (or reflected) microwave amplitude returns a signal that depends on the qubit state without exchanging energy with the qubit itself: a quantum non-demolition (QND) readout~\cite{Schuster2005PRL,blais2021circuit}. The dispersive readout is the readout mechanism assumed throughout the chapter, and the cQED Hamiltonian is the starting point of the two-qubit analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings}.

\subsection{Recent developments and motivations}
\label{sec:state_of_the_art_survey}

Superconducting qubits have emerged as one of the most promising platforms for scalable quantum computing due to their lithographic compatibility, strong nonlinearity, and relatively high coherence times. The relaxation time $T_{1}$ denotes the characteristic time for a qubit to decay from the excited state $\ket{1}$ to the ground state $\ket{0}$ due to energy loss to its environment. 
The coherence time $T_{2}$ quantifies how long a qubit can preserve quantum phase 
information before decohering as a result of environmental fluctuations.
The transmon qubit, introduced by Koch \emph{et al.}~\cite{Koch2007Transmon}, mitigates charge noise sensitivity by increasing the ratio $E_J/E_C$\footnote{The ratio $\tfrac{E_J}{E_C}$, where 
$E_J = \frac{\Phi_0 I_c}{2\pi}, E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C}$,
is the figure of merit distinguishing different regimes of Josephson qubits. 
Here $E_J$ is the Josephson energy determined by the junction critical current $I_c$, 
and $E_C$ is the charging energy associated with the junction capacitance $C$.}, and has become the de facto standard in large-scale processors~\cite{kjaergaard2020}. Recent devices have reached coherence times exceeding 0.3~ms~\cite{place2021} and, very lately, even beyond~\cite{tuokkola_methods_2025}, though typically require operation below 20~mK in dilution refrigerators.

Alternative designs aim to enhance robustness or integrate new functionalities. Flux qubits~\cite{yan2016flux} offer fast tunability via magnetic flux, while capacitively shunted flux qubits (C-shunt) and fluxonium~\cite{manucharyan2009fluxonium} achieve improved noise resilience. However, these designs still rely on ultra-low-temperature operation to suppress thermal excitations ($k_BT \ll \hbar \omega_q$), imposing significant cryogenic overheads.

High-$T_c$ superconductors, such as NbN, have been explored for kinetic inductance detectors~\cite{zmuidzinas2012superconducting} and Josephson Junctions~\cite{tafuriHIGH-TC2014}, showing potential for operation at above 4~K. Recent developments in epitaxial NbN/AlN/NbN junctions, pioneered by Nakamura et al.~\cite{nakamura2011epitaxialNbN}, have demonstrated significant progress: Kim et al.~\cite{kim2021} achieved $T_1 = 16.3$~$\mu$s and $T_2$, echo = 21.5~$\mu$s in all-nitride qubits on silicon substrates, with Tc $\approx $ 16~K and superconducting gap 2$\Delta \approx $ 5.2~meV. While these coherence times of NbN-based Josephson-junction qubits remain below those of conventional Al/AlO$_x$/Al junctions ($\approx 300$~$\mu$s), the larger superconducting gap significantly suppresses quasiparticle generation. Such qubits could co-locate with cryogenic CMOS electronics~\cite{yang2019cryoBGR}, reducing control latency and wiring complexity.

Circuit quantum electrodynamics (cQED), the on-chip superconducting analogue of Cavity QED (CQED), provides a robust framework for qubit readout and interaction~\cite{blais2021circuit}. Dispersive coupling to superconducting resonators enables high-fidelity, quantum non-demolition measurements~\cite{Schuster2005PRL,blais2021circuit}. Multi-qubit gates, such as CNOTs, have been demonstrated with fidelities above 99\% using microwave-activated interactions~\cite{Barends2014}.

Experimental efforts toward elevated-temperature Josephson Junctions remain limited. Yamashita \emph{et al.} demonstrated NbN-based Josephson devices operating up to 10~K~\cite{yamashita17highTc}. Subsequent work has pushed the operating-temperature frontier further. 
Anferov et al.~\cite{Anferov2024HighTemp} demonstrated niobium-based transmon qubits operating at frequencies up to 24 GHz with coherence times of $\sim 1 \mu$s at temperatures exceeding 200~mK, showing that quasiparticle decoherence can be suppressed up to approximately 1~K when using high-gap superconductors. This work aims to validate the fundamental approach of combining elevated frequencies with high-Tc materials for higher-temperature operation, though our target of 4-10~K at 300~GHz represents a further $\sim$40× increase in operating temperature beyond the current state-of-the-art. More recent approaches propose hybrid systems, embedding High-$T_c$ junctions in low-loss resonator environments, or leveraging materials such as NbTiN and granular aluminium for reduced surface loss~\cite{grunhaupt2019granular}. The main obstacles remain dielectric losses, quasiparticle generation at higher temperatures, and maintaining anharmonicity for gate operations~\cite{Martinis2005TLS, Muller2019TLS, Catelani2011QP, Wang2014QP, Koch2007Transmon, Peterer2015Multilevel}.

In conclusion, while superconducting qubits at millikelvin temperatures have achieved state-of-the-art performance, their cryogenic requirements limit scalability and deployment. High-$T_c$ approaches, though nascent, could unlock simpler cryogenic architectures and integration with cryo-CMOS, potentially enabling more compact and cost-effective quantum processors that could benefit both quantum computation~\cite{Lyu2024} and quantum sensing~\cite{Alesini2023} for HEP and Theoretical physics. Moreover, by exploiting the strong anharmonicity of an asymmetric-SQUID transmon~\cite{Bianchetti2010,Xu2016}, 
the $\ket{0}$, $\ket{1}$, and $\ket{2}$ levels can be spectrally resolved, 
allowing the device to operate as a qutrit (qudit). 
Such multilevel control enables its use for simulating three-flavor neutrino 
oscillations, where each flavor state can be mapped onto a distinct qutrit level~\cite{Goss2022,Nguyen2023,Turro2025}.
Further progress in the future will obviously depend on material engineering, specific assembly setup, and the development of high-frequency readout schemes compatible with elevated operating temperatures.
These constraints motivate exploring alternative operating regimes, provided that coherence and controllability can be preserved.

\section{The NbN-based elevated temperature transmon qubit project}
\label{sec:nbn_project_overview}

The system under study is based on a capacitively coupled transmon-like qubit, implemented as an asymmetric dc SQUID to enable in-situ frequency tunability via magnetic flux bias. 
The asymmetry prevents the Josephson energy from vanishing exactly at half a flux quantum, thereby stabilising the qubit frequency against flux noise at the so-called \emph{sweet spot}~\cite{Koch2007Transmon, nakamura2011epitaxialNbN}.
Hutchings et al.~\cite{Hutchings2017} demonstrated that weakly tunable asymmetric qubits can achieve flux-independent dephasing times ($T_2$, echo $\approx$ 40-45~$\mu$s) over a $\sim$~340 MHz tunable range, validating this design approach.

For a SQUID with junction energies $E_{J1}$ and $E_{J2}$, the effective flux-dependent Josephson energy is
\begin{equation}
E_{J}(\Phi) = \sqrt{E_{\Sigma}^2 \cos^2\!\left(\pi \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right) +
E_{\Delta}^2 \sin^2\!\left(\pi \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right)} ,
\label{eq:EJ_flux}
\end{equation}
where $E_{\Sigma} = E_{J1}+E_{J2}$, $E_{\Delta}=E_{J1}-E_{J2}$, and $\Phi_0$ is the flux quantum.  
This reduces to the symmetric SQUID result for $E_{J1}=E_{J2}$ ($E_\Delta = 0$), while for finite asymmetry it ensures a non-zero minimum Josephson energy at $\Phi=\Phi_0/2$. In the Symmetric case $E_\Delta = 0$, the usual SQUID modulation is recovered.

The Hamiltonian of qubit $i$ is then of the transmon\footnote{A qubit is said to operate in the \emph{transmon} regime when the ratio of 
Josephson to charging energy is large, $E_J/E_C \gtrsim 50$. In this limit, the sensitivity of the qubit transition frequency to offset charge $n_g$ is exponentially suppressed, while a finite anharmonicity $\alpha \approx -E_C$ remains to isolate the computational subspace. This defines the transmonic character of the device.} type~\cite{Koch2007Transmon}:
\begin{equation}
H_{q,i} = 4E_{C,i}(\hat{n}_i-n_{g,i})^2 - E_{J,i}(\Phi_i)\cos\hat{\varphi}_i,
\label{eq:transmon_ham}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{n}_i$ is the Cooper-pair number operator, $n_{g,i}$ the offset charge, and $\hat{\varphi}_i$ the superconducting phase across the junction.

\subsection{Anharmonicity: Definition and Physical Origin}
Starting from the Josephson potential energy
\begin{equation}
U_J(\phi) = -E_J \cos\phi ,
\end{equation}
and assuming small phase fluctuations around the potential minimum
(\(\langle \phi^2 \rangle \ll 1\)), as justified in the transmon regime
\(E_J / E_C \gg 1\), the cosine potential can be expanded in a Taylor
series around \(\phi = 0\) up to fourth order:
\begin{equation}
- E_J \cos\phi \simeq
\frac{E_J}{2}\phi^2
- \frac{E_J}{24}\phi^4
+ \mathcal{O}(\phi^6).
\end{equation}
The quadratic term corresponds to an effective harmonic oscillator,
while the quartic correction provides the leading-order anharmonicity
responsible for the non-equidistant energy level spacing of the
transmon qubit.

The $\varphi^2$ term gives rise to a harmonic oscillator potential, while the $\varphi^4$ term introduces \emph{negative} anharmonicity. After a canonical transformation to ladder operators $(a, a^\dagger)$, the Hamiltonian becomes:
\begin{equation}
H \approx \hbar\omega_{01}\left(a^\dagger a + \frac{1}{2}\right) + \frac{\alpha}{2}(a^\dagger a)(a^\dagger a - 1)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
\omega_{01} &\approx \sqrt{8E_J E_C} - E_C \label{eq:omega01} \\
\alpha &\approx -E_C \label{eq:alpha}
\end{align}

The \textbf{negative sign} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha} arises from the fact that the Josephson cosine potential has a finite barrier height, unlike a pure harmonic potential which extends to infinity. Consequently, energy levels become progressively closer as one climbs the energy ladder:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{01} > \omega_{12} > \omega_{23} > \omega_{34} > \cdots
\end{equation}

This level bunching is characteristic of a Morse-like potential and is fundamental to transmon operation.

%\subsubsection{Physical Interpretation}

The negative anharmonicity reflects the ``softening'' of the potential at large phase excursions. As the system is excited to higher energy levels, the wavefunction extends into regions where the cosine potential deviates significantly from its harmonic (parabolic) approximation near the minimum. The reduced curvature at larger $\varphi$ leads to decreased level spacings, manifesting as $\alpha < 0$.

\subsection{Qubit characterisation}

Qubit characterisation will be carried out at the 
theoretical and numerical level. The relevant metrics are the energy relaxation 
time $T_1$ and the dephasing time $T_2$, related by
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \Gamma_\varphi ,
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_\varphi$ is the pure dephasing rate. 
For asymmetric SQUID-based qubits, modelling at the flux sweet spot predicts a 
suppression of flux-noise contributions to $\Gamma_\varphi$, thus enhancing 
coherence. Numerical simulations will be employed to 
estimate these coherence times and to identify optimal operating regimes. 
This approach allows us to benchmark expected performance and define feasibility 
criteria for subsequent experimental validation, following protocols such as 
Ramsey fringes, echo sequences, and Purcell-filtered spectroscopy as described in 
literature~\cite{Paik2011,Jeffrey2014}.

\subsection{Models and Simulations}

To capture the dynamics of our coupled qubit–resonator system we employ the 
Lindblad master equation formalism, which provides a rigorous framework for 
open quantum systems interacting with their environment~\cite{Breuer2002,blais2021circuit}. 
This approach is essential since superconducting qubits are never perfectly isolated: 
energy relaxation, pure dephasing, and cavity photon loss all contribute to 
non-unitary dynamics.

The time evolution of the system density matrix $\rho$ is governed by
\begin{equation}
\dot{\rho} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho] + \sum_{k}\mathcal{D}[c_k]\rho,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}[c_k]\rho = c_k\rho c_k^\dagger - \tfrac{1}{2}\{c_k^\dagger c_k,\rho\}$ 
is the Lindblad dissipator. The operators $c_k$ are the 
\emph{collapse operators} associated with physical decoherence channels:
\begin{align}
c_{i,\downarrow} &= \sqrt{\gamma_{1,i}(1+\bar{n}_i)}\, \sigma_{-}^{(i)} 
&& \text{(qubit relaxation)}, \\
c_{i,\uparrow}   &= \sqrt{\gamma_{1,i}\bar{n}_i}\, \sigma_{+}^{(i)} 
&& \text{(thermal excitation)}, \\
c_{i,\phi}       &= \sqrt{\gamma_{\phi,i}}\, \sigma_{z}^{(i)} 
&& \text{(pure dephasing)}, \\
c_{r}            &= \sqrt{\kappa}\, a 
&& \text{(resonator photon loss)}.
\end{align}
Here $\gamma_{1,i}=1/T_{1,i}$ and $\gamma_{\phi,i}$ are the qubit relaxation and pure 
dephasing rates, $\kappa$ is the resonator linewidth, and 
$\bar{n}_i = (e^{\hbar \omega_{01,i}/k_BT}-1)^{-1}$ is the thermal photon number at temperature $T$, for the $i-th$ qubit.

Numerical integration of the master equation is carried out with the 
\textsc{QuTiP} library~\cite{Johansson2013,qutip5}, enabling both time-domain gate simulations 
(with DRAG pulse shaping \footnote{The DRAG (Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate) technique is a pulse-shaping 
method designed to suppress leakage out of the computational subspace in weakly 
anharmonic qubits such as the transmon. By adding a corrective quadrature component 
proportional to the derivative of the main drive envelope, DRAG reduces unwanted 
population of higher levels (e.g.\ $\ket{2}$) and mitigates phase errors, thereby enhancing single-qubit gate fidelities~\cite{Motzoi2009, Gambetta2011}.}) and steady-state readout analysis. This allows us 
to extract coherence metrics, gate fidelities, and readout signal-to-noise ratios 
under realistic conditions.

Parameter ranges are chosen consistently with established experimental 
benchmarks for superconducting qubits in circuit QED: 
$\omega_{01}/2\pi \in [5,8]$~GHz, 
anharmonicity $\alpha/2\pi \in [-0.10,-0.30]$~GHz, 
qubit–resonator coupling $g/2\pi \in [50,150]$~MHz, 
resonator linewidth $\kappa/2\pi \in [0.2,2]$~MHz, 
and operation temperature $T \in [4,10]$~K. 
These values are compatible with transmon designs in the literature~\cite{krantz2019,Paik2011,Barends2014} and ensure operation in the dispersive 
regime with $\chi/\kappa \gg 1$. They also guarantee that the Purcell-limited 
relaxation time $T_1$ exceeds several microseconds, which is necessary 
for gate fidelities above the surface-code threshold.

\medskip
The Lindblad framework, combined with QuTiP~\cite{qutip5} simulations, will provide 
a quantitatively reliable method to predict the coherence properties, gate 
performance, and readout fidelity of our elevated-temperature NbN qubits.

\subsection{Resonator coupling}
Each transmon qubit is coupled with a planar superconducting resonator bus.
The superconducting resonator bus of frequency $\omega_r$ is modelled as a harmonic oscillator:
\begin{equation}
H_r = \hbar \omega_r a^\dagger a.
\end{equation}

Coupling between qubit $i$ and the resonator is of dipole type:
\begin{equation}
H_{qr,i} = \hbar g_i (a+a^\dagger)\hat{n}_i ,
\end{equation}
In a multi-qubit system possible direct capacitive couplings between neighbouring qubits give
\begin{equation}
H_{qq} = \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} J_{ij}\,\hat{n}_i \hat{n}_j .
\end{equation}

The total Hamiltonian for the multi-qubit system, in line with standard circuit-QED formulations~\cite{Blais2004,Devoret2007}, is
\begin{equation}
H = H_r + \sum_{i=1}^N H_{q,i} + \sum_{i=1}^N H_{qr,i} + H_{qq},
\end{equation}
where the terms respectively describe the resonator, the qubits, their coupling to the resonator, and possible direct qubit–qubit interactions.

\subsection{Dispersive regime}

\paragraph{Conventions.}
Throughout these sections, we report coupling rates and dispersive shifts in \emph{frequency units} (Hz),
i.e., \(g/2\pi\), \(\Delta/2\pi\), \(\alpha/2\pi\), \(\chi/2\pi\), \(\kappa/2\pi\).
This avoids \(2\pi\) ambiguities when comparing text, tables, and figure captions.

In the dispersive limit, defined by $|\Delta_i| = |\omega_{q,i}-\omega_r|\gg g_i$, the Hamiltonian can be diagonalised perturbatively~\cite{blais2021circuit}:
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{disp}} = \hbar \left(\omega_r + \sum_i \chi_i \sigma_z^{(i)}\right) a^\dagger a +
\frac{\hbar}{2}\sum_i \tilde{\omega}_{q,i}\sigma_z^{(i)} +
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\zeta_{ij}\,\sigma_z^{(i)}\sigma_z^{(j)},
\end{equation}
where $\chi_i \simeq g_i^2 \alpha_i /[\Delta_i(\Delta_i+\alpha_i)]$ are the dispersive shifts (with anharmonicity $\alpha_i$), and $\zeta_{ij}$ are effective cross-Kerr interactions.

At elevated temperature (4--10~K), the thermal occupation of the qubit transition
\begin{equation}
\bar{n}(T) = \frac{1}{\exp(\hbar \omega_{01}/k_BT)-1}
\end{equation}
must remain small. Our design must ensure $\hbar\omega_{01} \gg k_BT$, so that $\bar{n}(T)<0.05$ even at 4~K.

\subsection{Control electronics critical aspects and possible solutions}
\label{subsec:control-electronics}

To keep thermal noise acceptable, we are forced to operate our transmons at frequencies of around \SIrange{250}{300} {GHz}. A critical challenge in the present project is the generation of coherent control
pulses at the qubit idle frequency, expected to lie in the 250--300~GHz range
to ensure thermalisation at 4~K. Standard quantum control electronics are designed 
for the 4--20~GHz regime, and extending their performance into the sub-THz domain 
is non-trivial. We identify three possible strategies:

\textbf{Flux-dip control.} The qubit frequency $\omega_{01}(\Phi)$ can be dynamically shifted down with fast flux pulses during gate operations~\cite{Stefanski2024}.

\textbf{Commercial sub-THz signal sources.} Direct synthesis of the control tones at $\sim$300~GHz from commercial millimetre-wave equipment.

\textbf{Frequency mixing and multiplication chains.} Employ frequency mixing and harmonic multiplication starting from a low-phase-noise local oscillator in the conventional microwave range.
The choice between flux-dip control, direct sub-THz sources, and frequency mixing will depend on the balance between cost, complexity, and simulation results.
\\
A feasibility study will provide the optimal choice for the experimental phase of the project.

\section[Comprehensive analysis of asymmetric-squid-based qubit]{Comprehensive analysis of\\ asymmetric-squid-based qubit}
\textbf{Part A} has presented a comprehensive analysis of asymmetric SQUID-based superconducting qubits in two operational regimes. \textbf{Part B} demonstrates baseline performance at standard cryogenic temperatures (10--20~mK) with qubit frequencies of 5--6~GHz, achieving CNOT gate fidelities approaching 99\% (98.7\%). \textbf{Part C} explores an extension to elevated temperatures (4--10~K) by pushing qubit frequencies to 300~GHz. The two regimes serve complementary roles in the analysis. The \textbf{baseline configuration} is used to verify that, at conventional operating conditions, the asymmetric SQUID architecture and the modelling framework adopted here reproduce the performance level reported for state-of-the-art transmon platforms. The \textbf{elevated-temperature configuration} extends the same framework to a $300$~GHz qubit at $4$--$10$~K and quantifies the conditions under which transmon operation appears viable outside the dilution-refrigerator regime.

The baseline therefore plays the role of a controlled reference for the modelling framework, while the elevated-temperature configuration defines the design point whose feasibility the remainder of the chapter examines in detail.

\subsection{Cavity-mediated qubit--qubit couplings}
\label{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings}

The dispersive coupling of two transmons to a common cavity bus generates two
distinct effective qubit--qubit interactions, the transverse exchange $J$
and the longitudinal cross-Kerr $\zeta_{zz}$. This section derives them
separately.

\subsubsection{Transverse exchange coupling}
\label{subsec:transverse_J}

Consider two transmon qubits with frequencies $\omega_{q,1}$ and $\omega_{q,2}$,
each capacitively coupled to a common cavity at $\omega_r$ via the
Jaynes--Cummings interaction
\begin{equation}
\hat{H}_{\text{JC}} = \omega_r \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
+ \sum_{i=1,2}\frac{\omega_{q,i}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z^{(i)}
+ \sum_{i=1,2} g_i\!\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_-^{(i)}
+ \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_+^{(i)}\right) ,
\label{eq:JC_2qubit}
\end{equation}
in the dispersive regime $|\Delta_i| \equiv |\omega_{q,i} - \omega_r| \gg g_i$.
A Schrieffer--Wolff (SW) transformation with antihermitian generator
\begin{equation}
\hat{S} = \sum_{i=1,2}\frac{g_i}{\Delta_i}
\!\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_-^{(i)}
- \hat{a}\,\hat{\sigma}_+^{(i)}\right)
\end{equation}
cancels the linear coupling and, at second order in $g_i/\Delta_i$, generates the
familiar single-qubit Lamb shift
$\chi_i = g_i^2/\Delta_i$ together with a residual qubit--qubit term of
\emph{transverse} character~\cite{Majer2007,blais2021circuit}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{H}_{\text{exch}} = J\!\left(\hat{\sigma}_+^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_-^{(2)}
+ \hat{\sigma}_-^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_+^{(2)}\right),\quad
J = \frac{g_1\,g_2}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_1} + \frac{1}{\Delta_2}\right).
\label{eq:transverse_J}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:transverse_J} is the well-known transverse exchange (XY)
coupling first identified by Majer~\textit{et~al.}~\cite{Majer2007}; it is a
real-photon swap term, not a longitudinal $\hat{\sigma}_z\!\otimes\!\hat{\sigma}_z$
interaction. We have verified by exact diagonalisation (with Fock
truncation $N_{\text{cav}} = 6$) that the coefficient of the
$\hat{\sigma}_+^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_-^{(2)}$ matrix element in the projection of
the JC eigenstates onto the computational subspace agrees with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:transverse_J} to four significant figures.

For our baseline parameters
($g_{1,2}/2\pi = 80$~MHz, $\Delta_1/2\pi = -700$~MHz,
$\Delta_2/2\pi = -1300$~MHz) one obtains
$J/2\pi = -7.03$~MHz.

\subsubsection{Longitudinal cross-Kerr coupling}
\label{subsec:longitudinal_zeta}

The genuine longitudinal coupling
$\hat{H}_{ZZ} = \zeta_{zz}\,\hat{\sigma}_z^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_z^{(2)}$
arises only when the qubits are not strict two-level systems. It is a
fourth-order effect that requires the transmon anharmonicity~$\alpha$, since
the relevant perturbative path involves the virtual excitation of the second
transmon level $\ket{2}$. Following the derivation in Blais~\textit{et~al.}
(Ref.~\cite{blais2021circuit}, \S{IV.B}), one performs a second SW transformation
on the post-cavity Hamiltonian to eliminate the transverse $J$ in favour of an
effective $\zeta_{zz}$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\zeta_{zz} = 2J^{2}\!\left[
\frac{1}{\Delta_q - \alpha_2} - \frac{1}{\Delta_q + \alpha_1}
\right] ,
}
\label{eq:zeta_zz_correct}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_q \equiv \omega_{q,1} - \omega_{q,2}$ is the qubit--qubit detuning
and $\alpha_i < 0$ is the anharmonicity of qubit~$i$. Equation~\eqref{eq:zeta_zz_correct}
gives the ZZ \emph{level shift}
$\xi_{ZZ}\equiv E_{|11\rangle}+E_{|00\rangle}-E_{|01\rangle}-E_{|10\rangle}$,
which is the directly measurable ``ZZ rate'' quoted throughout this work; the
coefficient of the $\hat\sigma_z^{(1)}\hat\sigma_z^{(2)}$ Hamiltonian term is
$\zeta_{zz}=\xi_{ZZ}/4$. For
$\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha$ and $|\alpha|\ll|\Delta_q|$, this reduces to
$\xi_{ZZ}\approx 4J^2 \alpha/\Delta_q^2$, which is suppressed by an additional
factor $|\alpha/\Delta_q|$ compared to the naive estimate $J^2/\Delta_q$.

We have evaluated $\xi_{ZZ}$ by exact diagonalisation of a three-level transmon
model (3~levels per qubit, Fock~6 for the cavity), extracting
$\xi_{ZZ}=E_{|11\rangle}+E_{|00\rangle}-E_{|01\rangle}-E_{|10\rangle}$ directly
from the dressed spectrum. For the baseline parameters this yields
$\xi_{ZZ}/2\pi = \BaseXiZZ$~kHz, in agreement with the perturbative
Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_zz_correct} at the $\sim15\%$ level. At the 300~GHz operating
point the condition $|\alpha|\ll|\Delta_q|$ is strongly violated
($|\alpha|=\InnAlpha$~GHz $\gg \Delta_q=\InnDeltaQ$~MHz), so the perturbative
expression is no longer quantitatively reliable and we quote the
exact-diagonalisation value $\xi_{ZZ}/2\pi = \InnXiZZ$~kHz instead.

The numerical values of the two interactions in our system are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:J_vs_zetazz}. The transverse $J$ dominates by nearly two orders
of magnitude over the longitudinal $\xi_{ZZ}$, which has direct consequences
for the choice of two-qubit gate protocol (\S\ref{sec:gate_strategy}).

\subsection{Single-qubit gates}
\label{sub:tqb_singleq}

Single-qubit rotations are implemented with resonant microwave drives at the qubit frequency. A general rotation is
\begin{equation}
R_i(\theta, \phi) = \exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{2}\left[\cos(\phi)\hat{\sigma}_x^{(i)} + \sin(\phi)\hat{\sigma}_y^{(i)}\right]\right).
\end{equation}
Common gates are obtained as
\begin{align}
X_i &= R_i(\pi, 0) \quad \text{(bit flip)}, \\
Y_i &= R_i(\pi, \pi/2), \\
H_i &= R_i(\pi/2, \pi/2) \cdot R_i(\pi, 0) \quad \text{(Hadamard)}.
\end{align}
Typical gate times are $\tau_{\text{1Q}} \sim \SI{20}{ns}$ for high-fidelity operations with DRAG pulse shaping.

\subsection{Two-qubit gate: cross-resonance with echo correction}
\label{sec:gate_strategy}
\label{sub:tqb_twoq_cr}

Two natural two-qubit gate implementations are available for cavity-QED
transmons: a resonant $\sqrt{i\text{SWAP}}$ driven by dynamic flux tuning of
the qubit frequency, and a free-evolution CZ driven by the residual
longitudinal cross-Kerr $\sigma_z\!\otimes\!\sigma_z$ coupling. The resonant
$\sqrt{i\text{SWAP}}$ is incompatible with elevated-temperature operation
because it requires flux-control electronics on the cold stage. The
free-evolution CZ is in principle compatible with the present hardware
constraints, but the actual cross-Kerr obtained from the exact
diagonalisation (Eq.~\ref{eq:zeta_zz_correct} and
Table~\ref{tab:J_vs_zetazz}) is $\xi_{ZZ}/2\pi = \BaseXiZZmag$~kHz at the
operating point of this work, nearly two orders of magnitude smaller than the
transverse exchange $J$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transverse_J}; the corresponding gate
time $t_{\mathrm{CZ}} \simeq \pi/|\xi_{ZZ}|$ would be of order several
microseconds, comparable to the coherence time $T_2$ and therefore incompatible
with a high-fidelity gate (Fig.~\ref{fig:CZ_unfeasible}).

The two-qubit gate is therefore implemented as an \emph{echo
cross-resonance} protocol at the qubit--qubit detuning $\Delta_q = \BaseDeltaQ$~MHz,
which exploits the dominant transverse exchange $J$ rather
than the suppressed longitudinal cross-Kerr. The resulting gate has
$t_{\mathrm{CNOT}} \simeq \tCNOT$~ns and $F_{\mathrm{CNOT}} \simeq \FCNOT\%$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}); the full derivation
follows below.

\subsubsection{Two-Qubit Gates: CNOT Gate Implementation}

\subsubsection{CNOT Truth Table}
The CNOT gate with qubit 1 as control and qubit 2 as target produces:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Input} & \text{Output} \\
\hline
\ket{00} & \ket{00} \\
\ket{01} & \ket{01} \\
\ket{10} & \ket{11} \\
\ket{11} & \ket{10}
\end{array}
\end{equation}

This implements the logical operation: if control qubit is $\ket{1}$, flip the target qubit.

\subsubsection{CNOT Implementation Strategy: CZ Gate + Single-Qubit Rotations}

The CNOT gate can be decomposed in terms of a controlled-$Z$ (CZ) entangling gate
sandwiched between two single-qubit $Y$-rotations on the target qubit:
\begin{equation}
\text{CNOT}_{12} = R_y^{(2)}\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) \cdot \text{CZ} \cdot R_y^{(2)}\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right),
\label{eq:cnot_decomp}
\end{equation}
where the standard controlled-$Z$ is the diagonal unitary
$\text{CZ}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,-1)$.
The decomposition relies on the basis-change identity
$R_y(\pi/2)\,\hat{\sigma}_z\,R_y(-\pi/2)=\hat{\sigma}_x$,
equivalent to the textbook conjugation
$\hat{H}\,\hat{\sigma}_z\,\hat{H} = \hat{\sigma}_x$ with the standard Hadamard
$\hat{H}=(\hat{\sigma}_x+\hat{\sigma}_z)/\sqrt{2}$.
We note that the bare identification $\hat{H}\equiv R_y(\pi/2)$
would yield $-\hat{\sigma}_x$ instead of $+\hat{\sigma}_x$ on conjugation;
the correct relation is $\hat{H}=R_y(\pi/2)\cdot\hat{\sigma}_z$ (up to a global
phase), which fixes the sign convention used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cnot_decomp}.

In a hardware implementation, the standard CZ is generated by a
$\hat{\sigma}_z^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_z^{(2)}$ interaction of duration
$\tau = \pi/(4 J_{\text{ZZ}})$, equivalent to
$\exp\!\left(-i\,\tfrac{\pi}{4}\,\hat{\sigma}_z^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_z^{(2)}\right)$
supplemented by local $\hat{\sigma}_z$ rotations on each qubit that absorb
the residual single-qubit phases.

The dominance of the transverse exchange coupling $J$ over the longitudinal
cross-Kerr $\zeta_{zz}$ established in
\S\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings} forces a reconsideration of the gate
protocol. A free-evolution CZ gate, which accumulates the conditional phase
$\zeta_{zz}\,t$, would require gate times of microseconds in our parameter
regime~--- well in excess of the achievable coherence
(see Fig.~\ref{fig:CZ_unfeasible}). We therefore adopt the
\emph{cross-resonance} (CR) protocol of Sheldon~\textit{et~al.}
\cite{Sheldon2016}, which converts the available $J$ coupling into an
all-microwave entangling interaction with sub-microsecond gate times.

\subsubsection{Cross-resonance Hamiltonian}
\label{subsec:CR_hamiltonian}

The CR gate is implemented by driving the control qubit $C$ at the frequency
of the target qubit $T$. In the rotating frame of the target, the static
detuning of the control is $\Delta_q = \omega_{q,C} - \omega_{q,T}$, and the
drive Hamiltonian reads
\begin{equation}
\hat{H}_{\text{drive}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Omega\,\hat{\sigma}_x^{(C)},
\end{equation}
with $\Omega$ the Rabi amplitude. Since $\Omega \ll \Delta_q$, the drive is
off-resonant for the control and does not significantly populate it; however,
the always-on $J$ coupling transfers the drive to the target through a
state-dependent virtual exchange. A second SW transformation in the dressed
two-qubit basis~\cite{Sheldon2016, blais2021circuit} produces the effective
Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}^{\text{CR}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Omega_{ZX}\,
\hat{\sigma}_z^{(C)}\!\otimes\hat{\sigma}_x^{(T)}
+ (\text{spurious } IX,\, ZI,\, IZ,\, ZZ\, \text{terms}),
\end{equation}
where the dominant ZX rate, including the leading transmon-anharmonicity
correction, is~\cite{Sheldon2016}
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\Omega_{ZX} = -\,\frac{J\,\Omega\,\alpha}{\Delta_q\,(\Delta_q + \alpha)}.
}
\label{eq:omega_ZX}
\end{equation}
The desired entangling operation is
$\mathrm{ZX}(\pi/2) \equiv \exp\!\left(-i\,\tfrac{\pi}{4}\,
\hat{\sigma}_z^{(C)}\!\otimes\hat{\sigma}_x^{(T)}\right)$, achieved at gate
duration $t_{\mathrm{CR}} = \pi/(2|\Omega_{ZX}|)$. A CNOT is recovered by
single-qubit pre- and post-rotations, which are virtually instantaneous in our
implementation~\cite{krantz2019}.

\paragraph{Gate speed is not limited by the anharmonicity.}
A property of Eq.~\eqref{eq:omega_ZX} that is central to the elevated-temperature
design concerns its behaviour at large anharmonicity. In the regime
$|\alpha| \gg \Delta_q$ --- which is precisely that of the $300$~GHz device,
where $|\alpha| = 15$~GHz while $\Delta_q = \InnDeltaQ$~MHz --- the factor
$(\Delta_q + \alpha) \to \alpha$ and the rate reduces to
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{ZX} \;\xrightarrow{\;|\alpha|\gg\Delta_q\;}\;
-\,\frac{J\,\Omega}{\Delta_q},
\label{eq:omega_ZX_largealpha}
\end{equation}
which is \emph{independent of} $\alpha$. The cross-resonance gate speed is thus
set by the exchange coupling $J$, the drive amplitude $\Omega$ and the
qubit--qubit detuning $\Delta_q$ alone; it is not degraded by the large
anharmonicity that the design deliberately adopts to suppress thermal excitation
and leakage. The two requirements --- a large $|\alpha|$ for thermal and leakage
protection, and a fast entangling gate --- are therefore not in tension. With
the canonical values this gives $\Omega_{ZX}/2\pi \simeq 1.8$~MHz and
$t_{\mathrm{CNOT}} \simeq \tCNOT$~ns, shorter than the coherence time
$T_{2,\mathrm{echo}} = \TTwoEcho~\mu$s by more than two orders of magnitude. The
two-qubit gate is consequently realised at fixed qubit frequency, with the
SQUID held at its flux-insensitive sweet spot, and requires no active flux
tuning during operation.

\subsubsection{Optimisation of the qubit--qubit detuning}
\label{subsec:dq_optimization}

Equation~\eqref{eq:omega_ZX} is non-monotonic in $\Delta_q$: at small
$\Delta_q$ the prefactor $1/[\Delta_q(\Delta_q+\alpha)]$ enhances $\Omega_{ZX}$,
but the drive amplitude is throttled by the constraint
$\Omega < \min(|\alpha|/3,\,\Delta_q/3)$ to suppress single-qubit leakage and
crosstalk. The qualitative behaviour at fixed $g$ and $\alpha$ is mapped in
Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff_Dq}: gate speed favours the lower end of the
\emph{stretched-CR} regime~\cite{Sheldon2016}, $\Delta_q \gtrsim 1.4|\alpha|$,
while the static $\zeta_{zz}$ and the robustness margins favour detunings far
from the resonant divergence at $\Delta_q = |\alpha|$.

The unconstrained speed optimum lies in the 230--350~MHz window
(panel~A), but the canonical operating point---for both configurations---is

for three margins. First, distance from the $\zeta_{zz}$ divergence at
$\Delta_q = |\alpha| = 200$~MHz: a target of $280$~MHz would leave only
$80$~MHz of margin, comparable to typical frequency-targeting scatter of
fixed-frequency transmons, whereas $600$~MHz is robust to realistic
fabrication spread. Second, the static cross-Kerr drops from
$|\zeta_{zz}| \sim 0.9$~MHz near the fast window to
$|\xi_{ZZ}|/2\pi = \BaseXiZZmag$~kHz at the canonical point (exact
diagonalisation, Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings}). Third, the
spectral selectivity between the two qubits doubles (panel~C). The cost is
$|\Omega_{ZX}|/2\pi \simeq 0.35$~MHz and $t_{\mathrm{CR}} \simeq 714$~ns,
still more than two orders of magnitude below the baseline coherence time
($T_2 \sim 100~\mu$s, Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}): the decoherence-limited
fidelity at the canonical point is $\simeq 99.1\%$ (panel~D), consistent
with the ratified $F_{\mathrm{CNOT}} = \FCNOTbase\%$ once control errors are
included. The canonical $\Delta_q$ is a \emph{fabrication} target: it is
fixed by the chosen qubit frequencies and reached with the SQUID at its
flux-insensitive sweet spot, so that operation remains fixed-frequency and no
active flux tuning is applied during gates.

\subsubsection{Echo-CR pulse sequence}
\label{subsec:echoCR}

The bare Hamiltonian Eq.~\eqref{eq:omega_ZX} contains spurious $IX$, $ZI$, $IZ$
and static $ZZ$ terms that, if uncompensated, degrade the gate fidelity to
$\sim 80\%$ even for ideal coherence times. The standard mitigation is the
\emph{echo-CR} sequence~\cite{Sheldon2016}: the drive is split in two halves of
equal duration with opposite sign, separated by a $\pi$-pulse on the control
qubit. By the symmetry of the conjugation $\hat{\sigma}_x^{(C)}
\hat{\sigma}_z^{(C)}\hat{\sigma}_x^{(C)} = -\hat{\sigma}_z^{(C)}$, the
spurious terms anti-commuting with $\hat{\sigma}_x^{(C)}$ are cancelled while
the desired ZX term is reinforced.

We have validated this protocol via direct integration of the master equation
(QuTiP~5.2~\cite{Johansson2013, qutip5}) for the parameters of
Table~\ref{tab:gate_params}, including decoherence at fixed illustrative
values ($T_1=25~\mu$s, $T_2=12~\mu$s, chosen before the final budget of
Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence} was consolidated) and thermal occupation
$\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = 0.028$ at $T=4$~K. With the canonical budget values
($T_1 = \TOneTotal~\mu$s, $T_2 = \TTwo~\mu$s) the decoherence contribution
to the gate error changes only at the $10^{-3}$ level, leaving the
conclusions of this validation unchanged: the dominant error of the unshaped
drive is coherent, not dissipative. The results are summarised in
Figure~\ref{fig:lindblad_validation}.

\textbf{The Challenge:} Thermal energy at 4~K is much higher. We need a radically different approach.

\subsection{Thermal constraints at elevated temperatures}

\subsubsection*{Thermal Energy at Elevated Temperatures}

At temperature $T$, the thermal energy scale is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{k_B T}{h} = 20.84~\text{GHz/K}
\end{equation}

Therefore:
\begin{align}
\text{At } T = 4~\text{K}: \quad \frac{k_B T}{h} &= 83.3~\text{GHz} \\
\text{At } T = 10~\text{K}: \quad \frac{k_B T}{h} &= 208.4~\text{GHz}
\end{align}

\subsubsection*{Quantum Regime Condition}
%At 300~GHz and various temperatures:

The quantum ratio at 300 GHz and 4 K is:
\begin{equation}
R_Q = \frac{\hbar\omega_{q1}}{\kB T} = \frac{300 \text{ GHz}}{83.3 \text{ GHz}} = 3.60
\label{eq:quantum_ratio}
\end{equation}
    
This value satisfies $R_Q > 3$, placing the system in a good quantum regime. Figure~\ref{fig:system_parameters}(a) shows the quantum ratio landscape across temperature and frequency space.

\subsubsection{Thermal Population}

The thermal occupation of the first excited state is:
\begin{equation}
n_{\text{th}} = \frac{1}{\exp(R_Q) - 1} = \frac{1}{\exp(3.60) - 1} = 0.0281 = 2.81\%
\label{eq:thermal_pop_300ghz}
\end{equation}

This thermal population contributes to gate errors but remains manageable. At lower temperatures, $n_{\text{th}}$ decreases exponentially:
\begin{align}
\text{At } T = 3 \text{ K}: \quad n_{\text{th}} &= 0.83\% \\
\text{At } T = 2 \text{ K}: \quad n_{\text{th}} &= 0.07\%
\end{align}

Figure~\ref{fig:system_parameters}(b) shows thermal population versus temperature at 300 GHz.

\subsubsection{Coupling and gate-time targets}

For the 300~GHz / 4~K design point we adopt, by analogy with the validated
baseline, a coupling $g = 80$~MHz with the cavity placed $\sim 1$~GHz above the
qubits, giving detunings $\Delta_1 = \InnDeltaOne$~GHz and
$\Delta_2 = \InnDeltaTwo$~GHz. These keep the dispersive ratios
$|\Delta_i|/g \in \{\InnRatioOne, \InnRatioTwo\}$ comfortably above the
$|\Delta|/g > 5$ threshold while still allowing a fast two-qubit gate. The
coupling $g$ is a representative microwave-analog value: its absolute magnitude
at $300$~GHz must be confirmed by the electromagnetic simulation discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:em_simulation} (reviewer comment), but the dispersive ratio
$g/\Delta\sim 0.08$ that governs the gate is preserved regardless. Inserting
these values in Eq.~\ref{eq:J_dispersive} gives a transverse exchange
$J/2\pi \simeq \InnJmag$~MHz, which through the echo cross-resonance protocol of
Sec.~\ref{sec:gate_strategy} yields a CNOT time
$t_{\mathrm{CNOT}} \simeq \tCNOT$~ns. The free-evolution alternative
based on the longitudinal cross-Kerr is excluded for the reasons summarised
in Sec.~\ref{sub:tqb_twoq_cr} and Fig.~\ref{fig:CZ_unfeasible}.

\subsubsection{Detuning Selection}

With both qubits near $300$~GHz at the canonical qubit--qubit detuning
$\Delta_q = \InnDeltaQ$~MHz and the cavity at $\omega_r \simeq 301.3$~GHz, the
qubit--cavity detunings are:
\begin{align}
\Delta_1 &= \omega_{q1} - \omega_r = 300.3 - 301.3 = \InnDeltaOne \text{ GHz} \\
\Delta_2 &= \omega_{q2} - \omega_r = 299.7 - 301.3 = \InnDeltaTwo \text{ GHz}
\end{align}

The dispersive ratios are:
\begin{align}
\frac{|\Delta_1|}{g} &= \frac{1.0 \text{ GHz}}{80 \text{ MHz}} = \InnRatioOne \gg 5 \quad \checkmark \\
\frac{|\Delta_2|}{g} &= \frac{1.6 \text{ GHz}}{80 \text{ MHz}} = \InnRatioTwo \gg 5 \quad \checkmark
\end{align}

Both ratios greatly exceed the requirement $|\Delta|/g > 5$, confirming valid dispersive operation. Large detunings also suppress Purcell decay (Section~\ref{sec:purcell}).

\subsubsection{Energy Scales}

For a transmon qubit with $E_J/E_C = 50$, the qubit frequency is:
\begin{equation}
\omega_q = \frac{\sqrt{8 E_J E_C}}{\hbar}
\end{equation}

With $E_J = 50 E_C$:
\begin{equation}
\omega_q = \frac{\sqrt{8 \cdot 50 E_C^2}}{\hbar} = \frac{20 E_C}{\hbar}
\end{equation}

Therefore:
\begin{equation}
E_C = \frac{\hbar \omega_q}{20} = \frac{(1.055 \times 10^{-34})(2\pi \times 300 \times 10^9)}{20} = \boxed{9.94 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
E_C = \boxed{0.062 \text{ meV} = 15.0 \text{ GHz}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
E_J = 50 E_C = \boxed{3.10 \text{ meV} = 750 \text{ GHz}}
\end{equation}

The anharmonicity is:
\begin{equation}
\alpha = -\frac{E_C}{\hbar} = \boxed{-15.0 \text{ GHz}}
\end{equation}

We note that the harmonic expression $\omega_q = \sqrt{8E_JE_C}/\hbar$ used
above omits the leading anharmonic correction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:omega01};
including it, $\omega_{01} \approx (\sqrt{8 \cdot 50}-1)\,E_C/\hbar = 19\,E_C/\hbar
\approx 285$~GHz at the same $(E_J, E_C)$, a $5\%$ shift well inside the
250--350~GHz design window. Targeting exactly 300~GHz with the corrected
expression corresponds to $E_J/E_C \approx 55$ at $E_C = 15$~GHz. Throughout
the chapter we quote the harmonic value for consistency with the canonical
parameter set; none of the conclusions is sensitive to this distinction.

\subsubsection{Junction geometry and the capacitance-limited design point}
\label{sec:junction_capacitance_point}

For a junction of critical-current density $J_c$, the area and the (circular) diameter follow directly from the target critical current,
\begin{equation}
A = \frac{I_c}{J_c}, \qquad d = \sqrt{\frac{4A}{\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{4 I_c}{\pi J_c}} .
\end{equation}
With $I_c = 1.51~\mu$A fixed, $J_c$ alone sets the junction size. The value of $J_c$ is, however, not a free choice: it is pinned by the shunt-capacitance budget.

\paragraph{The $E_C$--capacitance constraint fixes $J_c$.}
The two design choices $\omega_{01}/2\pi = 300$~GHz and $E_J/E_C = 50$ fix \emph{both} the charging energy and the critical current,
\begin{equation}
E_C = \frac{\hbar\omega_{01}}{20} = 15~\text{GHz} \;\Rightarrow\; C_{\Sigma}=\frac{e^2}{2E_C}=1.29~\text{fF},
\qquad
I_c = \frac{2\pi E_J}{\Phi_0}=1.51~\mu\text{A},
\end{equation}
so that neither $C_{\Sigma}$ nor $I_c$ is an adjustable parameter. The junction's own capacitance, $C_{J,\text{tot}}=c_s A = c_s I_c/J_c$ with specific capacitance $c_s$, must fit inside this budget. Because $I_c$ is fixed, the requirement $C_{J,\text{tot}}\le C_{\Sigma}$ reduces to a constraint on a single material ratio,
\begin{equation}
\frac{c_s}{J_c} \;\le\; \frac{C_{\Sigma}}{I_c} \;\approx\; 0.85~\frac{\text{fF}}{\mu\text{A}} .
\label{eq:cs_jc_constraint}
\end{equation}
This inverts the conventional transmon picture: at $5$--$6$~GHz the total capacitance $C_{\Sigma}\sim 50$--$100$~fF is dominated by a deliberately patterned shunt, whereas at $300$~GHz the budget is so small that the junction capacitance alone saturates it. The design is therefore driven toward \emph{small} junctions and \emph{high} $J_c$ -- the opposite of standard low-$J_c$ transmons -- and $J_c$ is an output of the capacitance budget rather than an independent fabrication parameter.

\paragraph{Sizing.}
Table~\ref{tab:junction_sizes} lists the junction diameter and capacitance as a function of $J_c$, using the empirical NbN/AlN scaling $c_s\simeq 20\sqrt{J_c\,[\text{kA/cm}^2]}~\text{fF}/\mu\text{m}^2$ reported for these tunnel junctions~\cite{Wang1994HighJcNbN}. Since $c_s$ grows with $J_c$ while $A\propto 1/J_c$, the capacitance falls only as $C_{J,\text{tot}}\propto 1/\sqrt{J_c}$, and the budget is met around $J_c\simeq 6$~kA/cm$^2$, where $d\simeq 180$~nm and $C_{J,\text{tot}}\simeq 1.2$~fF. Lower densities ($J_c\lesssim 1$~kA/cm$^2$) yield larger junctions whose capacitance overshoots the budget by $2$--$3\times$ or more; substantially higher densities provide little further headroom while degrading the junction's sub-gap quality (see below).

\paragraph{Is $J_c\simeq 6$~kA/cm$^2$ achievable?}
At the material level this density is modest for the NbN/AlN/NbN platform: submillimetre SIS mixers built on this trilayer use comparable or substantially higher densities, with $J_c$ in the tens of kA/cm$^2$ at barrier thicknesses of order $1$~nm~\cite{Wang1994HighJcNbN}. The genuine constraint is coherence rather than lithography: a qubit-grade junction must keep sub-gap leakage low, and the sub-gap quality factor $R_{sg}/R_N$ of all-nitride junctions falls from $\sim 23$ at $J_c\sim 50$~A/cm$^2$ to $\sim 6$ at $3$~kA/cm$^2$~\cite{kim2021}. Recent work addresses precisely this trade-off: all-nitride transmons grown entirely by atomic-layer deposition tune $J_c$ over seven orders of magnitude with sub-{\aa}ngstr\"om barrier-thickness control while retaining microsecond relaxation times above $300$~mK~\cite{Wang2026ALDNitride}. The chosen point -- $J_c\simeq 6$~kA/cm$^2$ at an AlN barrier of $\sim 1.3$~nm -- therefore sits at the frontier of qubit-grade fabrication, but is supported both by the maturity of high-$J_c$ NbN/AlN mixer technology and by the most recent coherence demonstrations.

\paragraph{Junction arrays are not required.}
A single junction of $d\simeq 180$~nm already satisfies \emph{both} the critical-current target and the capacitance budget, so the design does not depend on a large junction array. This is reinforced by the capacitance analysis of the next section: parallel arrays leave the total junction area -- and hence $C_{J,\text{tot}}=c_s I_c/J_c$ -- unchanged, and so cannot be used to relax the budget. Arrays therefore remain available only as an optional means of improving fabrication yield and uniformity; they are not part of the baseline single-junction solution.

\subsubsection{Shunt Capacitance Constraint and the Limits of Array Solutions}
\label{sec:shunt_cap_array}

The capacitance budget of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_jc_constraint} is tight enough that
it is natural to ask whether a junction \emph{array} could be used to relax it.
It cannot. The total junction capacitance,
\begin{equation}
C_{J,\text{tot}} \;=\; c_s\,\frac{I_c}{J_c},
\label{eq:CJ_parallel}
\end{equation}
depends only on the total junction area $I_c/J_c$, which an $N\times N$
\emph{parallel} array leaves unchanged: distributing the required critical
current over $N^{2}$ junctions divides the area of each by $N^{2}$ but restores
exactly the same total, and the individual capacitances add back in parallel.
A parallel array therefore reproduces the single-junction capacitance and
offers no relief on $C_{\Sigma}$ -- for instance, any parallel array delivering
$I_c=1.51~\mu$A at $J_c=1$~kA/cm$^2$ has $C_{J,\text{tot}}\approx 3$~fF,
independently of its multiplicity, more than twice the budget.

A \emph{series} array of $N$ junctions works in the opposite direction:
the capacitance becomes $C_{\text{eff}}=C_{\text{single}}/N$, but the
effective Josephson energy is reduced in the same proportion
($E_{J,\text{eff}}=E_{J,\text{single}}/N$, see e.g.\
Ref.~\cite{manucharyan2009fluxonium}),
so the ratio $E_J/E_C$ scales as $1/N^{2}$ and is degraded rather than improved.
Series geometries thus relax the capacitance constraint only at the cost of
demanding even larger individual junctions to recover the target $E_J$,
moving the design back into the high-capacitance regime ruled out by
Tab.~\ref{tab:junction_sizes}.

In summary, the constraint $C_{\Sigma}\approx 1.3$~fF is genuinely tight and is
\emph{not} relaxed by array multiplicity. It is met instead by the
single-junction route identified above: a high-density junction
($J_c\simeq 6$~kA/cm$^2$, $d\simeq 180$~nm) with a $\sim 1.3$~nm AlN barrier,
combined with a 3D cavity environment (Sec.~\ref{sec:dielectric_loss_300ghz})
in which $C_{\text{stray}}$ to the substrate is suppressed by orders of
magnitude relative to a planar 2D geometry. The hardware programme outlined
here adopts both measures; arrays are retained only as an optional aid to
fabrication yield and uniformity, not as a means of meeting the capacitance
budget.

\section{Two-qubit system: elevated-temperature configuration}

\subsection{Design requirements at 300\,GHz / 4\,K}
\label{sub:tqi_design}

The three parameters that fix the device — the charging energy $E_C$, the
two junction Josephson energies $E_{J1},\,E_{J2}$, and the resulting SQUID
asymmetry $\eta$ — are not independent design choices but are tied together
by the physics of the transmon and by the intended high-temperature
operating point. We therefore briefly motivate each value in
Tab.~\ref{tab:squid_design_params} before presenting the table itself.

The charging energy $E_C = e^{2}/(2 C_{\Sigma})$ is set by the total qubit
shunt capacitance and selects the operating regime of the device.
Established transmon implementations on Al/AlO$_x$/Al junctions adopt
$E_C/h \in [0.15,\,0.30]\,\text{GHz}$~\cite{Koch2007Transmon,kjaergaard2020};
this range balances two competing requirements. Larger $E_C$ improves the
anharmonicity $\alpha \approx -E_C$ and therefore the spectral selectivity
of microwave control, suppressing leakage to the
$\lvert 2\rangle$ state during fast pulses; smaller $E_C$ pushes the
device deeper into the transmon regime ($E_J/E_C \gg 1$) and
exponentially suppresses charge-noise dephasing. The value $E_C/h =
\SI{0.2}{GHz}$ adopted here sits in the middle of the standard range and
is therefore representative rather than extremal — it is the same charging
energy a state-of-the-art mK transmon would adopt.

The junction Josephson energies $E_{J1} = \SI{15.0}{GHz}$ and
$E_{J2} = \SI{12.0}{GHz}$ are chosen with two distinct constraints in mind.
First, their sum $E_{J1}+E_{J2}=\SI{27.0}{GHz}$ at zero flux gives
$E_J/E_C = 135$, which is deep in the transmon regime as defined by
Koch~\textit{et~al.}~\cite{Koch2007Transmon} (the condition is conventionally
quoted as $E_J/E_C \gtrsim 50$, where charge dispersion drops below
$10^{-4}$ of the qubit frequency); this ratio guarantees that residual
charge noise is no longer a leading dephasing channel. Second, the
asymmetry $\eta=(E_{J1}-E_{J2})/(E_{J1}+E_{J2})\approx 11\%$ is chosen to
preserve a finite minimum Josephson energy
$E_J(\Phi_0/2) = |E_{J1}-E_{J2}| = \SI{3}{GHz}$ at the
flux-insensitive sweet spot, so that the qubit retains anharmonic
structure over the whole flux-tuning range. The 11\,\% asymmetry has been
demonstrated in standard Al-based asymmetric SQUID transmons and is
within reach of one-pass shadow-evaporation
fabrication~\cite{Hutchings2017,nakamura2011epitaxialNbN};
it is therefore a fabrication target, not a fundamental design freedom.

The resulting parameter set, summarised in Tab.~\ref{tab:squid_design_params},
is therefore representative of a standard asymmetric SQUID transmon and
will serve in the rest of this chapter as the baseline against which
high-temperature variants are compared.

\subsubsection{Anharmonicity as Percentage of Qubit Frequency}

\begin{equation}
\frac{|\alpha|}{\omega_{01}} = \frac{362}{1972} \approx 18.4\%
\end{equation}

This is within the acceptable range for transmon operation. We note that the relatively large \(|\alpha|/\omega_{01}\) at the sweet spot enhances spectral selectivity, but it also indicates that the device is less deep in the transmon limit at that bias point, implying a trade-off between tunability, noise protection, and level structure.  While typical Al-based transmons operate with $|\alpha|/\omega_{01} \sim 1-5\%$, the larger relative anharmonicity here is a consequence of the lower qubit frequency necessitated at this temperature regime of operation.

\subsubsection{Significance of Negative Anharmonicity for Quantum Gates}

\subsubsection{Selective Addressing}

Negative anharmonicity enables frequency-selective control. A microwave drive at frequency $\omega_{01}$ can excite the $|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$ transition without significantly affecting the $|1\rangle \leftrightarrow |2\rangle$ transition at frequency $\omega_{12}$, provided:
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{\text{Rabi}} \ll |\alpha| \ll \omega_{01}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_{\text{Rabi}}$ is the gate Rabi frequency.

For our design:
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{\text{Rabi}} \lesssim \frac{|\alpha|}{3} \approx \SI{120}{MHz}
\end{equation}
This corresponds to single-qubit gate times:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{gate}} \sim \frac{\pi}{2\Omega_{\text{Rabi}}} \approx \SI{13}{ns}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Leakage Suppression}

During gate operations, unwanted transitions to $|2\rangle$ (leakage) must be suppressed. The anharmonicity provides a natural energy detuning:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\text{leak}} = |\alpha| = \SI{362}{MHz}
\end{equation}

For a gate pulse with bandwidth $\Delta\omega \sim 1/\tau_{\text{gate}} \approx \SI{80}{MHz}$, the leakage probability scales as:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{leak}} \sim \left(\frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta_{\text{leak}}}\right)^2 \approx \left(\frac{80}{362}\right)^2 \approx 5\%
\end{equation}

This can be further reduced to $< 0.1\%$ using DRAG (Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate) pulse shaping~\cite{Motzoi2009}, which explicitly compensates for the finite anharmonicity.

\subsubsection{Two-Qubit Gate Feasibility}

For two-qubit gates mediated by the cavity bus, two distinct interactions
emerge from the Schrieffer--Wolff reduction. The dominant transverse exchange
coupling (Majer~\textit{et~al.}~\cite{Majer2007}) takes the form
\begin{equation}
J = \frac{g_1 g_2}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_1}+\frac{1}{\Delta_2}\right),
\label{eq:transverse_J_intro}
\end{equation}
where $g_i$ is the qubit--resonator coupling and
$\Delta_i = \omega_{q,i}-\omega_r$ the qubit--cavity detuning. The
longitudinal cross-Kerr coupling requires the transmon anharmonicity~$\alpha$
and reads~\cite{blais2021circuit}
\begin{equation}
\zeta_{zz} = 2J^{2}\!\left[\frac{1}{\Delta_q-\alpha_2}-\frac{1}{\Delta_q+\alpha_1}\right],
\label{eq:cross_kerr_intro}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta_q = \omega_{q,1}-\omega_{q,2}$ the qubit--qubit detuning. The
two interactions have different physical origins and very different
magnitudes: in the relevant operating regime $J$ dominates over $\zeta_{zz}$
by an order of magnitude, with direct consequences for the choice of
two-qubit gate protocol. The full derivation of both couplings, with
numerical validation against exact diagonalisation, is given in
Section~\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings}, while the gate-protocol
analysis is presented in Section~\ref{sec:gate_strategy}.

\subsubsection{Flux-Tunability and Sweet-Spot Operation}

\subsubsection{Frequency Range}

The qubit frequency varies with applied flux:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{01}(\Phi) \approx \sqrt{8E_J(\Phi)E_C} - E_C
\end{equation}

For our parameters:
at $\Phi = 0$, $E_J = \SI{27}{GHz}$, giving $\omega_{01} \approx \SI{6.37}{GHz}$, while at $\Phi = \Phi_0/2$, $E_J = \SI{3}{GHz}$, giving $\omega_{01} \approx \SI{1.97}{GHz}$.

This provides a tuning range of approximately \SI{4.4}{GHz}, enabling:
enabling frequency-addressable multi-qubit architectures, dynamic flux-based gates (e.g.\ adiabatic CZ gates), and compatibility with flux-dip control schemes for high-frequency operation.

\subsubsection{Sweet-Spot Benefits}

At the flux sweet spot ($\Phi = \Phi_0/2$), the first-order sensitivity to flux noise vanishes:
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial\omega_{01}}{\partial\Phi}\right|_{\Phi=\Phi_0/2} = 0
\end{equation}

This leads to enhanced pure dephasing time $T_2^*$ due to suppression of low-frequency flux noise. The asymmetry $\eta = 11.1\%$ ensures:
offering a non-zero $E_J(\Phi_0/2) = |E_\Delta| = \SI{3}{GHz}$ that maintains the anharmonicity, a reduced charge dispersion compared to symmetric SQUIDs, and a stable operating point well suited to high-fidelity gates.


\subsection{Dispersive regime in the elevated-temperature design}
\label{sec:dispersive}
\label{sub:tqi_dispersive}

\subsubsection{Criterion for Dispersive Regime}

The system operates in the dispersive regime when:
\begin{equation}
|\Delta_i| \gg g_i \quad \text{for all qubits } i
\end{equation}

For our parameters:
\begin{align}
\frac{|\Delta_1|}{g_1} &= 8.8 \quad (\text{satisfied})\\
\frac{|\Delta_2|}{g_2} &= 16.2 \quad (\text{satisfied})
\end{align}

In this regime, real photon exchange between qubits and cavity is suppressed, but virtual processes lead to effective qubit-qubit interactions mediated by the cavity.

\subsubsection{Effective Dispersive Hamiltonian}

Under a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation (second-order perturbation theory in $g_i/\Delta_i$), the Hamiltonian becomes:
\begin{equation}
\hat{H}_{\text{disp}} = \hbar\tilde{\omega}_r \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + \sum_{i=1}^{2} \frac{\hbar\tilde{\omega}_{q,i}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z^{(i)} + \sum_{i=1}^{2} \hbar\chi_i \hat{\sigma}_z^{(i)} \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + \hbar\zeta \hat{\sigma}_z^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_z^{(2)}
\label{eq:dispersive_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}

where the dispersive shifts are:
\begin{equation}
\chi_i = \frac{g_i^2}{\Delta_i}
\end{equation}

and the effective \emph{transverse} qubit--qubit exchange coupling
(Majer~\textit{et~al.}~\cite{Majer2007}) is
\begin{equation}
J = \frac{g_1 g_2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_1} + \frac{1}{\Delta_2}\right) .
\label{eq:J_dispersive}
\end{equation}
This is the coupling appearing as $J(\hat{\sigma}_+^{(1)}\hat{\sigma}_-^{(2)}
+ \mathrm{h.c.})$ in the rotated frame; the residual longitudinal
$\hat{\sigma}_z\!\otimes\!\hat{\sigma}_z$ cross-Kerr~$\zeta_{zz}$ requires
the transmon anharmonicity and is derived in
Section~\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings}.

In the dispersive regime and for weak anharmonicity, the transmon-induced dispersive shift is well
approximated by
\begin{equation}
\frac{\chi_i}{2\pi}\simeq
\frac{\left(\frac{g_i}{2\pi}\right)^2 \left(\frac{\alpha_i}{2\pi}\right)}
{\left(\frac{\Delta_i}{2\pi}\right)\left(\frac{\Delta_i}{2\pi}+\frac{\alpha_i}{2\pi}\right)} ,
\label{eq:chi_transmon}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta_i = \omega_i-\omega_r\) and \(\alpha_i=\omega_{12,i}-\omega_{01,i}<0\).

The renormalised frequencies are:
\begin{align}
\tilde{\omega}_r &= \omega_r + \chi_1 + \chi_2 \\
\tilde{\omega}_{q,i} &= \omega_{q,i} + \chi_i
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Numerical values at the baseline operating point}

Adopting the baseline operating point $\Delta_q/2\pi = \BaseDeltaQ$~MHz
($\omega_{q,1}/2\pi = 5.8$~GHz, $\omega_{q,2}/2\pi = 5.2$~GHz,
$\omega_r/2\pi = 6.5$~GHz; the qubit--qubit detuning lies in the
stretched-CR regime discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:dq_optimization}),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:chi_transmon} with $g_{1,2}/2\pi = 80$~MHz and
$\alpha/2\pi = -200$~MHz gives
\begin{align}
\chi_1 &= \frac{g_1^2}{\Delta_1}\frac{\alpha}{\Delta_1+\alpha}
       = \SI{\BaseChiOne}{MHz} ,\\
\chi_2 &= \frac{g_2^2}{\Delta_2}\frac{\alpha}{\Delta_2+\alpha}
       = \SI{\BaseChiTwo}{MHz} ,
\end{align}
and the transverse exchange coupling is
\begin{equation}
J = \frac{g_1 g_2}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_1}+\frac{1}{\Delta_2}\right)
  = \SI{\BaseJ}{MHz} .
\end{equation}
The corresponding longitudinal cross-Kerr, computed by exact
diagonalisation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_zz_correct}), is
$\xi_{ZZ}/2\pi \simeq \BaseXiZZ$~kHz: far too small to drive a
free-evolution CZ at high fidelity, which motivates the echo-CR gate of
Section~\ref{subsec:echoCR}, and simultaneously small enough that the
residual ZZ phase accumulated during gates is a sub-dominant error.

The cavity-frequency differences (relevant for dispersive readout) are:
\begin{align}
\omega_r(\ket{00}) - \omega_r(\ket{01}) &= 2\chi_2 = \SI{-1.31}{MHz} \\
\omega_r(\ket{00}) - \omega_r(\ket{10}) &= 2\chi_1 = \SI{-4.06}{MHz} \\
\omega_r(\ket{00}) - \omega_r(\ket{11}) &= 2(\chi_1 + \chi_2) = \SI{-5.37}{MHz}
\end{align}
Both qubits are red-detuned from the cavity, so $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$
have the same sign and the four computational states map onto four
distinct, ordered cavity frequencies; the minimum separation is
$2|\chi_2| \simeq 1.3$~MHz, between $\ket{00}$ and $\ket{01}$
(cf.\ Fig.~\ref{fig:dispersive_shifts}).

\subsubsection{Readout Protocol}

The readout proceeds in four steps: \emph{(i)} the cavity is driven with a weak
coherent tone at $\omega_r$; \emph{(ii)} the transmitted (or reflected) signal is
measured by IQ demodulation; \emph{(iii)} the measured phase and amplitude depend
on the qubit state; \emph{(iv)} the state is then discriminated based on the
measured IQ point.

\subsubsection{Resolution Criterion}

For good state discrimination, the dispersive shift must exceed the cavity linewidth:
\begin{equation}
2|\chi_i| > \kappa
\end{equation}

For the baseline cavity linewidth $\kappa/2\pi = \SI{1}{MHz}$
($Q = \omega_r/\kappa = 6500$):
\begin{align}
\frac{2|\chi_1|}{\kappa} &= \frac{4.06}{1} \simeq 4.1 \quad (\text{comfortable}) \\
\frac{2|\chi_2|}{\kappa} &= \frac{1.31}{1} \simeq 1.3 \quad (\text{adequate})
\end{align}

The criterion is met for both qubits, comfortably for Qubit~1 and
adequately for Qubit~2; the smaller margin on Qubit~2 can be compensated
by a longer readout integration window or a slightly reduced $\kappa$.

\subsubsection{Dispersive Regime and Readout}

The qubit is coupled to a microwave resonator (cavity) with Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}
H = \hbar\omega_q \hat{\sigma}_z/2 + \hbar\omega_r \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + \hbar g(\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a})(\hat{\sigma}_+ + \hat{\sigma}_-)
\label{eq:jaynes_cummings}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_q$ is the qubit frequency, $\omega_r$ is the resonator frequency, $g$ is the coupling strength, and $\hat{a}$, $\hat{\sigma}$ are annihilation and Pauli operators.

In the dispersive regime, where the detuning $\Delta = \omega_q - \omega_r$ satisfies $|\Delta| \gg g$, the Hamiltonian can be diagonalised to give:
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{disp}} = \hbar\tilde{\omega}_q \hat{\sigma}_z/2 + \hbar\tilde{\omega}_r \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + \hbar\chi \hat{\sigma}_z \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}
%\label{eq:dispersive_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}

The dispersive shift $\chi$ quantifies the state-dependent cavity frequency:
\begin{equation}
\chi = \frac{g^2}{\Delta} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\Delta + \alpha}
\label{eq:dispersive_shift}
\end{equation}

This enables quantum non-demolition (QND) readout: the cavity frequency shifts by $2\chi$ when the qubit flips from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$, allowing state discrimination without collapsing the quantum state.

\subsubsection{Dispersive Shift Calculations}

From Eq.~\ref{eq:dispersive_shift}, the dispersive shift for each qubit is:
\begin{equation}
\chi_i = \frac{g^2}{\Delta_i} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\Delta_i + \alpha}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Qubit 1 ($\Delta_1 = \InnDeltaOne$~GHz)}

\begin{align}
\chi_1 &= \frac{(80)^2}{-1000} \frac{-15000}{-1000 - 15000} = \boxed{\InnChiOne \text{ MHz}}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Qubit 2 ($\Delta_2 = \InnDeltaTwo$~GHz)}

\begin{align}
\chi_2 &= \frac{(80)^2}{-1600} \frac{-15000}{-1600 - 15000} = \boxed{\InnChiTwo \text{ MHz}}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Physical Interpretation}

The dispersive shift $\chi_i$ has three physically important consequences that we now
describe in turn.

\textbf{Quantum non-demolition readout.} The cavity frequency shifts by $2\chi$
when the qubit transitions $\ket{0} \to \ket{1}$:
\begin{equation}
\omega_r(\text{state}) = \begin{cases} \omega_r - \chi & \text{if qubit in } \ket{0} \\ \omega_r + \chi & \text{if qubit in } \ket{1} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Readout fidelity.} A larger $|\chi|$ enables faster, higher-fidelity readout.
Typical targets are $|\chi| > 1$~MHz; our baseline values
($|\chi_1| = \BaseChiOneMag$~MHz, $|\chi_2| = \BaseChiTwoMag$~MHz) meet this
target for the control qubit and are workable for the target qubit.

\textbf{Stark shift.} Readout photons in the cavity cause AC Stark shifts of the
qubit frequencies, which must be calibrated out before each operation.

Figure~\ref{fig:dispersive_shifts}(b) illustrates the cavity frequency shifts for the four two-qubit states.

\begin{align}
\frac{|\Delta_1|}{g} &= \frac{1.0 \text{ GHz}}{80 \text{ MHz}} = \InnRatioOne \\
\frac{|\Delta_2|}{g} &= \frac{1.6 \text{ GHz}}{80 \text{ MHz}} = \InnRatioTwo
\end{align}

Both ratios exceed the requirement, confirming dispersive operation. This ensures that perturbation theory is accurate, that Lamb-shift corrections remain at the percent level, that qubit--cavity hybridisation is limited, and that two-qubit gates can reach high fidelity.

\subsubsection{Purcell Decay}
\label{sec:purcell}

The qubit coupling to the lossy cavity enhances decay through the Purcell effect:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{\text{Purcell}} = \kappa \left(\frac{g}{\Delta}\right)^{2},
\label{eq:purcell_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = \omega_r/Q_L$ is the cavity decay rate. For the 300~GHz
design parameters ($g/2\pi = 80$~MHz, $\Delta_1/2\pi = \InnDeltaOne$~GHz,
$\Delta_2/2\pi = \InnDeltaTwo$~GHz, $\omega_r/2\pi \simeq 301$~GHz) and the
optimistic sub-THz quality factor $Q_L = 10^5$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_integration_300ghz} ($\kappa/2\pi \approx 3.0$~MHz,
$1/\kappa \approx 53$~ns), the \emph{bare} Purcell limits are
\begin{equation}
T_{1,\text{Purcell},1}^{\text{bare}} = \frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\frac{\Delta_1}{g}\right)^{2}
\approx 8~\mu\text{s},
\qquad
T_{1,\text{Purcell},2}^{\text{bare}} \approx 21~\mu\text{s},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ comparable to the intrinsic $T_1$ budget and therefore \emph{not}
negligible. Meeting the $T_{1,\text{Purcell}} = 320~\mu$s entry adopted in
the decoherence budget requires suppressing the cavity-mediated density of
states at the qubit frequency by a factor $\gtrsim 40$ ($\approx 16$~dB),
equivalent to an effective $\kappa_{\text{eff}}/2\pi \lesssim 78$~kHz
($Q_{\text{eff}} \gtrsim 4\times10^{6}$). Since bare quality factors of
this magnitude exceed the $Q_0 \sim 10^4$--$10^5$ projected for sub-THz
cavities in Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_integration_300ghz}, the design adopts a
\emph{Purcell filter}---a bandpass element in the readout line, standard in
GHz-band circuit QED, routinely providing $\geq 20$~dB of out-of-band
suppression at the qubit frequency~\cite{blais2021circuit}. With 20~dB of
suppression the Purcell limits become
$T_{1,\text{Purcell},1} \approx 0.8$~ms and
$T_{1,\text{Purcell},2} \approx 2.1$~ms; the budget entry of $320~\mu$s is
the conservative (16~dB) value for the more exposed qubit. The demonstration
of a Purcell-filtered readout at sub-THz frequencies is an open hardware
requirement, listed as part of P6 in Sec.~\ref{subsec:tech_prereq_4K}.

\section{Decoherence Analysis}
\label{sec:decoherence}

\subsection{Overview of Decoherence Mechanisms}

Quantum coherence is limited by interactions with the environment. Three time scales are conventionally introduced to characterise it. The \emph{relaxation time} $T_1$ is the characteristic time for the qubit to decay from the excited state $\ket{1}$ to the ground state $\ket{0}$ by emitting an energy quantum $\hbar\omega_q$ to its environment, so that the diagonal density-matrix element $\rho_{11}(t) = \rho_{11}(0)\,e^{-t/T_1}$. The \emph{dephasing time} $T_\phi$ is the time scale on which off-diagonal elements decay because of phase noise that does not exchange energy with the qubit, typically dominated, in transmon architectures, by low-frequency fluctuations of the magnetic flux at non-sweet-spot bias points and by photon-number fluctuations in the readout resonator. The \emph{coherence time} $T_2$ is the time scale on which off-diagonal elements decay overall, with relaxation and dephasing contributing additively to the rate~\cite{krantz2019,blais2021circuit}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_2} \;=\; \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\phi}.
\label{eq:t2_relation}
\end{equation}

A fourth time scale, $T_2^*$, is operationally defined and arises whenever the noise that drives dephasing is slow on the time scale of a single experimental run but fluctuates from run to run. The free-induction signal in a Ramsey experiment then decays with a characteristic time $T_2^* < T_2$ because the slow component of the noise produces inhomogeneous broadening across the ensemble of single-shot acquisitions. Three echo-style protocols~\cite{krantz2019,kjaergaard2020} probe these time scales experimentally: a $\pi/2$ pulse followed by free evolution and a final $\pi/2$ pulse (\emph{Ramsey sequence}) returns $T_2^*$; the same sequence with an additional $\pi$ pulse at the midpoint (\emph{Hahn echo}) refocuses the slowly-varying noise component and returns the longer $T_2$; and an $N$-pulse periodic refocusing scheme (\emph{CPMG sequence}) extends the noise filter to higher frequencies and provides spectroscopic access to the dephasing-noise spectrum. Throughout the rest of the chapter, the notation $T_1, T_2$ refers to the time scales defined above; $T_2^*$ is invoked only where the distinction between Ramsey and Hahn-echo measurements matters, in particular in Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity}.

\subsection{$T_1$ Decoherence Mechanisms}

Multiple physical mechanisms contribute to $T_1$. The total decay rate is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_1^{\text{total}}} = \sum_i \frac{1}{T_{1,i}}
\label{eq:t1_total}
\end{equation}

We analyse each mechanism individually.

\subsubsection{Dielectric Loss ($\propto \omega$)}
\label{sec:dielectric_loss_300ghz}

Energy dissipation in substrate and capacitor dielectrics contributes:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_{1,\text{diel}}} = \omega \cdot \tan\delta \cdot p
\label{eq:t1_dielectric}
\end{equation}
where $\tan\delta$ is the dielectric loss tangent and $p$ is the participation ratio (fraction of electric field energy stored in lossy dielectric materials).

\paragraph{Participation Ratio.} 
For elevated-temperature operation at 300~GHz, minimising dielectric loss requires careful design. The participation ratio $p$ quantifies what fraction of the qubit's electric-field energy resides in lossy dielectrics versus vacuum. In planar 2D architectures, where the qubit sits directly on a substrate with planar capacitors, typical values are $p \sim 0.5$--$0.9$, leading to severe dielectric loss at high frequencies. 

However, with a \textbf{3D electromagnetic cavity design}—where the qubit is suspended within a metallic enclosure and the electric field is predominantly confined to vacuum—the participation ratio can be reduced to $p \sim 0.05$--$0.15$. Through geometric optimisation including air-bridge capacitors, minimised dielectric interfaces, and thin junction barriers, we adopt a conservative target of \textbf{$p = 0.1$}, which represents a factor of 5--10 improvement over 2D planar designs~\cite{place2021,Wang2015}.

\paragraph{Frequency Scaling.} 
Dielectric loss scales \textit{linearly} with frequency. At 300~GHz compared to the 5.8~GHz baseline:
\begin{equation}
\frac{T_{1,\text{diel}}(5.8 \text{ GHz})}{T_{1,\text{diel}}(300 \text{ GHz})} = \frac{300}{5.8} \approx 52
\end{equation}

This factor-of-52 degradation represents a major challenge, requiring both ultra-low-loss substrates and 3D cavity architecture to maintain adequate coherence times.

\paragraph{Loss tangent at cryogenic and sub-THz conditions: an open assumption.}
The linear $\omega$-scaling above assumes a frequency-independent $\tan\delta$.
At room temperature this is already optimistic, as $\tan\delta$ itself
typically scales at least linearly with frequency~\cite{Martinis2005TLS},
which would make $\Gamma_{\text{diel}}$ super-linear in $\omega$.
At cryogenic temperatures the dominant two-level-system (TLS) contribution
to substrate loss is partially saturated and depends only weakly on frequency
in the 1--30~GHz band routinely characterised in the literature~\cite{Muller2019TLS},
but direct measurements of $\tan\delta$ for crystalline sapphire or
high-resistivity silicon at 4~K and 300~GHz are scarce in the published record.
The values reported in Table~\ref{tab:substrate_t1_diel} are therefore best
read as extrapolations from the 1--30~GHz cryogenic data of
Refs.~\cite{Krupka1999Sapphire,Krupka2006Silicon,Janezic2014Silicon,Read2023Sapphire}.
We treat the cryogenic loss tangent at 300~GHz as a design parameter with
at least a factor-of-three uncertainty band: $\tan\delta_{\text{sapphire}}
\in [10^{-7},\,3\times 10^{-7}]$ at $4\,\text{K}$ and $300\,\text{GHz}$
would map onto $T_{1,\text{diel}}\in[\,18,\,53\,]\,\mu$s with $p=0.1$.
A dedicated cryogenic sub-THz characterisation of the candidate substrates
is therefore an explicit prerequisite to a hardware demonstration of the elevated-temperature design.

\paragraph{Numerical Calculation.}
For a sapphire substrate with $\tan\delta = 10^{-7}$ (typical for high-quality crystalline c-axis sapphire at 4~K~\cite{place2021,Romanenko2020}), the dielectric decay rate at 300~GHz is:
\begin{align}
\Gamma_{\text{diel}} &= \omega \cdot \tan\delta \cdot p \nonumber \\
                     &= (2\pi \times 300 \times 10^9) \times (10^{-7}) \times (0.1) \nonumber \\
                     &= 1.885 \times 10^{4} \text{ s}^{-1}
\end{align}

Therefore:
\begin{equation}
T_{1,\text{diel}} = \frac{1}{\Gamma_{\text{diel}}} = \frac{1}{1.885 \times 10^{4}} = 5.305 \times 10^{-5} \text{ s} = 53.1~\mu\text{s}
\label{eq:t1_sapphire}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{3D Cavity Integration and Qubit--Cavity Coupling at 300\,GHz}
\label{sec:cavity_integration_300ghz}

The dielectric-loss analysis above implicitly assumes that the qubit can be
embedded in a 3D superconducting cavity with $p \sim 0.1$ — a non-trivial
requirement at 300\,GHz. We address here three related concerns that the
present analysis is not able to settle definitively, and that constitute open
questions for an experimental programme.

\paragraph{Geometrical scaling.}
The free-space wavelength at 300\,GHz is $\lambda = c/\nu \approx 1\,\text{mm}$,
so a fundamental-mode rectangular cavity (TE$_{101}$) has linear dimensions of
order $\lambda/2 \sim 0.5\,\text{mm}$ and volume
$V_{\text{cav}} \sim 0.1\,\text{mm}^{3}$. This is more than two orders of
magnitude smaller than a standard transmon chip
($\sim 5\times 5\times 0.3\,\text{mm}^{3} \approx 7.5\,\text{mm}^{3}$),
so the qubit chip cannot simply be inserted into the cavity in the conventional
millikelvin geometry. Three architectural directions can in principle restore
geometric compatibility: (i) fabrication of the qubit and shunt capacitor
directly on the cavity inner wall in a monolithic ``qubit-as-cavity'' geometry;
(ii) drastic chip miniaturisation to a $\sim 0.3 \times 0.3\,\text{mm}^{2}$
footprint, exploiting the small junction sizes already imposed by the
$C_{\Sigma}\sim 1\,\text{fF}$ budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:shunt_cap_array}; or
(iii) operation in a higher-order cavity mode TE$_{m0p}$ with $m,p\gg 1$
to accommodate a larger physical volume at the same nominal frequency, at
the cost of reduced field uniformity at the qubit location. None of these
options has been demonstrated experimentally for a transmon qubit at 300\,GHz,
and the choice is left as part of the next-step hardware-design phase.

\paragraph{Cavity quality factor at sub-THz.}
The cavity-limited radiative coherence time is
$T_{1,\text{cav}} = Q_L/\omega$, where $Q_L$ is the loaded quality factor.
For a 3D superconducting cavity the internal quality factor is
$Q_0 = G/R_s$, with $G$ a purely geometric form factor
($\sim 200$--$400\,\Omega$ for half-wave geometries) and $R_s$ the surface
resistance. In the BCS regime $T \ll T_c/2$, $R_s$ scales as
$\omega^{2} \exp(-\Delta/k_B T)$, but a frequency-independent residual term
$R_{s,\text{res}}\sim 10$--$100\,\mu\Omega$ typically dominates at sub-THz for
high-quality superconducting films~\cite{Romanenko2020}. Combining the two
contributions yields $Q_0 \sim 10^{4}$--$10^{5}$ for a NbTiN cavity at 300\,GHz
and 4\,K, two to three orders of magnitude below the $Q\sim 10^{7}$--$10^{8}$
achievable at 5\,GHz and 20\,mK. The corresponding bare radiative
$T_1 \sim 50$--$500\,\mu$s (for the optimistic $Q_L=10^{5}$) is comfortable
for gate operation but leaves little margin once chip-induced loading and
dielectric participation are also accounted for, reinforcing the conclusion
of Sec.~\ref{sec:dielectric_loss_300ghz} that all loss channels must be
suppressed simultaneously.

\paragraph{Qubit--cavity coupling and the need for electromagnetic simulation.}\label{sec:em_simulation}
The coupling strengths $g_1=g_2=80\,\text{MHz}$ adopted in
Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings} and
Tab.~\ref{tab:heatsQ_baseline_params} were fixed at the architecture level by
the dispersive-regime requirement $g/\Delta \sim 0.1$, rather than computed
from a physical qubit--cavity geometry. At 300\,GHz, the qubit's effective
dipole moment depends sensitively on the small junction-array geometry of
Sec.~\ref{sec:shunt_cap_array}, and the local cavity field at the qubit
location depends on the precise mode pattern of the chosen cavity geometry.
Both quantities are difficult to estimate analytically with sub-percent accuracy.
Quantitative validation of $g$ therefore requires finite-element electromagnetic
simulation (Ansys HFSS, COMSOL or CST Microwave Studio) of the kind routinely
used for millikelvin 3D transmon design~\cite{Wang2015,blais2021circuit}.
We treat $g_{1,2}/2\pi=80\,\text{MHz}$ as an architecture-level target that
must be confirmed (or revised) by such a simulation as part of the next
hardware-design step. A factor-of-two discrepancy with respect to the simulated
value would propagate to a factor-of-four shift in the dispersive shifts
$\chi_i = g_i^{2}/\Delta_i$ and in the cross-resonance gate timing of
Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_mediated_couplings}, and would have to be reflected in
the parameter set of Tab.~\ref{tab:heatsQ_baseline_params}.

\subsubsection{Quasiparticle Tunneling (Independent of $\omega$)}

Thermally excited quasiparticles in the superconductor can tunnel
through the junctions, contributing to relaxation. Their normalised
density follows~\cite{Catelani2011QP}
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{qp}}(T) \;\equiv\; \frac{n_{\text{qp}}}{n_{\text{cp}}}
   \;\simeq\; \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\kB T}{\Delta_{\text{sc}}}}
     \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_{\text{sc}}}{\kB T}\right) \,,
\label{eq:nqp}
\end{equation}
normalised to the Cooper-pair density $n_{\text{cp}} = 2 N(0)
\Delta_{\text{sc}}$, where $\Delta_{\text{sc}} = \Delta_{\rm NbN} =
2.6$~meV is the superconducting gap of the NbN electrodes of the
junctions ($2\Delta = 5.2$~meV, canonical value). At $T = 4$~K,
$\Delta_{\text{sc}}/\kB T \approx 7.5$ and
$x_{\text{qp}}^{\,\text{th}} \simeq 4.8\times 10^{-4}$.
Combined with the quasiparticle-tunneling rate
$1/T_1^{\text{qp}} = (\omega_{01}/\pi)\,x_{\text{qp}}\,
\sqrt{2\Delta_{\text{sc}}/\hbar\omega_{01}}$~\cite{Catelani2011QP},
for $\omega_{01}/2\pi = 5$~GHz this yields
\begin{equation}
T_1^{\text{qp,\,intrinsic}} \;\simeq\; \boxed{13~\text{ns}} \,.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Key Insight:} The intrinsic quasiparticle-limited coherence at
$4$~K is more than three orders of magnitude shorter than the operational
target set by the algorithms of Chapter~3. Reducing $x_{\text{qp}}$
below its thermal-equilibrium value is therefore a defining requirement
of the elevated-temperature design rather than an optional optimisation,
and the dedicated engineering intervention that delivers it is the
subject of the next paragraph.

\paragraph{Engineering pathway to the operational target.}
We adopt $T_1^{\text{qp}}\simeq 49\,\mu\text{s}$ as the operational
coherence-time target for the elevated-temperature qubit, compatible with
the gate fidelities required for the algorithms in Chapter~3. Reaching this
value at $T=4\,\text{K}$ requires reducing $x_{\text{qp}}$ below its
thermal-equilibrium value. Two engineering knobs are available:
gap-asymmetric junctions, which exponentially suppress the uphill
tunneling channel via $\exp[-(\Delta_1-\Delta_2)/\kB T]$ in the
non-equilibrium regime~\cite{Marchegiani2022}, and normal-metal traps on
both electrodes, which lower $x_{\text{qp}}$ in the steady state of the
underlying kinetic balance~\cite{Riwar2016}. A target-driven analysis of
the kinetic equation indicates that $T_1^{\text{qp}}\simeq 49\,\mu\text{s}$
is consistent with bilateral traps providing $s\sim 10^{7}\,\text{s}^{-1}$
on symmetric NbN/AlN/NbN junctions, a regime approximately one order of
magnitude beyond the trap rates demonstrated in aluminium-based
devices~\cite{Riwar2016}. Combining gap engineering with trap engineering
does not relax this requirement at 4\,K because of the larger thermal
quasiparticle population on the low-gap electrode. The realisation of
trap-engineered NbN qubits at this performance level remains an open
experimental question, and constitutes a natural extension of the work
presented here.

\subsubsection{Radiative Loss ($\propto \omega^2$)}

The qubit acts as an antenna, radiating electromagnetic energy. The loss rate scales as:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_{1,\text{rad}}} \propto \left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^2 A_{\text{eff}}
\label{eq:t1_radiative}
\end{equation}

Unshielded:
\begin{equation}
T_1^{\text{rad}}(300 \text{ GHz}) = T_1^{\text{rad}}(5.8 \text{ GHz}) \times \left(\frac{5.8}{300}\right)^2 = \boxed{0.37 \text{ }\mu\text{s}}
\end{equation}

This bare radiative lifetime is roughly two orders of magnitude shorter
than typical single-qubit gate durations
($\tau_{\text{1Q}} \sim \SI{20}{ns}$) and would by itself preclude any
coherent operation. Suppression through 3D cavity shielding, discussed
in the next paragraph, is therefore not an optional optimisation but a
structural requirement of the architecture.

With 3D cavity (60 dB shielding):
\begin{equation}
T_1^{\text{rad,shielded}} = 0.37 \times 10^{60/20} = \boxed{374 \text{ }\mu\text{s}}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Attention point:} A 3D cavity providing electromagnetic shielding is \textit{essential} for 300 GHz operation. This is not optional—it is a fundamental requirement.

\subsubsection{Two-Level System (TLS) Defects}

\begin{equation}
T_{1,\text{TLS}} \boxed{\sim 50 \text{ $\mu$s}}
\end{equation}

This contribution is similar for baseline and 300 GHz systems.

cavity), the Purcell channel is engineered to contribute
\begin{equation}
T_1^{\text{Purcell}} = \boxed{320 \text{ }\mu\text{s}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{$T_2$ Dephasing Analysis}

\subsubsection{Pure Dephasing Sources}

Pure dephasing (the $1/T_\phi$ term in Eq.~\ref{eq:t2_relation}) arises from several mechanisms.

\textbf{1. Charge Noise:} Exponentially suppressed in transmon regime:
\begin{equation}
T_{\phi,\text{charge}} \sim 1 \text{ ms} \quad (\text{negligible})
\end{equation}

\textbf{2. Flux Noise:} Dominant low-frequency noise source ($1/f$ noise):
\begin{equation}
T_{\phi,\text{flux}} \sim 20-50 \text{ $\mu$s}
\end{equation}
At the flux-insensitive sweet spot the first-order flux sensitivity vanishes; the residual pure dephasing (second-order flux noise plus readout-photon shot noise) is taken at $T_\phi = 23$~$\mu$s, within the window above:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\phi}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\boxed{T_2 = \TTwo \text{ }\mu\text{s}, \quad T_{2,\text{echo}} = \TTwoEcho \text{ }\mu\text{s}}
\end{equation}

The $T_2/T_1$ ratio is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{\TTwo}{\TOneTotal} = 0.86
\end{equation}

This good ratio ($> 0.5$) indicates that pure dephasing is not the dominant limitation. A Hahn echo refocuses the quasi-static ($1/f$-dominated) component of the dephasing, so that
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_{2,\text{echo}}} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \Gamma_{\phi}^{\text{echo}},
\qquad \Gamma_{\phi}^{\text{echo}} \ll \frac{1}{T_\phi}.
\end{equation}
Taking an echo suppression factor of $\approx 8$ for the $1/f$-dominated
dephasing ($T_\phi^{\text{echo}} \approx 190~\mu$s) gives
$T_{2,\text{echo}} \approx \TTwoEcho~\mu$s, approaching---but remaining
below---the relaxation ceiling $2T_1 \simeq 30.7~\mu$s.

the free-evolution estimate $2t_{\text{CNOT}}/T_2$. The error budget

\begin{align}

\end{align}

\varepsilon_{\text{total}} \approx 2.10\%, \quad \boxed{\mathcal{F}_{\text{CNOT}} \approx 97.90\%}
\end{equation}

The coherence budget alone would permit a ceiling of $\sim\FdecohCeiling\%$ ($1-\varepsilon_{\text{decoh}}$), so the residual gap to threshold is set primarily by the thermal population rather than by the coherence time.

\subsubsection{Gap to Threshold}

The surface code error correction threshold is typically $F_{\text{threshold}} \approx 98\%$. We have:
\begin{equation}
\Delta F = F_{\text{CNOT}} - F_{\text{threshold}} = 97.90\% - 98.00\% = -0.10\%
\end{equation}

The resulting CNOT fidelity at \SI{4}{K} therefore falls only $0.10$
percentage points below the surface-code threshold. This narrow margin is
within the recovery budget of standard error-mitigation techniques —
dynamical decoupling of idle qubits during multi-qubit operations,
DRAG-like pulse shaping to suppress leakage to the
$\lvert 2 \rangle$ state, and improved substrate engineering to reduce
dielectric loss are each independently expected to contribute
fidelity gains in the range $0.05$--$0.20$ percentage points, so that
combinations of two or more should plausibly close the budget without
redesigning the device. The next subsection enumerates these strategies
quantitatively.

\section{Frequency Trade-off at Elevated Temperature}
\label{sec:frequency_tradeoff}

\subsection{The Non-Monotonic Fidelity Problem}

At elevated temperatures ($T = \SI{4}{\kelvin}$) the choice of qubit frequency
is governed by two competing error sources. The thermal-excitation error,
$\varepsilon_{\text{th}} = \bar{n}_{\text{th}}/2$, falls exponentially with
frequency; the decoherence error accumulated during a gate,
$\varepsilon_{\text{dec}} = t_{\text{CNOT}}\,\Gamma_d$, grows approximately
linearly with it. Their sum is non-monotonic and admits an interior optimum.
This section derives that optimum, locates it quantitatively, and explains why
the design nevertheless operates at \SI{300}{\giga\hertz}, below the formal
optimum.

The analysis deliberately uses a \emph{simplified, dielectric-plus-thermal
model}: the only decay channel retained is dielectric loss, with the canonical
loss model of Sec.~\ref{sec:dielectric_loss_300ghz}
($Q_{\text{eff}} = 1/(p\tan\delta) \simeq 1.0\times10^{8}$ for $p = 0.1$ and
$\tan\delta = 10^{-7}$, the same inputs that yield the $53.1~\mu$s budget entry
of Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}), and the gate time is the canonical
$t_{\text{CNOT}} = \tCNOT$~ns. Radiative shielding, quasiparticle and TLS
channels, and pulse-level details are excluded in order to isolate the
frequency competition; the full design-point projections remain those of
Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity} ($F_{\text{CNOT}} \simeq \FCNOT\%$). All numbers and
figures in this section are generated by a single script
(\texttt{frequency\_tradeoff\_canonical.py} in the \texttt{qh\_hardware}
repository) from the canonical parameter set.

\subsection{Competing Physical Effects}

The dielectric loss rate at finite temperature is
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_d = \frac{\omega}{Q_{\text{eff}}} \left(1 + \bar{n}_{\text{th}}\right),
\label{eq:dielectric_loss_full}
\end{equation}
with $\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = [\exp(hf/k_BT) - 1]^{-1}$. The corresponding
dielectric-limited relaxation time is
\begin{equation}
T_1^{\text{diel}} = \frac{Q_{\text{eff}}}{\omega\,(1 + \bar{n}_{\text{th}})} .
\end{equation}

It is tempting to expect a maximum of $T_1^{\text{diel}}(f)$ at intermediate
frequency, from the competition between the linear factor $\omega$ and the
exponentially decaying $\bar{n}_{\text{th}}$. This expectation is incorrect.
Writing $x = hf/k_BT$ and $n = \bar{n}_{\text{th}}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{df}\!\left[f\,(1+n)\right] = (1+n)\,(1 - x\,n),
\qquad
x\,n = \frac{x}{e^{x}-1} < 1 \quad \forall\, x > 0,
\label{eq:monotonicity_proof}
\end{equation}
so $f(1+n)$ is strictly increasing and $T_1^{\text{diel}}(f)$ is
\emph{strictly decreasing} at every frequency, with a low-frequency plateau
$\Gamma_d \to 2\pi k_B T/(h\,Q_{\text{eff}})$ (equivalent to
$T_1^{\text{diel}} \to 197~\mu$s at \SI{4}{\kelvin} for the canonical
$Q_{\text{eff}}$) in the classical regime $x \ll 1$. There is therefore no
frequency that maximises the coherence time itself
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frequency_tradeoff}b); any apparent maximum in a finite scan is
an artefact of the scan window. The non-monotonic quantity is the gate
\emph{fidelity}, to which we now turn.

\subsection{Quantitative Analysis: the Fidelity Optimum}

The per-gate error in the simplified model is
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(f)
= \underbrace{t_{\text{CNOT}}\,\frac{\omega}{Q_{\text{eff}}}\,(1+\bar{n}_{\text{th}})}_{\text{decoherence}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bar{n}_{\text{th}}}_{\text{thermal}} .
\label{eq:eps_frequency_model}
\end{equation}
Setting $d\varepsilon/df = 0$ and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:monotonicity_proof} gives the transcendental optimum condition
\begin{equation}
\frac{t_{\text{CNOT}}\,\omega}{Q_{\text{eff}}}\,\bigl(1 - x\,\bar{n}_{\text{th}}\bigr)
= \frac{x\,\bar{n}_{\text{th}}}{2} ,
\label{eq:optimal_frequency_condition}
\end{equation}
which balances the linear growth of the decoherence term against the
exponential decay of the thermal term. For the canonical inputs the numerical
solution is
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{opt}} \simeq \SI{540}{\giga\hertz},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{\min} \simeq 0.58\%
\quad (F \simeq 99.4\%).
\end{equation}
The optimum is broad: every frequency between roughly \SI{400}{} and
\SI{800}{\giga\hertz} keeps $\varepsilon \leq 0.8\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frequency_tradeoff}c and
Table~\ref{tab:frequency_comparison}). Below $\sim$\SI{200}{\giga\hertz} the
thermal term dominates and the error grows steeply; a qubit at
\SI{50}{\giga\hertz} and \SI{4}{\kelvin} sits at $\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = 1.2$
and is thermally inoperable, its long dielectric $T_1$ notwithstanding.

\subsection{Comparison with Cryogenic Operation}

This behaviour contrasts with operation at \SI{20}{\milli\kelvin}, where
$\bar{n}_{\text{th}} \ll 1$ across all frequencies of interest. In this regime
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_d \approx \frac{\omega}{Q} \quad \text{(cryogenic limit)},
\end{equation}
the thermal error is negligible, and both $T_1$ and the fidelity improve
monotonically as the frequency is \emph{lowered}: the optimal cryogenic
strategy is to operate at the lowest practical frequency
($\sim$\SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\giga\hertz}), as conventional transmon platforms do.
At \SI{4}{\kelvin} this intuition fails, and the optimum moves to the sub-THz
regime.

\subsection{Why \SI{300}{\giga\hertz}: Descending from the Formal Optimum}
\label{subsec:why_300ghz}

The formal optimum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:optimal_frequency_condition} lies at
$\simeq \SI{540}{\giga\hertz}$; the design nevertheless targets
\SI{300}{\giga\hertz}. The fidelity cost of this choice is modest---
$\varepsilon$ rises from $0.58\%$ to $1.69\%$, about $1.1$ percentage
points, and the full budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity} remains within reach of
the surface-code threshold---while the feasibility cost of climbing towards
the optimum grows rapidly:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Junction capacitance.} At fixed $E_J/E_C = 50$ the charging energy
scales linearly with frequency, so the total shunt capacitance scales as
$C_\Sigma \propto 1/\omega$: from $1.29$~fF at \SI{300}{\giga\hertz} to
$0.72$~fF at \SI{540}{\giga\hertz}. Since the junction self-capacitance budget
of Sec.~\ref{sec:junction_design} tightens as
$c_s/J_c \leq C_\Sigma/I_c \propto 1/\omega^{2}$, moving to the optimum would
harden the already binding fabrication constraint by roughly a factor of
three.
\item \emph{Control electronics.} \SI{300}{\giga\hertz} is reachable with
established millimetre-wave multiplier and mixer chains; the
\SI{450}{}--\SI{550}{\giga\hertz} range pushes into markedly less mature
territory for phase-coherent pulsed control at cryogenic interfaces
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:control-electronics}).
\item \emph{Pair-breaking margin.} The qubit frequency must remain well below
the pair-breaking threshold $2\Delta_{\text{gap}}/h \approx 1.26$~THz of the
NbN electrodes (canonical $2\Delta = 5.2$~meV); at \SI{540}{\giga\hertz} the
second harmonic of the drive already reaches $86\%$ of the gap, eroding the
margin that protects the quasiparticle budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}.
\item \emph{Loss-tangent uncertainty.} The $\tan\delta$ values entering
$Q_{\text{eff}}$ are extrapolated from $1$--$30$~GHz cryogenic data
(Sec.~\ref{sec:dielectric_loss_300ghz}); the extrapolation uncertainty grows
with frequency, and within the factor-of-three band the optimum itself moves
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frequency_tradeoff}d). Anchoring the design at
\SI{300}{\giga\hertz} keeps it on the steep, thermally-dominated side of the
curve, where the location of the optimum is insensitive to $\tan\delta$.
\end{itemize}

For context at the opposite end of the frequency axis, Anferov
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{Anferov2024HighTemp} demonstrated operation up to
\SI{250}{\milli\kelvin} with qubits up to \SI{24}{\giga\hertz}; at
\SI{4}{\kelvin}, frequencies of that order sit deep in the thermally dominated
regime (Table~\ref{tab:frequency_comparison}), which is why the present design
requires the sub-THz range. In summary, \SI{300}{\giga\hertz} is not the
unconstrained optimum of the error model; it is the knee of the
feasibility-adjusted curve, where most of the thermal suppression has been
banked at a fabrication and control cost that Sec.~\ref{sec:challenges}
assesses as demanding but tractable.

\subsection{In-Situ Frequency Tunability of the Asymmetric SQUID}
\label{subsec:insitu_tunability}

The asymmetric SQUID offers two flux-insensitive sweet spots per flux period,
at $E_J(\Phi{=}0) = E_\Sigma$ and
$E_J(\Phi{=}\Phi_0/2) = |E_\Delta| = \eta\,E_\Sigma$,
\begin{equation}
E_J(\Phi) = \sqrt{E_\Sigma^2 \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right)
+ E_\Delta^2 \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}\right)} ,
\end{equation}
so that, in the harmonic limit $\omega \propto \sqrt{E_J}$, the in-situ
frequency ratio between the two protected points is
$\omega_{\text{upper}}/\omega_{\text{lower}} \approx \eta^{-1/2}$. For the
reference asymmetry $\eta = 11.1\%$ (Tab.~\ref{tab:squid_design_params}) this
is a factor $\approx 3$---e.g.\ a \SI{300}{\giga\hertz} upper sweet spot paired
with a lower one near \SI{95}{\giga\hertz}.

Two limitations bound what this tunability can and cannot do at
\SI{4}{\kelvin}. First, a ``dual-band'' scheme that stores at
\SI{300}{\giga\hertz} and gates at $\sim$\SI{10}{\giga\hertz} would require
$\eta \approx (10/300)^2 \sim 10^{-3}$, an asymmetry so extreme that the lower
sweet spot loses the finite Josephson energy and anharmonicity that make it
usable---precisely the feature the architecture is built on. Second, and more
fundamentally, the corrected error model of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_frequency_model} shows that a gate executed at
\SI{10}{}--\SI{20}{\giga\hertz} and \SI{4}{\kelvin} would sit at
$\bar{n}_{\text{th}} \approx 4$--$10$, i.e.\ in a thermally depolarised
regime: low-frequency gating at elevated temperature is not merely
suboptimal, it is inoperable. Since \SI{300}{\giga\hertz} is simultaneously
adequate for storage ($\varepsilon_{\text{th}} = 1.41\%$) and for gates
($\varepsilon_{\text{dec}} \leq 0.58\%$ in the full budget), fixed-frequency
operation at the upper sweet spot is the design choice, and no frequency
scheduling is required.

The factor-$\approx 3$ tunability retains genuine, more modest uses: material
and junction spectroscopy at reduced frequency, where control electronics are
simpler; avoidance of TLS collisions by small flux excursions around either
sweet spot; and the fact that both endpoints are first-order flux-insensitive,
so this diagnostic flexibility comes without a dephasing penalty at the
operating points.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Alternative Platforms}

The location of the optimum scales with the quality factor: within the
factor-of-three $\tan\delta$ uncertainty band, $f_{\text{opt}}$ moves between
$\sim$\SI{450}{} and \SI{540}{\giga\hertz} and the design-point error spans
$1.7$--$2.2\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:frequency_tradeoff}d). The dedicated cryogenic
sub-THz substrate characterisation already identified as a hardware
prerequisite (Sec.~\ref{sec:dielectric_loss_300ghz}) would directly sharpen
this plot and is, in this sense, the single most informative measurement for
the frequency choice. Protected low-frequency architectures (fluxonium,
$0$--$\pi$ qubits) are attractive at millikelvin temperatures but do not evade
the thermal term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_frequency_model}: at \SI{4}{\kelvin} any
single-mode qubit below $\sim$\SI{100}{\giga\hertz} inherits
$\bar{n}_{\text{th}} \gtrsim 0.4$, so the trade-off analysed here applies to
the platform class, not to the transmon specifically.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Results}

At $T = \SI{4}{\kelvin}$ the dielectric-limited $T_1$ decreases monotonically
with frequency, but the gate fidelity does not: the exponential suppression of
the thermal-excitation error against the linear growth of the decoherence
error produces a broad fidelity optimum at
$f_{\text{opt}} \simeq \SI{540}{\giga\hertz}$
($\varepsilon_{\min} \simeq 0.58\%$). The \SI{300}{\giga\hertz} design point
concedes about $1.1$ percentage points of error relative to this optimum in
exchange for substantially relaxed junction, control-electronics and
pair-breaking constraints, and sits where the conclusion is robust to the
dominant model uncertainty (the sub-THz loss tangent). Within this picture,
single-frequency operation at the upper flux-insensitive sweet spot is
sufficient: the frequency that protects the qubit thermally is also a
frequency at which it can be operated.

\subsection{Challenge 1: Frequency-Dependent Material Losses}

The 52-fold increase in operating frequency from the baseline 5.8~GHz to 300~GHz results in unfavorable scaling of loss mechanisms. Dielectric losses scale as $T_{1,\text{diel}} \propto 1/\omega$, leading to a 52-fold increase in loss rate, while radiative losses scale even more dramatically as $T_{1,\text{rad}} \propto 1/\omega^2$, resulting in a catastrophic 2675-fold increase for unshielded configurations:
\begin{align}
T_{1,\text{diel}} &\propto \frac{1}{\omega} \quad \Rightarrow \quad 52\times \text{ worse} \\
T_{1,\text{rad}} &\propto \frac{1}{\omega^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2675\times \text{ worse (unshielded)}
\end{align}
This challenge represents the highest severity risk, with the potential to reduce $T_1$ below 5~$\mu$s—a regime incompatible with quantum computing. However, the probability of failure can be reduced to approximately 30\% through comprehensive mitigation strategies.

The first mitigation approach employs ultra-low-loss substrates such as sapphire with loss tangents below $10^{-7}$, or high-resistivity silicon. By minimising the dielectric participation ratio through 3D cavity designs, the expected dielectric-limited coherence time can exceed 300~$\mu$s. The second critical mitigation is the implementation of a 3D electromagnetic cavity—an essential requirement rather than an optional enhancement. Full electromagnetic shielding using copper or aluminium cavities provides approximately 60~dB suppression of radiative losses, achieved through quarter-wave or half-wave coaxial cavity geometries. This approach is expected to yield radiative-limited coherence times exceeding 400~$\mu$s. The third mitigation strategy focuses on reducing the dielectric participation ratio by maximising capacitance to vacuum rather than to dielectric materials. A 3D architecture naturally achieves participation ratios below 0.1, further enhanced by implementing air-bridge capacitor structures.

With all three mitigation strategies implemented, the success probability for maintaining adequate coherence times increases to approximately 70\%. Figure~\ref{fig:challenges}(a) illustrates the comparative scaling of loss mechanisms between the baseline 5.8~GHz system and the proposed 300~GHz system, highlighting both the challenges and the effectiveness of the mitigation approaches.

\subsection{Challenge 2: Josephson Junction Fabrication}

The high operating frequency necessitates Josephson junctions of small physical size, coupled with stringent requirements on critical current ($I_c = 1.51$~$\mu$A, derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:junction_design}), uniformity (less than 5\% variation), and current density ($J_c \simeq 6$~kA/cm$^2$, Sec.~\ref{sec:junction_capacitance_point}). While this represents a medium-severity challenge, the fabrication is achievable with specialised facilities, with an estimated 30\% probability of failure primarily due to facility access and process complexity.

Advanced lithography techniques form the foundation of the mitigation strategy. E-beam lithography provides the necessary 10~nm resolution, implemented through the Dolan bridge shadow evaporation technique. Multiple test fabrication runs allow for process optimisation and yield improvement. Achieving the required current density demands thin AlN barriers of approximately $1.3$~nm thickness, deposited by precisely controlled reactive sputtering or atomic layer deposition (ALD) for enhanced uniformity~\cite{Wang2026ALDNitride}.

Junction arrays may optionally be used to improve yield and uniformity, although a single junction of $\sim 180$~nm already meets both the critical-current and capacitance targets (Sec.~\ref{sec:junction_capacitance_point}), so an array is not required. Additionally, the asymmetric SQUID architecture provides intrinsic flux tunability, allowing $\pm 5\%$ frequency tuning to compensate for fabrication variations. This multi-pronged approach yields a success probability of 70--80\% when implemented in a specialised fabrication facility. Figure~\ref{fig:challenges}(b) compares different substrate options, while Figure~\ref{fig:system_parameters}(c) illustrates the junction sizing considerations.

\paragraph{Current Density Engineering and Facility Requirements}

The current density of Josephson tunnel junctions depends on the barrier properties according to:
\begin{equation}
J_c \propto \exp\left(-\frac{d_{\text{ox}}}{\lambda}\right)
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{ox}}$ is the barrier thickness and $\lambda \approx 0.3$~nm is the characteristic decay length~\cite{Wang2026ALDNitride}. Achieving $J_c \simeq 6$~kA/cm$^2$ demands:

\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
AlN barrier thickness $d_{\text{ox}}$ & $\sim 1.3$~nm \\

These specifications are achievable at specialised facilities, including MIT Lincoln Laboratory, IBM Quantum, and NIST Boulder, which possess the necessary e-beam lithography systems with 10~nm resolution and in-situ process monitoring capabilities. While advanced techniques such as extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography could potentially enable even smaller junction geometries in future iterations, current e-beam capabilities are amply sufficient for the $\sim$180~nm junction diameters required by our 300~GHz design (Table~\ref{tab:junction_sizes}).

\subsection{Challenge 3: 300~GHz Microwave Control}

The implementation of quantum control at 300~GHz faces challenges related to the limited commercial availability of appropriate sources, amplifiers, low-noise cryogenic components, and waveguide transition elements. This medium-severity challenge increases system complexity, though the probability of failure is relatively low (approximately 20\%) given that the underlying technology exists and has been demonstrated in related fields.

A key insight is that the required 300~GHz technology has already been developed and matured for millimeter-wave astronomy applications. The ALMA telescope project has established 300~GHz technology as a reliable standard, with commercial suppliers such as Virginia Diodes Inc. providing multiplier chains extending to 1~THz. The WR-3 waveguide standard (220--325~GHz) is well-established and widely available. This existing infrastructure significantly de-risks the development.

An alternative approach involves frequency conversion, where control pulses are generated at more accessible frequencies around 10~GHz and subsequently upconverted through on-chip frequency multiplication (factor of approximately 30) using Josephson parametric amplifiers. The combination of mature existing technology and alternative implementation pathways results in a success probability of 80--90\%.

\paragraph{On-Chip Frequency Conversion Architecture}

An alternative implementation strategy that may further reduce system complexity employs on-chip frequency conversion. In this hybrid architecture, control pulses are generated at more accessible intermediate frequencies around 10~GHz using conventional microwave sources, then upconverted to 300~GHz locally on the chip itself. This approach leverages Josephson mixers and parametric amplifiers, which can provide the necessary $\times 30$ frequency multiplication while maintaining quantum-limited noise performance. The on-chip conversion reduces requirements on room-temperature control electronics, but introduces additional on-chip complexity and requires careful management of spurious mixing products and intermodulation distortion.

\subsection{Challenge 4: Thermal Management at 4~K}

Operating at 4~K rather than the conventional 20~mK introduces requirements for temperature stability below 1~mK, thermal isolation of the qubit chip, and maintenance of a low-noise environment. However, this challenge represents the lowest severity risk, as the engineering principles are well-understood and the technology is mature, resulting in less than 10\% probability of failure.

Modern pulse-tube coolers, such as the Cryomech PT420, provide 1~W of cooling power at 4~K with temperature stability better than 0.5~mK. Vibration isolation is achieved through passive damping systems that prevent mechanical noise from coupling to the qubit. Multi-stage thermal filtering at 40~K, 10~K, and 4~K, combined with copper braid thermalisation and Eccosorb microwave filters, ensures both thermal and electromagnetic isolation of the qubit environment. The maturity and reliability of these technologies result in a success probability exceeding 90\%.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary_of_findings}

This chapter has analysed asymmetric SQUID transmon qubits in two operating regimes: a baseline configuration at conventional cryogenic temperatures (qubit frequency $5.8$~GHz, $T = 20$~mK) and an elevated-temperature configuration ($300$~GHz, $4$~K). The principal results, established quantitatively in Sections~\ref{sec:thermal_analysis}--\ref{sec:challenges}, can be summarised as follows.

\subsubsection*{Technical feasibility of the elevated-temperature regime}

At $4$~K with a qubit frequency of $300$~GHz, the dimensionless ratio $\hbar\omega_{01}/(k_B T) = 3.6$ keeps the device in the quantum regime, with a thermal population of $\bar{n} \simeq 2.81\%$. The coherence-time estimates derived from the dielectric- and radiative-loss budget of Section~\ref{sec:decoherence} yield $T_1 \simeq \TOneTotal~\mu$s and $T_2 \simeq \TTwo~\mu$s (with $T_{2,\text{echo}} \simeq \TTwoEcho~\mu$s under Hahn-echo refocusing). The quasiparticle contribution to this budget assumes the trap-engineered configuration discussed in Section~\ref{sec:decoherence}, with $T_1^{\text{qp}}$ at the target value of $\sim 49~\mu$s rather than the bare thermal-floor value of $\sim 20$~ns. Combining these decoherence rates with the gate-fidelity model of Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}, and adopting the echo cross-resonance protocol of Section~\ref{sec:gate_strategy}, we project a single-qubit fidelity of $\Fsingle\%$ and a two-qubit fidelity of $\FCNOT\%$. The latter sits approximately $0.1$ percentage points below the $98\%$ surface-code threshold adopted as a working reference; the gap and the candidate mechanisms that could close it are discussed below. A compact view of these indicators is collected in Table~\ref{tab:tech_feasibility_4K}.

\subsubsection*{Baseline validation}

The baseline configuration at $5.8$~GHz and $20$~mK reaches a CNOT fidelity of $98.7\%$, consistent with values reported for state-of-the-art transmon platforms based on comparable junction technology~\cite{krantz2019, kim2021}. Since the same modelling framework yields both this baseline number and the elevated-temperature projections discussed above, the agreement at $20$~mK provides a controlled reference against which the $4$~K extrapolations may be assessed.

\subsubsection*{Identified challenges and mitigation strategies}

Four technological challenges have been examined in detail in Section~\ref{sec:challenges}, each accompanied by a candidate mitigation strategy. Dielectric and radiative losses, which dominate the coherence budget at high frequency, can be addressed through ultra-low-loss substrates combined with three-dimensional cavity geometries. Josephson-junction fabrication at the required critical-current density is achievable with electron-beam lithography and Dolan-bridge shadow evaporation. Coherent control at $300$~GHz can leverage the millimetre-wave technology developed for radio astronomy and atmospheric science. Thermal management at $4$~K is supported by commercial pulse-tube coolers with sub-mK stability. A summary mapping is given in Table~\ref{tab:tech_challenges_addressable}. Combining the per-challenge probabilities of success of Section~\ref{sec:challenges} as independent multiplicative factors yields an overall technical success probability in the range $10$--$15\%$.

\subsubsection*{Resource and infrastructure considerations}

Operation at $4$~K rather than $20$~mK has direct implications for the infrastructure required to host the device. Replacing dilution refrigeration with a compact closed-cycle cryocooler shortens cool-down times from days to hours, increasing the rate at which fabrication--test iterations can be performed, and the accessibility of the $4$~K stage is compatible with cryogenic CMOS control electronics co-located with the qubit chip, reducing wiring length and control latency. These are operational consequences of the elevated-temperature design; a quantitative cost analysis is deferred to dedicated future work.

Taken together, the results of this chapter outline a coherent design space for the elevated-temperature regime. The baseline configuration confirms that the asymmetric SQUID architecture, the loss models, and the gate-fidelity estimators adopted here are calibrated against established transmon performance, while the elevated-temperature configuration extends the same modelling framework to an operating point that, to our knowledge, has not previously been analysed at this level of detail.

The principal limitation is the gap of approximately $0.1$ percentage points between the projected two-qubit fidelity at $4$~K and the $98\%$ surface-code threshold used as reference. Several improvements that lie within the reach of current research directions could plausibly close this gap: lower-loss substrates and junction technologies, which directly raise $T_1$ and $T_2$; pulse-shaping schemes such as DRAG-like protocols, which reduce leakage and control-induced infidelity; and refined coupler designs that mitigate the residual cross-Kerr at elevated temperatures. None of these is uniquely required by the present design; their combined effect is, however, only loosely constrained by the present analysis, and quantitative confirmation requires fabrication and measurement.

The four-phase outline of Section~\ref{sec:challenges} should accordingly be read as a logical sequencing of the experimental questions raised by the chapter rather than as an industrial roadmap: a baseline demonstration of NbN/AlN/NbN transmons at conventional cryogenic conditions, an extension to intermediate frequencies (a few tens of GHz at temperatures of $1$--$2$~K), and only afterwards the full $300$~GHz / $4$~K target. Each step probes a distinct subset of the modelling assumptions on which the elevated-temperature projections rely, so the progression is informative regardless of where it eventually stops.

In this perspective, the simulation results presented here define a quantitative starting point for an experimental investigation of the asymmetric SQUID transmon outside the millikelvin regime; whether and how to pursue that investigation is a question that lies beyond the scope of the present thesis.


\section{Wrap-up and Final Considerations}

\subsubsection*{Baseline configuration}

In the baseline regime (qubit frequency $5$--$6$~GHz, $T \in [10,20]$~mK), asymmetric SQUID transmons reach a CNOT fidelity of $98.7\%$ with thermal occupation below $10^{-5}\%$. This figure matches values reported for transmon devices employing comparable junction technology~\cite{krantz2019,kim2021}; in the present context, the agreement provides a controlled reference against which the elevated-temperature projections can be assessed.

\subsubsection*{Elevated-temperature configuration}

At $300$~GHz with $T \in [4,10]$~K the same architecture maintains thermal populations in the $3$--$10\%$ range and yields projected single- and two-qubit fidelities above the surface-code threshold considered in Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity}, modulo the residual gap of $\sim 0.1$ percentage points discussed above. The configuration thus defines a design point at which the simulation framework predicts viable transmon operation outside the dilution-refrigerator regime. Whether the same regime can be reached experimentally is a question that the present work formulates quantitatively but does not, by itself, answer.

\subsubsection*{Materials and integration context}

State-of-the-art superconducting qubits based on Al/AlO$_x$/Al junctions operated at $10$--$20$~mK in dilution refrigerators have reached coherence times exceeding $300~\mu$s~\cite{krantz2019, place2021}, the present benchmark against which any elevated-temperature design will be assessed. The architecture analysed here adopts epitaxial NbN/AlN/NbN junctions on sapphire~\cite{nakamura2011epitaxialNbN, kim2021}, which have been demonstrated at conventional cryogenic temperatures with $T_1 \approx 16~\mu$s and $T_c \approx 16$~K; the present modelling extends those materials to a $4$--$10$~K operating window, where compact cryocoolers replace the dilution refrigerator. Whether the lower thermal budget at this stage of the cryogenic stack can be exploited to co-integrate the control electronics in the cryo-CMOS technology developed for sub-Kelvin work~\cite{Acharya2022, VanWinckel2022, Underwood2024} is a separate engineering question; here we note only that the elevated-temperature operating point is compatible with such an integration in principle, and we do not attempt a quantitative resource accounting.

In summary, the chapter has developed a model of an asymmetric SQUID transmon based on NbN/AlN/NbN junctions, characterised its baseline behaviour at $10$--$20$~mK against established transmon performance, and used the same modelling framework to examine an elevated-temperature operating point at $300$~GHz and $4$~K. The projections at the elevated-temperature operating point sit close to the gate-fidelity range required by the algorithms of Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_algorithms}; whether they survive a fabrication and measurement campaign is the open experimental question on which the practical interest of the design depends.

\subsection{Technological prerequisites for high-fidelity 4~K operation}
\label{subsec:tech_prereq_4K}

The cross-resonance protocol of \S\ref{sec:gate_strategy} reduces the
two-qubit gate fidelity question to a clear set of materials and engineering
requirements. We identify here, with quantitative targets, the six
prerequisites for reaching $F_{\text{avg}} \ge 99\%$ on the elevated-temperature
operating point ($\omega_q\!\sim\!300$~GHz, $T = 4$~K).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapters/qh_figures/cross_resonance_sweetspot_map_4K.png}
    \caption{Cross-resonance gate at $T = 4$~K, with the qubit--qubit detuning
    $\Delta_q$ independently optimised at each point.
    \textbf{(Left)} Achievable average gate fidelity $F_{CR}$ as a function of
    coherence time $T_1$ and qubit frequency $\omega_q$. The solid contour at
    $F_{CR} = 99\%$ defines the boundary of the operational sweet spot.
    The blue star marks the operating point of this work
    ($T_1 = 50~\mu$s, $\omega_q = 300$~GHz), which sits between the $99.5\%$ and
    $99.9\%$ contours.
    \textbf{(Right)} Optimal gate time $t_{\rm gate}^{\rm opt}$ as a function
    of $\omega_q$. The optimum over $\Delta_q$ is $T_1$-independent by
    construction (the gate time is set by $\Omega_{ZX}$, which does not depend
    on $T_1$), so the dependence is one-dimensional. The gate time grows
    approximately linearly with $\omega_q$, from $\sim\!68$~ns at $250$~GHz to
    $\sim\!130$~ns at $500$~GHz; at the operating point it is $\sim\!79$~ns,
    safely within the fast-gate region ($t_{\rm gate} < 100$~ns).}
    \label{fig:sweetspot_4K}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{P1: Thermal occupation.}
The qubit frequency must satisfy
$\omega_q \gtrsim 250$~GHz so that
$\bar{n}_{\text{th}}(\omega_q,4~\text{K}) < 0.05$. At $\omega_q = 300$~GHz
the thermal population is $\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = 2.8\%$, marginal but
acceptable provided that active reset is employed at the start of every
circuit~\cite{blais2021circuit}.

\paragraph{P2: Dielectric loss tangent.}
At $\omega_q = 300$~GHz, achieving $T_1 \ge 25~\mu$s
(the threshold for $F_{\text{avg}}\ge 99\%$, see Fig.~\ref{fig:sweetspot_4K})
requires
$\tan\delta \le 1/(\omega_q T_1) \simeq 2\!\times\!10^{-8}$.
This figure is at the optimistic end of state-of-the-art
microwave dielectric measurements
(\cite{Krupka1999Sapphire, Wang2015, Read2023Sapphire}, all reporting
$\tan\delta\sim 10^{-8}$ for ultra-pure single-crystal silicon and
sapphire), but is not unreasonable given the sub-mK $\tan\delta$ values
achieved in the bulk-niobium 3D cavities of
Ref.~\cite{Romanenko2020}. A direct experimental verification of $\tan\delta$
at sub-THz frequencies in 4~K cavities is, to our knowledge, an open question
and is identified here as a key prerequisite.

\paragraph{P3: Junction superconducting gap above $\hbar\omega_q$.}
Direct pair-breaking by the qubit photon is energetically forbidden when
$\hbar\omega_q < 2\Delta_{\text{gap}}$. The NbN/AlN/NbN
junctions of Refs.~\cite{nakamura2011epitaxialNbN, Delahaye2015} have
$2\Delta_{\text{gap}}/h \approx 1.26$~THz (canonical $2\Delta = 5.2$~meV), well above $\omega_q = 300$~GHz,
so this channel is closed at the operating frequency.  The
complementary contribution from the thermally-populated quasiparticle
density---which is \emph{not} suppressed by the
$\hbar\omega_q / 2\Delta_{\text{gap}}$ ratio and constitutes the dominant
non-radiative channel at $T\gtrsim 1$~K---is addressed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:decoherence}, where trap engineering is identified as the
route compatible with the operational $T_1^{\text{qp}}$ target.
This places the NbN junction technology, together with the trap-engineering
strategy outlined there, at the centre of the elevated-temperature design.

\paragraph{P4: Stretched-CR detuning regime.}
Following \S\ref{subsec:dq_optimization}, the optimal qubit--qubit detuning
is $\Delta_q \approx 600$~MHz, corresponding to qubit
frequencies $\omega_{q,1}/2\pi = 300.30$~GHz and
$\omega_{q,2}/2\pi = 299.70$~GHz. This stretched-detuning regime
keeps the prefactor in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:omega_ZX} large; spectral addressability with single-qubit
pulses of duration $\tau\!\sim\!20$~ns is preserved, since $\Delta_q$ exceeds
the pulse bandwidth $\sim 50$~MHz by an order of magnitude.

\paragraph{P5: Echo-CR pulse engineering.}
The echo-CR sequence~\cite{Sheldon2016}, complemented by Gaussian-Flat-Gaussian
shaping with DRAG correction~\cite{Motzoi2009, Gambetta2011},
brings the gate fidelity from the bare-Hamiltonian
$\sim 80\%$ to the surface-code-compatible $\gtrsim 99\%$ (see
Table~\ref{tab:fidelity_budget}). All necessary techniques are routinely
employed in current transmon processors at GHz frequencies; their
extension to sub-THz drives is a control-electronics challenge addressed in
\S\ref{subsec:control-electronics}.

\paragraph{P6: Cavity quality factor and Purcell control at 4~K.}
The Purcell limit~\cite{Schuster2005PRL} on $T_1$ is the binding cavity
requirement (Sec.~\ref{sec:purcell}): sustaining the budgeted
$T_{1,\text{Purcell}} = 320~\mu$s at $\Delta_1/2\pi = \InnDeltaOne$~GHz
requires an effective quality factor $Q_{\text{eff}} \gtrsim 4\times10^{6}$
at the qubit frequency, well beyond the $Q_0 \sim 10^4$--$10^5$ projected in
Sec.~\ref{sec:cavity_integration_300ghz} for bare sub-THz cavities. The
design therefore assumes a Purcell filter providing $\geq 16$~dB (target
20~dB) of stop-band suppression at $\omega_q$, which relaxes the bare-cavity
requirement to $Q_L \sim 10^4$--$10^5$ for the readout bandwidth. High-purity
bulk-Nb fabrication~\cite{Romanenko2020} meets the bare-cavity requirement at
GHz frequencies, but neither such quality factors nor Purcell filters have
been demonstrated at $\omega \gtrsim 100$~GHz and $T = 4$~K. This represents
the second key open experimental question, in addition to P2.

\medskip

In summary, the high-temperature operation of the present design is shown to be feasible
within a sweet spot whose boundary is defined by quantitatively explicit and
independently testable engineering targets (P1--P6). Two prerequisites (P2 and
P6) require dedicated experimental validation at sub-THz frequencies, while
the others (P1, P3, P4, P5) draw on technology already demonstrated at lower
frequencies. The path to a high-fidelity universal gate set at 4~K, while
challenging, does not invoke any speculative physics: it is a problem of
materials and pulse engineering whose components have, individually, been
demonstrated.
