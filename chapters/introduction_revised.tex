\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Quantum technologies (QTs) leverage uniquely quantum resources--superposition, entanglement, and measurement back-action--to surpass classical limits in computing, sensing, communication, and simulation. The maturity of enabling hardware (superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photonics, spin defects) and of full software stacks has ushered in a second wave of quantum innovation.

  \centering

  \caption{Adapted from: Dowling \& Milburn, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A (2003); Deutsch, PRX Quantum (2020). From observation to engineering of quantum phenomena. The first quantum revolution (1900--2000) leveraged quantum mechanics for passive devices (transistors, lasers), while the second quantum revolution (2000--present) actively engineers quantum coherence and entanglement for computational and sensing advantages.}
  \label{fig:revolutions}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:revolutions} illustrates the conceptual shift from passive observation of quantum phenomena (first quantum revolution: transistors, lasers, MRI) to active engineering of quantum coherence and entanglement for computational and sensing advantages (second quantum revolution: quantum computers, quantum sensors, quantum networks). This paradigm shift drives contemporary research across academia, industry, and government laboratories worldwide.

This thesis explores quantum technologies along two complementary axes: \textbf{quantum computing} for more cost-effective  hardware platforms and enhanced algorithmic performance through quantum-classical hybridization, and \textbf{quantum sensing} for ultra-precise measurements beyond classical limits. While these domains are often studied in isolation, they share common foundational challenges that motivate the integrated approach adopted in this work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System modeling and control}: Understanding and optimizing quantum dynamics in realistic, noisy environments remains a central challenge. Practical quantum systems operate in the presence of decoherence, thermal fluctuations, and control imperfections that must be accurately modeled to predict performance.
    
    \item \textbf{Hardware-algorithm co-design}: Bridging theoretical capabilities with near-term hardware constraints requires careful consideration of qubit connectivity, gate fidelities, coherence times, and available quantum resources. Algorithms and sensing protocols must be tailored to the specific strengths and limitations of available platforms.
    
    \item \textbf{Quantum advantage characterization}: Identifying regimes where quantum approaches genuinely outperform classical counterparts---rather than simply demonstrating quantum behavior---is essential for justifying the significant resource investment required for quantum technology deployment.
\end{itemize}

These challenges are addressed through \textbf{theoretical modeling and numerical simulation} across three interconnected research contributions, spanning from individual qubit control to collective atomic sensing and hybrid quantum-classical machine learning architectures. By adopting a simulation-driven methodology, this thesis systematically explores parameter spaces and design trade-offs before committing to costly experimental implementations, while providing predictive guidance for future hardware deployments.

\section{Background: The Quantum Computing Landscape}
\label{sec:qcomp}

\subsection{Physical Platforms and Qubit Realizations}

Quantum computation encodes information in two-level quantum systems (qubits) that can exist in superpositions of $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, manipulated via unitary gates, and measured to extract classical outcomes~\cite{NielsenChuang2011}. Leading qubit platforms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Superconducting circuits}: Josephson-junction-based nonlinear oscillators (transmon, fluxonium, capacitively shunted flux) operating at 10--50~mK in dilution refrigerators, with nanosecond gate times and microsecond coherence~\cite{krantz2019,kjaergaard2020}. Major implementations include IBM, Google, Rigetti, IQM, and academic groups.
    
    \item \textbf{Trapped ions}: Hyperfine or optical transitions in $^{171}$Yb$^+$, $^{40}$Ca$^+$, $^{137}$Ba$^+$ ions confined by radiofrequency or optical fields, achieving millisecond coherence and 99.9\% gate fidelities~\cite{Bruzewicz2019}. Leading efforts include IonQ, Quantinuum, and Alpine Quantum Technologies.
    
    \item \textbf{Neutral atoms}: Rydberg-blockade gates in optical tweezers or lattices (QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing), offering flexible connectivity and mid-circuit readout.
    
    \item \textbf{Photonic systems}: Linear-optical or measurement-based approaches (Xanadu, PsiQuantum), promising room-temperature operation but facing challenges in deterministic entanglement generation.
    
    \item \textbf{Spin qubits}: Silicon quantum dots or donors (Intel, Diraq), offering semiconductor fabrication compatibility and compact footprints, though requiring sub-Kelvin temperatures and facing readout fidelity challenges.
\end{itemize}

Each platform presents distinct trade-offs in gate speed, coherence time, qubit connectivity, scalability, and operating temperature. Superconducting qubits, the focus of Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_hardware}, combine fast gates with mature fabrication processes but demand millikelvin operation and face limited coherence.

\subsection{Quantum Gates, Circuits, and Algorithms}

Universal quantum computation requires single-qubit rotations and entangling two-qubit gates. For superconducting qubits, single-qubit gates ($R_x$, $R_y$, $R_z$) are implemented via resonant microwave pulses, while two-qubit gates (e.g. CNOT, CZ, iSWAP) exploit dispersive or resonant qubit-qubit coupling through shared cavities or direct capacitive links. Gate compilation optimizes pulse shapes (Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate -- DRAG --, optimal control~\cite{Motzoi2009}) to suppress leakage to non-computational states and minimize gate times relative to coherence.

Quantum algorithms span:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fault-tolerant algorithms}: Shor's factoring and Grover's search require error-corrected logical qubits with $\sim 10^{-15}$ error rates, demanding millions of physical qubits under surface code overhead.
    
    \item \textbf{NISQ algorithms}: Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)~\cite{Peruzzo2014}, Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)~\cite{Farhi2014}, and quantum machine learning operate on $\mathcal{O}(10^2)$ noisy qubits, using classical-quantum hybrid loops to circumvent error accumulation~\cite{Preskill2018,cerezo2021}.
\end{itemize}

The NISQ era emphasizes near-term utility through variational approaches, as explored in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_algorithms} via hybrid quantum-classical convolutional neural networks (see Fig.~\ref{fig:qcnn_hybrid_architecture})

\subsection{Decoherence, Error Correction, and Scalability}

Qubit coherence is limited by two fundamental decoherence mechanisms: energy 
relaxation and dephasing. These processes are quantified by characteristic 
time scales $T_1$ and $T_2$, which determine the practical fidelity and 
duration of quantum operations.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Energy Relaxation Time ($T_1$).} The relaxation time $T_1$ 
characterizes the exponential decay of an excited qubit state $|1\rangle$ to 
the ground state $|0\rangle$ through energy dissipation to the environment. 
If a qubit is initialized in the excited state, its population evolves as
\begin{equation}
P_1(t) = e^{-t/T_1},
\end{equation}
where $P_1(t)$ is the probability of finding the qubit in $|1\rangle$ at 
time~$t$. Energy relaxation represents an irreversible loss of quantum 
information, as the system exchanges energy with its surroundings—typically 
through coupling to electromagnetic modes of the control circuitry, substrate 
phonons, or quasiparticle excitations in the superconductor. Longer $T_1$ 
implies slower energy decay and better preservation of the population in excited states.

\noindent\textbf{Dephasing Time ($T_2$).} The dephasing time $T_2$ quantifies 
how long a qubit can maintain phase coherence in a superposition state 
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$. Unlike $T_1$, which 
describes population loss, $T_2$ captures the decay of off-diagonal elements 
of the density matrix—the quantum phase relationships between $|0\rangle$ and 
$|1\rangle$. Dephasing occurs through two mechanisms: energy relaxation 
(which also destroys phase coherence) and \emph{pure dephasing}, which 
randomizes relative phases without energy exchange. The relationship between 
these times is
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\phi},
\end{equation}
where $T_\phi$ is the pure dephasing time arising from low-frequency noise in 
the qubit transition frequency (e.g., charge noise or flux noise). This 
equation shows that $T_2 \leq 2T_1$, with equality achieved only when pure 
dephasing is negligible ($T_\phi \to \infty$). In practice, $T_2$ is often 
significantly shorter than $2T_1$ due to environmental noise coupling to the 
qubit frequency.

\noindent\textbf{Operational Implications.} For quantum gates requiring time 
$t_{\mathrm{gate}}$, high-fidelity operation demands $t_{\mathrm{gate}} \ll 
T_1, T_2$. Single-qubit gates (typically 10--50~ns) are primarily limited by $T_2$, as phase errors accumulate faster than population errors for most superconducting qubits. Two-qubit gates (100--500~ns) can be limited by either $T_1$ or $T_2$ depending on the implementation. State-of-the-art superconducting qubits achieve $T_1 \sim 100$--300~$\mu$s and $T_2 \sim 
50$--150~$\mu$s~\cite{kjaergaard2020, place2021new, tuokkola_methods_2025} , enabling gate fidelities exceeding 99.9\% for single-qubit  operations and $>$99\% for two-qubit gates~\cite{Barends2014, kjaergaard2020}.

For superconducting qubits, dominant decoherence mechanisms limiting $T_1$ 
and $T_2$ include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dielectric loss}: Two-level system defects in substrate and junction interfaces, scaling as $T_{1,\text{diel}} \propto 1/\omega$ with qubit frequency~\cite{krantz2019}.
    \item \textbf{Quasiparticle poisoning}: Non-equilibrium quasiparticles from cosmic rays, background radioactivity, or residual thermal excitations, particularly problematic at elevated temperatures~\cite{Vepsalainen2020,McEwen2022,Martinis2021cosmic}.
    \item \textbf{Flux noise}: Low-frequency fluctuations in magnetic environment, affecting flux-tunable designs.
\end{itemize}

Recent work has identified ionizing radiation from cosmic-ray muons and gamma rays as a significant source of correlated errors in superconducting qubit arrays~\cite{McEwen2022}, with phonon-mediated quasiparticle bursts degrading coherence across entire chips~\cite{Martinis2021cosmic}. Mitigation strategies include underground operation, lead shielding, and phonon downconversion through back-side metallization~\cite{Vepsalainen2020, Iaia2022}, with experimental demonstrations showing over two orders of magnitude reduction in correlated error rates~\cite{Iaia2022}.

Quantum error correction (QEC) combats decoherence by encoding logical qubits redundantly in physical qubits, enabling fault-tolerant computation when physical error rates fall below thresholds ($\sim 10^{-3}$ for surface codes). However, QEC imposes multiplicative overhead ($\sim 10^3$--$10^6$ physical qubits per logical qubit), motivating hardware improvements and exploration of alternative operating regimes, such as the elevated-temperature approach investigated in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_hardware}.

\subsection{Current State and Open Challenges}

As of 2025, quantum processors have demonstrated quantum advantage for specific sampling problems (Google's 2019 supremacy claim, subsequent boson sampling experiments), yet practical utility for commercially relevant problems remains elusive. Key open challenges include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Improving gate fidelities beyond 99.9\% to enable shallow QEC codes
    \item Scaling to $10^3$--$10^6$ qubits with efficient classical control and cryogenics
    \item Developing application-specific algorithms that exhibit quantum advantage with limited resources
    \item Reducing infrastructure cost and complexity to enable broader deployment
\end{itemize}

The asymmetric SQUID transmon analysis in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_hardware} addresses the last challenge by investigating operation at 4~K (pulse-tube cooler accessible) rather than 20~mK (dilution refrigerator), potentially reducing system costs by 70--80\%~\cite{martinis2020}.

\textbf{By quantum resource:}

    \item \textbf{Type II}: Quantum-enhanced detection (homodyne, photon counting)
    \item \textbf{Type III}: Entanglement-enabled precision (GHZ states, spin squeezing)
\end{itemize}

Representative observables include magnetic and electric fields, frequency and time standards, acceleration and rotation, temperature and pressure, and force and displacement. Table~\ref{tab:qs-platforms} summarizes major platforms and their target observables.

\subsection{Sensitivity, Noise, and Bandwidth}

The figure of merit for a quantum sensor is its sensitivity---the minimum resolvable signal---versus bandwidth, constrained by sensor noise, sampling rate, and quantum decoherence. Sensitivity is quantified through the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), which provides channel-agnostic limits on parameter estimation precision. The QFI $\mathcal{F}$ relates to the minimum achievable variance via the quantum Cramér-Rao bound:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta \geq \frac{1}{\sqrt{M \mathcal{F}(\theta)}}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the number of measurements and $\theta$ is the parameter to be estimated.

Practical estimators (maximum likelihood, Bayesian) approach these bounds under regularity conditions. When compatible with the physical platform, squeezed or entangled probes can enhance sensitivity by factors up to $\sqrt{N}$ (Heisenberg limit) versus standard quantum limit (SQL) scaling. However, losses and dephasing quickly revert scaling to SQL, making robust operation in realistic noise environments critical.

Extracting signals often involves spectral lineshapes (e.g., optically detected magnetic resonance in NV centers, electromagnetically induced transparency in Rydberg atoms) and indirect estimators that map quantum observables to classical readout channels.

\subsection{Rydberg-Atom Electromagnetic Sensing}

Rydberg atoms---atoms excited to high principal quantum number $n$---possess giant electric dipole moments scaling as $n^2$ and exhibit electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT) and Autler-Townes splitting that encode radio-to-terahertz electromagnetic fields into optical readout signals~\cite{Saffman2010,Gallagher2005}. Key features include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Broad spectral reach}: Tunable from kHz to THz by varying $n$ (typically $n = 30$--100)
    \item \textbf{Room-temperature operation}: Atomic vapors in glass cells enable compact, portable sensors
    \item \textbf{Self-calibrated SI traceability}: Transition frequencies determined by fundamental constants
    \item \textbf{Sub-wavelength spatial resolution}: Enables field mapping at microscale
\end{itemize}

The large dipole moments enable single-photon sensitivity through avalanche amplification: a single Rydberg excitation can trigger a cascade of ionization events via dipole-dipole interactions, producing measurable ion signals. This avalanche mechanism forms the basis for the Rydberg-based single-photon detector analyzed in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_sensing}.

\subsection{Quantum Sensing Principles}

Quantum sensors exploit quantum mechanical phenomena to achieve measurement sensitivities beyond classical limits~\cite{Degen2017}. Three fundamental quantum resources enable enhanced sensing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quantum coherence}: Superposition states and interference enable phase-sensitive measurements with precision limited by quantum projection noise rather than classical shot noise.
    
    \item \textbf{Quantum squeezing}: Redistribution of quantum uncertainty between conjugate observables (e.g., reducing phase noise at the expense of amplitude noise) enables sub-shot-noise sensitivity in the measured quadrature.
    
    \item \textbf{Quantum entanglement}: Correlated multi-particle states (GHZ states, spin-squeezed states) can achieve Heisenberg-limited scaling $\Delta\theta \propto 1/N$ rather than Standard Quantum Limit (SQL) scaling $\Delta\theta \propto 1/\sqrt{N}$, where $N$ is the number of probes~\cite{Giovannetti2011}.
\end{itemize}

The achievable sensitivity depends on both the quantum resources employed and the robustness of these resources to decoherence. In practice, losses and dephasing often reduce entanglement-enhanced schemes back to SQL scaling, making the design of decoherence-resistant protocols critical for practical quantum sensing applications.

\subsection{Rydberg-Atom Electromagnetic Sensing}

Rydberg atoms---atoms excited to high principal quantum number $n$---offer unique advantages for electromagnetic field sensing~\cite{Saffman2010,Gallagher2005}. The orbital radius scales as $\langle r \rangle \propto n^2$, leading to giant electric 
dipole moments that couple strongly to external fields. Key features include:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Broad spectral coverage:} Tunability from kHz to THz by varying $n$ (typically $n = 30$--100), enabling sensing across the radio-frequency to terahertz spectrum~\cite{Sedlacek2012,Meyer2021,Downes2024}.

\item \textbf{SI-traceable calibration:} Transition frequencies determined solely by fundamental constants (Rydberg constant, fine structure constant), eliminating the need for external calibration~\cite{Holloway2014, Holloway2022, Anderson2024}. 
\textit{SI-traceable} refers to measurements that can be related to the International System of Units (SI) through an unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the measurement uncertainty. For Rydberg sensors, field strength is directly linked to measured spectral splitting and Planck's constant $\hbar$, providing intrinsic calibration without reference antennas.

\item \textbf{Room-temperature operation:} Atomic vapor cells enable compact, portable sensors without cryogenic infrastructure~\cite{Sedlacek2012, Jing2020}.

\item \textbf{Sub-wavelength spatial resolution:} Excitation volumes can be confined to micrometer scales, enabling field mapping with spatial resolution far below the RF wavelength~\cite{Fan2014, Simons2016}.

\item \textbf{Quantum-limited sensitivity:} Shot-noise-limited detection approaching fundamental quantum limits for RF field sensing~\cite{Cox2018, Jiao2023}.

\end{itemize}

Rydberg-based sensing protocols employ \textit{Electromagnetically Induced Transparency} (EIT) or Autler-Townes (AT) splitting, where external RF fields modify optical transmission through the Rydberg medium~\cite{Fleischhauer2005, Yuan2023}. 
\textit{Electromagnetically induced transparency} is a quantum interference effect in which a strong coupling laser renders an otherwise opaque medium transparent to a weak probe laser by creating quantum coherence between atomic states. When an RF field resonantly couples nearby Rydberg states, it induces an AC Stark shift that splits the EIT resonance---the Autler-Townes effect. The field amplitude $E_{\mathrm{RF}}$ maps 
directly to observable spectral splittings $\Delta f$ via the AC Stark shift:
\begin{equation}
\Delta f = \frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{RF}}}{\pi} = \frac{\mu E_{\mathrm{RF}}}{\pi \hbar},
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the transition dipole moment and $\Omega_{\mathrm{RF}}$ is the Rabi frequency induced by the RF field. This enables precise field measurements with uncertainties below 1~$\mu$V/cm~\cite{Holloway2014, Kumar2017, Zhang2024}.

For single-photon detection, dipole-dipole interactions between Rydberg atoms enable avalanche amplification: a single Rydberg excitation triggers a cascade of ionization events, producing macroscopic ion signals detectable with conventional electronics~\cite{Urban2009, Gaetan2009}. This avalanche mechanism, analyzed in detail in Chapter~4, provides single-photon sensitivity while operating at elevated temperatures where conventional photomultipliers face prohibitive dark count rates.

The QFI framework provides the theoretical foundation for Rydberg sensor sensitivity analysis. Performance is ultimately limited by quantum projection noise in the Rydberg population measurement, decoherence from blackbody radiation (scaling as $\Gamma_{\mathrm{BBR}} \propto n^2$ at 300~K)~\cite{Beterov2009}, and collisional dephasing in vapor cells~\cite{Carr2013}. Operating at reduced temperatures (4~K) 
dramatically suppresses blackbody-induced transitions while maintaining practical accessibility via pulse-tube coolers.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_hardware}} We develop a comprehensive theoretical framework and system-level design for asymmetric SQUID transmon qubits based on NbN/AlN/NbN Josephson junctions, targeting operation at 4~K rather than at the conventional 20~mK. This work addresses a critical scalability challenge: the infrastructure costs and complexity of dilution refrigerators, which currently limit quantum processor deployment.

\paragraph{Technical Approach.} Through detailed electromagnetic simulations and Hamiltonian analysis, we characterize flux-tunable sweet spots arising from SQUID asymmetry, enabling dynamic qubit frequency control while suppressing flux noise sensitivity. The analysis encompasses:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Derivation of flux-dependent effective Josephson energy and identification of asymmetry-induced sweet spots at non-zero flux bias (Fig.~\ref{fig:asq-transmon})
    \item Comprehensive decoherence modeling including thermal population effects, dielectric loss scaling, quasiparticle dynamics, and radiative decay at 5~GHz operating frequency, projected then at 300~GHz
    \item Dispersive readout analysis for two-qubit cavity QED systems with frequency allocation strategies
    \item Coherence-limited gate fidelity projections and comparison with baseline millikelvin systems
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Key Results.} 
Numerical simulations predict:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintenance of quantum regime at 4~K with thermal population $n_{\text{th}} \approx 2.8\%$ at 300~GHz
    \item Projected coherence times $T_1 > 20~\mu$s, $T_2 > 14~\mu$s with optimized materials (sapphire substrates, 3D cavity shielding)
    \item Single-qubit gate fidelities $>99.8\%$ and two-qubit CNOT fidelities $\sim 98\%$, approaching fault-tolerance thresholds
\end{itemize}
The possibility of operating at a 4~K regime could also imply until a 70--80\% reduction in cryogenic system cost and 30$\times$ faster cooldown (4 hours vs.\ 5 days)


\paragraph{Impact.} This work aims to provide hardware designers with predictive models for next-generation tunable qubits operating in the pulse-tube cooler temperature regime, potentially enabling scalable quantum processors with dramatically reduced infrastructure overhead while accepting modest ($< 1\%$) fidelity degradation compared to state-of-the-art millikelvin systems~\cite{martinis2020,kuo2020}.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_algorithms}} We design, implement, and benchmark hybrid quantum-classical convolutional neural network (Q-CNN) architectures for satellite image classification, demonstrating that near-term quantum processors can accelerate machine learning training dynamics despite limited qubit resources and gate fidelities~\cite{biamonte2017, Schuld2019PRA}.

\paragraph{Key Results.} Extensive simulations reveal:
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Training efficiency}: Approximately 3$\times$ reduction in iterations to reach target performance, representing time-to-solution quantum advantage
    \item \textbf{Regularization effects}: Quantum circuits exhibit implicit regularization, reducing overfitting on small datasets
    \item \textbf{Simulation-level validation with planned hardware execution}: Validated on Qiskit Simulator with plans for experimental confirmation on IQM 54-qubit quantum processor to verify functional correctness and latency characteristics
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Impact.} This work aims to identify \textit{training efficiency}---specifically, faster convergence to target accuracy---rather than final asymptotic accuracy as a primary quantum advantage in near-term hybrid architectures. This is crucial for resource-constrained applications where time-to-solution and computational cost matter more than marginal accuracy improvements, such as rapid model adaptation and transfer learning scenarios.



\subsection{Contribution 3: Rydberg Atom Avalanche Detector Modeling} 
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_sensing}} We develop a comprehensive theoretical and numerical model for Rydberg atom-based avalanche detectors targeting single-photon sensitivity, analyzing performance across magnetic field strengths relevant to axion dark matter searches and fundamental physics experiments~\cite{Saffman2010} (see Fig.~\ref{fig:B_field_scan_in})

\paragraph{Technical Approach.} The modeling framework encompasses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Magnetic field-dependent Rydberg level structure and dipole-dipole interaction calculations for principal quantum numbers $n = 40$--60
    \item Master equation simulations of spatiotemporal avalanche dynamics, including coherent dipole-dipole coupling, ionization pathways, and thermal decoherence
    \item Sensitivity analysis via quantum Fisher information and signal-to-noise optimization
    \item Operating condition optimization balancing amplification factor, dark count suppression, and detection time stability
\end{itemize}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapters/qs_figures/axion_detector_B_scan.png}
    \caption{Magnetic field dependence of the Rydberg avalanche detector. \textbf{(a)} Time evolution of the total Rydberg population $S(t)$ for magnetic fields $B = 0, 1, 3, 5$ T.\textbf{(b)} Amplification factor $\mathcal{A} = S(T_a)/S(0)$ versus magnetic field. \textbf{(c)} Dark count rate versus magnetic field (solid line) and temperature (dashed lines). \textbf{(d)} Spatiotemporal dynamics of the Rydberg density $S_j(t)$ at $B = 5$ T.}
    \label{fig:B_field_scan_in}

\paragraph{Key Results.} Numerical simulations predict:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Single-photon amplification factor $\mathcal{A} > 3$ maintained across 0--5~T magnetic fields despite Zeeman splitting
    \item Dark count rates below 0.01~Hz at $T = 4$~K, with approximately 100$\times$ suppression achievable at sub-Kelvin temperatures through reduced blackbody-induced ionization
    \item Robust detection time stability ($T_a \approx 8.8~\mu$s) under magnetic field variations, enabling consistent timing discrimination
    \item Field ionization readout proving superior to fluorescence imaging for high-magnetic-field operation
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Impact.} This work establishes theoretical performance bounds for Rydberg-based single-photon detection in challenging electromagnetic environments, directly informing experimental implementations for quantum-enhanced weak-signal detection in fundamental physics searches (axion detection, rare event searches) and precision metrology applications.

\begin{itemize}

    \item Density matrix evolution for avalanche propagation dynamics
    \item Quantum Fisher Information \footnote{The Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), sets the limit  on parameter estimation precision of an unknown parameter $\theta$, encoded in a quantum state $\rho(\theta)$. The QFI $\mathcal{F}$ relates to the minimum achievable variance via the quantum Cramér-Rao bound:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\theta \geq \frac{1}{\sqrt{M \mathcal{F}(\theta)}}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the number of independent measurements.}calculations for sensitivity bounds

\subsection{Advantages of Simulation-First Approach}

This methodology enables:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive parameter space exploration}: Systematic variation of design parameters (junction asymmetry, operating frequency, magnetic field strength) beyond experimental feasibility, identifying optimal operating regimes before fabrication
    
    \item \textbf{Isolation of physical effects}: Separation of fundamental quantum phenomena from experimental artifacts (e.g., fabrication variations, electromagnetic interference, thermal gradients), enabling unambiguous identification of limiting factors
    
    \item \textbf{Rapid iteration on design hypotheses}: Testing architectural modifications (e.g., 3D cavity shielding, junction array configurations, circuit topology changes) in hours rather than months required for experimental fabrication cycles
    
    \item \textbf{Predictive guidance for experiments}: Providing quantitative performance projections, design specifications, and failure mode analysis to inform experimental implementations, reducing trial-and-error iteration
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hardware Validation Strategy}

Where hardware validation is pursued, particularly for the quantum machine learning work (Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum_algorithms}), experiments serve to \textit{validate simulation correctness} rather than constitute primary experimental contributions. Selected benchmarks on IQM's 54-qubit processor will confirm:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Functional correctness of quantum circuit implementations
    \item Qualitative agreement between simulated and measured training dynamics
    \item Timing and latency characteristics under realistic control hardware
\end{itemize}

This validation strategy ensures that simulation predictions are experimentally grounded while maintaining the thesis's focus on theoretical and numerical contributions.

Throughout this thesis, we adopt the following notational conventions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Quantum states are denoted using Dirac notation: $\ket{\psi}$ for ket vectors, $\bra{\psi}$ for bra vectors, and $\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}$ for density operators
    \item Operators are distinguished by hat notation: $\hat{H}$ for Hamiltonians, $\hat{\rho}$ for density matrices, $\hat{O}$ for generic observables
    \item Pauli matrices are denoted $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$, $\sigma_z$ in the computational basis
    \item Vectors in Euclidean space are bold: $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$

    \item Frequency and angular frequency: $f$ denotes frequency in Hz, $\omega = 2\pi f$ denotes angular frequency in rad/s
\end{itemize}
